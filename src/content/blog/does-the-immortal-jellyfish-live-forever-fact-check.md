Somewhere in the warm surface waters of the Mediterranean, an animal the size of a pinky nail is doing something no other known creature on Earth can do. It is growing younger. Not in the loose, metaphorical sense of a spa weekend or a good night's sleep, but literally: a sexually mature adult jellyfish, having already reproduced, is dismantling its own body, cell by cell, and rebuilding itself as a juvenile. It is, in the most concrete biological sense available to science, pressing rewind on its own life.

The animal is *Turritopsis dohrnii*, and the internet calls it the immortal jellyfish. The nickname has travelled far beyond marine biology journals. It appears in listicles about creatures that never die, in social media posts promising that scientists have found the secret to eternal youth, and in breathless headlines suggesting that this tiny drifting animal has somehow solved the problem that has defeated every human civilisation. The claim, in its most shareable form, is simple: the immortal jellyfish lives forever.

It is one of those claims that is so appealing that people rarely stop to ask what it actually means. And when you do stop and ask — when you look at the experiments, the genome, and what actually happens to these animals in the open ocean — the answer turns out to be more interesting, and more honest, than the headline. The jellyfish really can reverse its life cycle. That part is true, and it is extraordinary. But "lives forever" is not what the evidence shows. The animal can dodge old age. It cannot dodge everything else.

## The Claim

The version of the story that circulates online usually goes something like this: scientists discovered a jellyfish that never dies. When it gets old or injured, it simply turns back into a baby and starts its life again, and it can do this forever, which means it is immortal. Sometimes the claim adds that a single individual could, in principle, have been alive since the time of the dinosaurs. Sometimes it adds that researchers are studying the animal to unlock human immortality.

Every element of that story contains a grain of truth, which is exactly what makes it so durable. The animal exists. It really does reverse its life cycle. Scientists really are studying its genome for clues about ageing. But the story as usually told leaves out the qualifications that scientists themselves attach to every one of those facts, and the qualifications change the conclusion. The question is not whether the jellyfish is remarkable — it is. The question is whether "lives forever" is an accurate description of what happens to it. On the evidence, it is not.

To see why, it helps to start with what this animal actually is, because the first surprise is that the creature in the famous story spent more than a century hiding under the wrong name.

## A Life Cycle That Runs Backwards

Most jellyfish live lives that follow a familiar script. A fertilised egg develops into a tiny larva called a planula, which settles on the seafloor and grows into a polyp — a small, stalk-like colony animal attached to rock or shell. The polyp then buds off free-swimming medusae, the familiar bell-shaped jellyfish, which grow up, reproduce sexually, and die. For most species, the whole adult medusa stage lasts somewhere between hours and a few months. The script runs in one direction, and death is the final line.

*Turritopsis dohrnii* follows the same script up to a point, and then it breaks it. When an adult medusa of this species is starved, injured, suddenly chilled or warmed, or exposed to a sharp drop in salinity, it does not simply die. Instead, it sinks to the seafloor, its bell deteriorates, its tentacles shrink away, and it collapses into a small cyst-like blob. That blob attaches to a surface and grows into a new polyp colony. The polyp then buds off new medusae, genetically identical to the one that just "died". The life cycle has run backwards — adult to juvenile — and the process can, in principle, repeat indefinitely.

The mechanism behind this reversal is called transdifferentiation. In most animals, once a cell has specialised — becoming a muscle cell, a nerve cell, a skin cell — its fate is fixed. Transdifferentiation is the rare ability of a mature, specialised cell to transform directly into a different type of mature cell. In *T. dohrnii*, cells from the bell surface and from the animal's internal canal system reprogramme themselves and rebuild the body in its juvenile form. It is a trick that, as far as scientists have been able to establish, no other animal performs after reaching sexual maturity.

It is worth pausing on that last qualification, because it is the detail that makes this animal scientifically famous. Rejuvenation in the animal kingdom is not entirely unknown. Hydras, the tiny freshwater relatives of jellyfish, show no measurable ageing under laboratory conditions. Some flatworms called planarians can regenerate their entire bodies from a fragment. But *T. dohrnii* is the only known animal that can take a sexually mature adult — an animal that has already reproduced, the stage at which evolution normally considers the job done — and send it all the way back to the beginning. That is the discovery that earned it the "immortal" label, and it is a genuine biological marvel.

It is also, crucially, a laboratory observation. The reversal has been demonstrated again and again in tanks and petri dishes. It has never been observed happening in the open ocean. That gap matters, and it is the first reason the "lives forever" claim starts to wobble.

## The 1996 Experiment That Changed Everything

The story of how science learned about this trick begins in the Mediterranean in the early 1990s. Italian marine biologists had noticed something odd about a small hydrozoan jellyfish collected near the Cheradi Isles, off Taranto in southern Italy. Young medusae that were damaged or stressed did not die the way other jellyfish died. They settled, shrank, and turned back into polyps.

A team led by Stefano Piraino, working with Ferdinando Boero, Brigitte Aeschbach and Volker Schmid, set out to test how far the trick went. Their paper, published in the journal *Biological Bulletin* in June 1996, is the foundation of everything written about the immortal jellyfish since. The researchers took medusae at every stage of life — newly released juveniles, growing adults, and fully mature individuals with developed gonads — and subjected them to the kinds of stress that would kill most animals: starvation, sudden temperature changes, reduced salinity, and physical damage to the bell with forceps or scissors.

The result was startling. All stages of the medusa, from newly liberated to fully mature individuals, could transform back into polyps. The paper's abstract states the finding plainly: the animal could "transform back into colonial hydroids, either directly or through a resting period, thus escaping death and achieving potential immortality". It was, the authors wrote, "the first metazoan known to revert to a colonial, juvenile morph after having achieved sexual maturity in a solitary stage".

A later study by E. C. Carlà and colleagues, working with colonies collected in the same waters in 1999, mapped the reversal in finer detail. They identified four recognisable stages in the inverted life cycle: a healthy medusa swimming actively, an unhealthy medusa that could no longer swim, a shrunken "four-leaf clover" stage, and finally a cyst that produced the polyp. The most reliable trigger they found was heat: when medusae kept at a normal 22°C were exposed to 30°C water for 30 minutes, about 80 percent of them began the reversal. The process was not a rare accident. Under the right stress, it was the animal's standard response.

Selective excision experiments in the 1996 study added another important detail. The transformation only occurred when differentiated cells from two specific tissues were present: the outer surface of the bell and part of the gastrovascular system, the network of canals that distributes nutrients through the body. Remove those tissues and the trick failed. This showed that the reversal was not driven by a hidden reserve of unspecialised stem cells, but by mature cells actively changing their identity — true transdifferentiation, happening in a whole adult animal.

For a decade after the 1996 paper, the finding remained mostly inside marine biology. Then the internet found it, and the animal acquired a nickname that the scientists themselves never used: the immortal jellyfish.

## The Name Mix-Up

Before going further, there is a piece of housekeeping that matters more than it might seem. For years, the famous immortal jellyfish was reported under the wrong species name. The 1996 paper, and much of the early coverage, called the animal *Turritopsis nutricula*. That species is a separate animal native to the western Atlantic. The jellyfish famous for its immortality is *Turritopsis dohrnii*, a species first described in 1883 and native to the Mediterranean, which has since spread to temperate and tropical waters worldwide — probably carried in the ballast water of ships, its powers of regeneration helping it survive the journey.

The distinction was untangled by taxonomic work in the 2000s, notably a molecular evaluation of the genus published by Maria Miglietta and colleagues in 2007. The correction matters because it shows how much of the popular story was assembled from second-hand retellings rather than from the primary science. It also matters for anyone trying to verify the claim: the older literature, including the landmark 1996 paper itself, uses the name *nutricula* for the same animal that modern science calls *dohrnii*.

What has not changed is the biology. Whatever the name on the label, the animal is a translucent medusa roughly 3 to 4.5 millimetres across when fully grown — about the size of a fingernail — drifting in waters between about 14 and 25 degrees Celsius. It is small, fragile, and, in the wild, extremely edible.

## What the Genome Revealed in 2022

For 26 years after the 1996 discovery, scientists knew what the jellyfish could do but not how it did it at the molecular level. That changed in 2022, when a team at the University of Oviedo in Spain, led by María Pascual-Torner, published a landmark study in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. The team sequenced the entire genome of *T. dohrnii* — about 390 million base pairs — and compared it with the genome of a close relative, *Turritopsis rubra*, a similar-looking species that cannot rejuvenate after reaching maturity.

The comparison, published on 29 August 2022, examined almost 1,000 genes related to ageing and DNA repair. The differences were striking. *T. dohrnii* carried roughly twice as many genes associated with DNA repair and protection as its mortal cousin. It had extra copies of genes encoding DNA polymerase, the enzymes that copy genetic material — notably POLD1 and POLA2 — suggesting an enhanced capacity for error-free replication. It also carried amplified copies of repair genes including XRCC5, GEN1, RAD51C and MSH2, a molecular maintenance crew that patrols the genome for breaks and mutations.

The study also found differences in how the animal handles telomeres, the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes that shorten every time a normal cell divides and that function as one of the better-understood molecular clocks of ageing. During life cycle reversal, *T. dohrnii* effectively resets its telomere length. It carries more copies of the GAR1 gene, which supports the telomerase machinery that maintains those chromosomal caps, suggesting that telomerase activity in this species may be enhanced or more finely regulated than in its relatives.

Perhaps most intriguingly, the researchers tracked which genes switched on and off during the reversal itself. They found the silencing of targets of Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 — a system that normally keeps developmental genes switched off in adult tissue — together with the activation of genes linked to pluripotency, the stem-cell-like property that lets a cell become almost any other type of cell. In other words, during reversal the animal unlocks the genetic programmes of its youth, then re-locks them as the new polyp grows. As the study's authors put it, these findings point to those transcription factors as pluripotency inducers in *T. dohrnii*.

The genome study made headlines around the world, and the headlines, once again, ran ahead of the science. The researchers themselves were careful. Co-author Carlos López-Otín and his colleagues emphasised that the findings were a starting point for understanding rejuvenation, not a blueprint for human immortality. As the authors of the coverage in IFLScience noted, one of the study's authors, Daniel Maeso Miguel, wrote with his colleague María Pascual-Torner that "if Juan Ponce de León had known the secrets kept by *Turritopsis dohrnii* during his search for the fountain of youth, he would have been left parched" — because, the article explained, "it wouldn't be possible for a human body to replicate what the jellyfish does".

That is the second reason the "lives forever" claim needs its qualifications. Even the animal's own genome does not grant it a life without end. It grants it a way to reset its biological clock. A reset clock can still be smashed.

## Why "Forever" Is the Wrong Word

Here is the central problem with the claim. Everything described so far — the reversal, the transdifferentiation, the doubled DNA repair genes, the telomere reset — answers the question of whether *T. dohrnii* can die of old age. The evidence says it probably cannot, at least not in the way other animals do. But old age is only one way to die, and for a four-millimetre jellyfish drifting in the open ocean, it is not the way that matters.

In nature, most *T. dohrnii* medusae are likely to be eaten, or to succumb to disease, long before they ever get the chance to revert. The animal is small, soft, slow and translucent — a snack for fish, sea turtles, sea slugs and other predators. As the IFLScience summary puts it, "the typical specimen has a much shorter life expectancy than your average human because no amount of Benjamin Button-ing can rescue you from the digestive enzymes of a turtle's gut". A jellyfish that has been swallowed cannot transdifferentiate its way out of a stomach.

The same limitation applies to injury. The reversal requires the animal to survive long enough, and intact enough, to settle on the seafloor and rebuild. A medusa torn apart by a predator or destroyed by a sudden environmental catastrophe has no second act. The 1996 experiments showed that reversal works after controlled damage — a snip with forceps, a temperature shock — not after being consumed.

Then there is the question of what "the same jellyfish" even means. When a medusa reverts to a polyp, that polyp buds off new medusae. Those new medusae are genetically identical to the original, but they are new individuals, built from new cells. The colony that grows from the cyst is a clone, not a resurrection. This raises a question that is as much philosophy as biology: if every cell of your body is replaced by a genetically identical copy, are you the same individual, or a copy of yourself? Science has no settled answer, and the question matters because the popular claim — "this jellyfish lives forever" — implies a single, continuous individual persisting through time. What the evidence actually shows is a genetic lineage that can keep restarting itself.

There is also the simple fact that the reversal has never been observed in the wild. Scientists have watched it happen in laboratories, where the water is clean, the temperature is controlled, and nothing is trying to eat the subject. In the ocean, the process is rapid, the animals are tiny, and field observation at exactly the right moment is unlikely. That does not mean it never happens in nature — the laboratory results are robust and repeatable — but it does mean that the claim "this jellyfish lives forever in the ocean" rests on an extrapolation, not a direct observation.

Finally, there is the question of scale. Even in the laboratory, where conditions are ideal, the reversal is a response to stress, not a routine event. A healthy medusa in comfortable water does not spontaneously turn back into a polyp. It lives its normal medusa life, reproduces, and only when conditions deteriorate does it hit rewind. The animal is not ageless in the sense of never changing; it is a creature with an emergency exit from death that it uses when it has to.

None of this diminishes the discovery. An animal that can reverse its life cycle after sexual maturity is one of the most remarkable findings in modern biology, and the 2022 genome study has given ageing researchers a genuine new model for studying DNA repair, telomere maintenance and cellular reprogramming. The scientists studying it are clear-eyed about its significance. It is the popular retelling that overreaches, turning "biologically immortal in principle" into "lives forever", and "a clue about ageing" into "the secret of eternal youth".

## The Verdict

So does the immortal jellyfish live forever? The honest answer is: not in any sense that the phrase normally means. It can escape death from ageing by reversing its life cycle, and in theory it can do this indefinitely. But in practice, individual jellyfish are eaten, diseased or destroyed, and the reversal has never been seen happening in the wild. "Biologically immortal" is a precise technical term with a narrow meaning. "Lives forever" is a slogan, and slogans are where accuracy goes to die.

That is why the verdict is **Misleading**. The core fact is real and extraordinary. The conclusion most people draw from it is not.

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| The immortal jellyfish never dies | It can die from predation, disease and injury — and in the wild, most individuals probably do |
| It lives forever | It can reverse its life cycle indefinitely *in principle*; no individual has been observed living indefinitely |
| It turns back into a baby whenever it wants | Reversal is triggered by stress — starvation, injury, temperature or salinity shock — not chosen at will |
| Scientists found the secret to human immortality | The 2022 genome study found clues about DNA repair and telomeres; researchers say a human body could not replicate the process |
| One jellyfish could have lived since the dinosaurs | The species is ancient, but the reversal produces genetically identical *new* medusae — a continuing lineage, not one continuous individual |

For context, *T. dohrnii* is not even the only animal with a claim to biological immortality. Hydras show no measurable ageing in the laboratory, and some planarian flatworms can regenerate indefinitely from fragments. What makes *T. dohrnii* unique is the direction of its trick: it is the only known animal that can go from a sexually mature adult back to a juvenile. That distinction is real, and it is enough. It does not need the exaggeration.

The claim also illustrates something worth remembering about how viral science stories work. A genuine discovery — a 1996 paper in a marine biology journal — gets a catchy nickname, the nickname gets repeated without the qualifications, and the qualifications are exactly where the truth lives. The jellyfish did not become less amazing when the exaggeration was removed. It became more interesting, because the real story, with its transdifferentiating cells and its doubled DNA repair genes and its predators waiting in the water, is stranger and more instructive than the slogan ever was.

And that is, in the end, the useful lesson of the immortal jellyfish. It is not that death has been defeated. It is that death, for one tiny animal, has a loophole — and that loopholes have limits. The jellyfish can rewind its life. It cannot rewind being eaten. The ocean does not care how clever your genome is.

## A Short Timeline of the Immortal Jellyfish

- **1883** — The species *Turritopsis dohrnii* is first described; it is native to the Mediterranean.
- **1992** — Bavestrello and colleagues show that newly released, sexually immature medusae can revert to the polyp stage.
- **1996** — Piraino and colleagues publish the landmark study in *Biological Bulletin* showing that medusae at *all* stages, including fully mature adults, can transform back into polyps through transdifferentiation.
- **2003** — Carlà and colleagues map the four stages of the inverted life cycle and find that a 30°C heat shock triggers reversal in about 80 percent of medusae.
- **2007** — Miglietta and colleagues untangle the taxonomy, establishing that the famous "immortal" jellyfish is *T. dohrnii*, not the western Atlantic *T. nutricula*.
- **2022** — Pascual-Torner and colleagues at the University of Oviedo publish the full genome comparison in *PNAS*, identifying doubled DNA repair genes, expanded telomere maintenance and pluripotency gene activation during life cycle reversal.

For readers who want to go deeper, the 1996 paper and the 2022 genome study are both worth reading in full — the first for the experiment that started it all, the second for the molecular detail of how the trick works.
