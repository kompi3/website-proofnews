It is the most reliable diagnosis in the emergency room, and no doctor makes it. "It must be a full moon." The phrase circulates wherever people work the night shift — in hospital wards, police dispatch centers, psychiatric crisis units, and maternity suites. Ask the nurses, and many will swear the full moon brings chaos: more admissions, more births, more injuries, more strange behavior. Ask the scientists who have spent decades testing the claim, and you get a very different answer.

ProofNews put the lunar effect under the microscope: the sleep research, the crime statistics, the birth records, the emergency-room logs, and the physics of the moon itself. What we found is a story with one small grain of truth wrapped in a mountain of folklore. The moon does appear to nudge our sleep, a little, in some studies. But the grand claim — that the full moon drives crime waves, madness, and maternity-ward bedlam — has been tested, retested, and debunked for more than half a century, and it keeps coming back anyway.

## The Claim, As It Circulates

The lunar-effect myth comes in several flavors, and they share one feature: the moon is treated as an invisible hand on human affairs. The strongest version says the full moon increases crime, violence, psychiatric emergencies, and general disorder. A related version holds that more babies are born — or more complications occur — on full-moon nights. A softer version concedes the big claims but insists the moon disturbs sleep, leaving people restless, irritable, and wired.

These are not fringe beliefs. A survey cited in a commentary in the journal *Frontiers in Pediatrics* found that 140 of 325 people surveyed — 43 percent — thought "lunar phenomena could alter personal behavior," and the belief was most popular among health professionals, including social workers, clinical psychologists, and nurses' aides. In a separate survey of emergency-medicine personnel by researcher Danzl, 80 percent of emergency department nurses and 64 percent of physicians believed the lunar cycle affects their patients. Among the nurses, 92 percent reported higher stress on lunar shifts. About 45 percent of college students, according to *Scientific American* reporting, think the full moon can make humans do strange things. This is a belief held not by the gullible fringe, but by trained professionals who work with human behavior every day.

The belief is old enough to be baked into the language. The word "lunacy" comes from the Latin word for the moon and the Roman goddess Luna, a relic of the ancient idea that the moon could unhinge the mind. Werewolf legends turned the full moon into a trigger for literal transformation, and the image endures in pop culture — from horror films to Creedence Clearwater Revival's "Bad Moon Rising." Before anyone had a mechanism, they had a story.

## Where the Myth Came From

The moon has always invited explanations, because its effects on Earth are real and dramatic. The moon's gravity drives the ocean tides — two high tides a day, pulled around the planet in a rhythm older than life. And the human body is mostly water. The folk reasoning writes itself: if the moon can move the oceans, perhaps it can tug at the water inside us, sloshing our moods and impulses around like a tide.

There is just one problem with that reasoning, and it is fatal. The moon's gravitational pull on your body is extraordinarily weak, far too weak to move anything inside you. A mosquito landing on your arm exerts a stronger gravitational pull than the moon does. More importantly for the claim, the moon's gravitational pull is essentially the same whether the moon is full, new, or a thin crescent. The phases of the moon are a matter of lighting, not gravity. A full moon and a new moon both align the sun and moon with Earth — both produce spring tides — but the new moon, invisible in the sky, pulls just as hard. If gravity were the mechanism, the "effect" should peak twice a month, not once, and it should not care whether the moon is shining. The folklore cares enormously about the shining.

The other proposed mechanism is moonlight itself. Before electric light, the full moon was the brightest thing in the night sky by an enormous margin, and it genuinely changed what humans could do after dark. Moonlight could keep travelers moving, extend evening gatherings, and — crucially — keep people awake. This mechanism at least has physics behind it. Light is the most powerful cue for the human sleep system. The question is whether the moon's contribution is big enough to matter, and the research on that question is where the story gets genuinely interesting.

## Crime and the Full Moon: The Evidence Is Overwhelmingly Negative

If any part of the lunar myth has been tested to exhaustion, it is the crime claim. For decades, researchers have combed through police reports, arrest records, psychiatric admissions, emergency-room logs, crisis-center calls, and even poison-control records, looking for a spike around the full moon. The result is one of the most one-sided literatures in the behavioral sciences.

The landmark study is a 1985 meta-analysis by psychologists James Rotton and Ivan Kelly, published in the journal *Psychological Bulletin*. They combined the results of 37 published and unpublished studies covering homicides, other criminal offenses, psychiatric admissions, crisis calls, and more. Their conclusion was blunt: lunar phase accounted for essentially none of the variation in these outcomes — the moon's phases explained no more than about 1 percent of the variance in the behaviors usually labeled "lunacy." The small effects that did occasionally appear, they argued, could be traced to inappropriate analyses and what they called "a willingness to accept any departure from chance as evidence of a lunar effect." In other words, when researchers went looking for a lunar pattern with weak methods, they sometimes found one — and published it.

Later work has mostly agreed. A 2010 study by Roger Williams University examined the relationship between the lunar cycle and reported crime in San Antonio over five years and found that "the moon's phase was not related to the level of crime and disorder reported to the police." A 2005 study by the Mayo Clinic, looking at four years of records from its psychiatric emergency department, found no association between the full moon and increased patient contacts. Study after study, from different cities and different decades, lands in the same place: the full moon does not bring a crime wave.

There is one intriguing exception that proves the rule. A 2016 study distinguished between indoor and outdoor crime and found no link between lunar phases and total crime or indoor crime — but it did find that moon illumination had a positive effect on outdoor criminal activity. The authors interpreted this through the "illumination hypothesis": criminals, like everyone else, operate better with some light to see by, but not so much that they are easily spotted. This is not the moon bewitching the mind; it is the moon acting as a streetlight. Brighter nights make outdoor activity of all kinds easier, lawful and otherwise.

What actually predicts a busy night for police and emergency services is far less romantic. Friday and Saturday nights, holiday weekends, paydays, warm weather, and major events move the numbers. These are the cycles that fill intake cells and stretch hospital shifts — and every so often, a full moon lands on one of them by coincidence, handing the myth a fresh anecdote. The myth does not need the moon to cause anything; it only needs the occasional alignment.

## Births, Emergency Rooms, and Hospital Chaos

The hospital version of the myth is just as persistent and just as thoroughly debunked. "Some nurses ascribe the apparent chaos to the moon, but dozens of studies show that the belief is unfounded," said Jean-Luc Margot, a UCLA professor of planetary astronomy, in research published in the journal *Nursing Research*. His conclusion, after reviewing the evidence: "The moon is innocent."

Margot's work is a useful case study in how lunar myths survive contact with data. A 2004 study in a nursing journal had suggested that the full moon influenced the number of hospital admissions in a medical unit in Barcelona, Spain — exactly the kind of finding that keeps the folklore alive. Margot went back to the data, identified multiple flaws in how it was collected and analyzed, and re-analyzed it properly. The apparent lunar effect vanished. The number of admissions was unrelated to the lunar cycle.

The broader record is decisive. Margot's review noted that the absence of a lunar influence on human affairs has been demonstrated across automobile accidents, hospital admissions, surgery outcomes, cancer survival rates, menstruation, births, birth complications, depression, violent behavior, and even criminal activity. The birth claim is especially well studied: a UCLA study conducted some forty years earlier had already shown that the timing of births does not correlate with the lunar cycle in any way. The belief survived anyway, migrating from one generation of maternity-ward lore to the next, immune to the data.

Why do intelligent professionals hold a belief the evidence contradicts? Margot's paper argues that this is precisely what makes the lunar myth worth studying: it shows how otherwise reasonable people can develop strong beliefs that are not aligned with reality. A chaotic night shift under a full moon is vivid and memorable; a chaotic shift under a crescent moon is just a busy Tuesday. Nobody tallies the misses.

## The Physics Problem

It is worth pausing on the physics, because the lunar myth requires the moon to do something extraordinary to human bodies, and the numbers do not cooperate.

The gravitational argument collapses on two points. First, the force is vanishingly small — far too weak to influence fluids in the body in any meaningful way. Second, and more decisively, the force does not change with the moon's phase. The new moon, which is invisible, pulls on you just as hard as the full moon. If the moon's gravity were driving behavior, the effect should be identical at new and full moon, and the folklore would have no reason to single out the full moon at all. The myth's focus on the visible, shining moon is a giveaway: this is a story about light, not gravity.

The moonlight argument is more plausible in principle, but it runs into the modern world. A full moon is bright by nighttime standards, but it is a feeble light source compared to the electric lighting that surrounds most people today — streetlights, screens, bedside lamps, the glow of a phone. For moonlight to disturb the sleep of someone in a lit city, it would have to outcompete all of that. The researchers who take the sleep question seriously tend to frame it as an ancestral story: moonlight mattered enormously before artificial light existed, and whatever traces remain in our biology are echoes of that older world.

## Sleep: Where the Moon Actually Has a Case

Here is where the fact-check gets nuanced, because sleep is the one domain where peer-reviewed studies have found something — small, contested, and inconsistent, but real enough that the claim cannot be dismissed outright.

The most striking finding came from a 2013 study by Christian Cajochen and colleagues at the University of Basel, published in *Current Biology*. The researchers retrospectively analyzed sleep data from 33 adults who had participated in carefully controlled laboratory sleep studies. Crucially, neither the volunteers nor the investigators knew at the time that the data would later be sorted by lunar phase, which rules out expectation effects. Under stringently controlled conditions — no time cues, no windows, no way to see the moon — they found that around the full moon, deep-sleep brain activity during non-REM sleep dropped by 30 percent, volunteers took about 5 minutes longer to fall asleep, total sleep time shrank by 20 minutes, and the sleep hormone melatonin fell. Subjective sleep quality declined too. It was, the authors wrote, the first reliable evidence that a lunar rhythm could modulate human sleep structure under controlled laboratory conditions.

That is a genuine result from a respected lab. It is also a small, retrospective analysis of 33 people, and it has not settled the field. A large population-based study by José Haba-Rubio and colleagues put 2,125 adults (average age about 59) through full overnight sleep recordings at home and compared objective sleep across lunar phases. The result: no significant differences. Objective sleep duration was 398 minutes near the full moon versus 403 minutes near the new moon — a gap that could easily be chance — and brain-wave and cortisol measures showed nothing either. The authors' conclusion was direct: the study provides no evidence of a significant effect of lunar phases on human sleep.

Children's data tell a similar story of tiny effects. A 2016 study in *Frontiers in Pediatrics* by Jean-Philippe Chaput and colleagues used waist-worn accelerometers to track sleep and activity in 5,812 children aged 9 to 11 across 12 countries, producing more than 33,000 days of recordings. Sleep was about 5 minutes — roughly 1 percent — shorter around the full moon than around the new moon. Activity levels did not differ at all between moon phases. The researchers' verdict: whether a five-minute difference is meaningful is "questionable," and the study offered solid evidence that the moon's links to children's sleep and activity are not meaningful from a public-health perspective. Kids are not more hyper under a full moon; they sleep about one percent less.

The most ambitious field study came in 2021, when Horacio de la Iglesia, Leandro Casiraghi, and colleagues published in *Science Advances* the results of tracking sleep with wrist monitors among 98 people in three Toba/Qom indigenous communities in the Argentine province of Formosa — communities with no, limited, and full access to electricity — plus 464 students at the University of Washington in Seattle. They found a clear pattern: in the nights before a full moon, when moonlight is available in the hours after dusk, people went to bed later and slept less, across all groups, including the electrified ones. "We see a clear lunar modulation of sleep, with sleep decreasing and a later onset of sleep in the days preceding a full moon," de la Iglesia said. The authors proposed that moonlight likely stimulated evening activity and inhibited sleep in preindustrial communities, and that modern artificial light may emulate that ancestral effect. A later review noted that in the top quartile of "lunar cyclers" in that study, sleep started on average 32 minutes later and lasted 58 minutes less near the full moon than near the new moon.

So where does that leave us? The honest summary is that the sleep literature is genuinely mixed. A rigorous lab study found real but small effects. A large field study across electricity gradients found clear sleep-timing shifts. A very large population study found nothing. A huge children's study found a trivial five minutes. These results do not cohere into the folklore — nobody is finding werewolf-grade transformations — but they do not cohere into "the moon does nothing" either. Sleep is the one corner of this myth where the science says "maybe, a little, sometimes," and researchers are still working out which of these findings replicate and why.

## Why the Belief Refuses to Die

With the evidence this lopsided, the persistence of the lunar myth is itself the most interesting finding — and psychologists have a good account of it.

The core mechanism is confirmation bias, amplified by what psychologists call illusory correlation: the tendency to see a relationship between two vivid things even when none exists. A chaotic full-moon shift is dramatic, visible, and matches the story everyone already knows, so it gets remembered, retold, and counted. A chaotic shift under a dark moon is filed under "busy night" and forgotten. A calm full-moon night — which happens most full moons — does not register at all. Over a career, a nurse or a police dispatcher accumulates a highlight reel of full-moon chaos and a blank tape of everything else, and the highlight reel feels like data.

Occupational folklore does the rest. The belief is passed from veteran to rookie as shared professional wisdom, which makes it feel corroborated: if everyone on the ward says it, it must be true. Surveys show the belief is actually strongest among the professionals closest to the supposed effect — 80 percent of emergency nurses in one survey — which tells us how powerfully social transmission can override statistical reality. Nobody is lying; they are all sampling their memories the same biased way.

There are also real confounds that feed the myth. Weekends, holidays, paydays, and warm summer nights genuinely increase crime, injuries, and hospital traffic, and a full moon occasionally falls on a Friday night. When it does, the moon gets the credit for what the calendar did. And in the one domain where the moon may actually do something — sleep — the effect is small enough to be invisible in daily life but real enough in some studies to keep the broader folklore feeling plausible. A grain of truth is the most effective fertilizer for a myth.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The claim that the full moon affects human behavior, sleep, and crime bundles together things the evidence treats very differently, and the bundle as a whole implies far more than the science supports.

On crime, violence, psychiatric emergencies, births, and hospital admissions, the evidence is about as negative as science gets. A 1985 meta-analysis of 37 studies found the moon's phases explain essentially none of the variation in crime, homicides, suicides, or psychiatric crises. A five-year study of San Antonio crime data found the moon's phase unrelated to reported crime. A four-year Mayo Clinic study found no association with psychiatric emergency contacts. On births and hospital admissions, UCLA planetary astronomer Jean-Luc Margot's review concluded the evidence of any lunar influence is absent across births, birth complications, surgery outcomes, accidents, depression, and more — "The moon is innocent." Decades of research, large samples, consistent null results. The behavior and crime claims are not unproven; they are disproven to any reasonable standard.

On sleep, the picture is genuinely mixed and modest. The strongest positive findings — a 2013 Basel laboratory study showing 20 minutes less sleep and 30 percent less deep-sleep activity around the full moon, and a 2021 field study showing later, shorter sleep in the nights before a full moon across Argentine indigenous communities and Seattle students — suggest the moon can nudge sleep timing and duration by minutes to tens of minutes in some populations. But a 2,125-person population study found no significant effect at all, and a 5,812-child international study found only about five minutes less sleep — one percent — with no change in activity. Whatever the moon does to sleep, it is small, inconsistent across studies, and nowhere near the folklore of moon-driven madness.

So the myth survives on a classic structure: a tiny, contested grain of truth about sleep, embedded in a large, thoroughly debunked story about crime and chaos. The full moon does not make people violent, does not fill emergency rooms, does not time human births, and does not excuse a bad night at work. It might, in some people, shave a few minutes off a night's sleep — possibly an echo of the era when moonlight was the brightest light in human life. That is a fascinating scientific footnote. It is not a license to blame the moon.

The next time someone says "it must be a full moon," the honest reply is simpler: it is probably a Friday. And the moon, shining innocently overhead, had nothing to do with it.
