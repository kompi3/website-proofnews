You know the moment. The cabin door closes, the seatbelt sign dings, and a flight attendant's voice comes over the public address system with the same instruction you have heard on every flight for the past fifteen years: please switch your phone to airplane mode. Around you, two hundred people perform the same ritual. Some of them do it. Some of them do not.

Because a lot of people do not. A poll carried out by travel insurer Allianz Travel in 2017 found that 40 percent of passengers did not always put their phone on airplane mode, and almost 14 percent admitted they had secretly sent a text or made a call during a flight. A 2006 study from Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh found something even more startling: researchers detected one to four cell phone calls being made during every commercial flight they examined. None of which, as far as anyone can tell, made any airplane fall out of the sky.

And that is the puzzle. If two hundred forgotten phones per flight have been riding along in people's pockets for decades without incident, why does the rule still exist? Was it ever really about the plane at all?

## The Claim

The claim, in its strongest and most cinematic form, is that a phone's radio signal can interfere with an aircraft's navigation or communication systems — and, in the worst telling, bring the plane down. This is the version that shows up in action movies, in chain emails, and in the nervous imagination of passengers who picture a pilot's instruments flickering because someone three rows back got a push notification.

There is a softer version too, one that sounds more technical and therefore more plausible: that even if one phone is harmless, two hundred phones all hunting for a signal at once create a storm of electromagnetic interference that can disturb the sensitive radio instruments in the cockpit, at least during takeoff and landing, the most dangerous phases of flight.

Both versions rest on the same intuition. Planes are flown by radios — radios that talk to pilots, radios that measure altitude, radios that find the runway. Phones are radios too. What happens when they talk over each other? It sounds dangerous. It even sounds like science. The question is whether any of it ever happened.

## Where the Rule Came From

The story of airplane mode does not begin with phones at all. It begins with a different device, in a different century of aviation electronics — and it tells you a lot about how this rule got its shape.

The earliest version of the ban dates to May 1961, when aviation authorities prohibited passengers from operating FM radio receivers on board. The reason was straightforward and, by all accounts, legitimate: aircraft communication frequencies and FM radio broadcasts occupy very similar parts of the electromagnetic spectrum. In the United States, FM radio uses frequencies between 87.5 and 108 megahertz, while aircraft voice communications begin just above 120 megahertz. An FM receiver — particularly a cheap, badly shielded one — could realistically leak enough signal to interfere with the pilot's radio. The FAA's own guidance on portable electronics, Advisory Circular 91.21-1D, explains in its history section that the ban "was established in May 1961 to prohibit the operation of frequency modulation (FM) receivers since they were determined to interfere with the operation of aircraft navigation and communication systems."

But notice what that 1961 rule does not establish. It says nothing about mobile phones, because mobile phones did not exist. Cell phones operate at much higher frequencies — the lowest cellular bands bottom out around 698 megahertz, and modern 4G and 5G networks mostly live in the gigahertz range. As one detailed technical explainer put it, "it seems extremely unlikely" that cell phones could disturb air communications, because the frequency gap between phones and cockpit radios is enormous. The FM radio problem and the phone problem were never the same problem. They just inherited the same rulebook.

The rule that actually governs phones came from a different agency, for a different reason. In 1991, the Federal Communications Commission adopted a ban on the airborne use of cellular phones — and the FCC's own documents are explicit that it had nothing to do with the aircraft's systems. The ban, codified in FCC rule Part 22.925, was "adopted in order to guard against the threat of harmful interference from airborne use of cellular phones to terrestrial cellular networks." An airborne phone at 35,000 feet does not politely connect to the nearest tower; it can be heard by dozens of ground towers at once, all trying to serve it. The fear was that flying phones would tie up the ground network's capacity and degrade service for everyone on earth below. The target of the regulation was never the plane. It was the cell towers.

This matters because it reframes the entire debate. When the FAA later explained its own position, it was careful to distinguish the two. Cell phones, the agency noted, are "a different animal" from most portable electronics: they are intentional transmitters that send out signals strong enough to be received at distances far away from the user. The FAA's regulation, 14 CFR 91.21, broadly restricts portable electronic devices on commercial flights unless the operator determines they will not cause interference. The FCC's rule targeted ground networks. The two agencies built the modern requirement on top of each other — and somewhere along the way, passengers fused them into a single myth: the phone will crash the plane.

## What the Evidence Actually Shows

If the ban were truly about aircraft safety, you would expect a long trail of incidents to justify it — phones glitching cockpits, navigation errors, forced landings. That trail does not exist. What exists instead is the opposite: decades of looking, and nothing confirmed.

The FAA has commissioned formal studies of the question. A 2006 study and a follow-up review in 2012 both examined whether cell phone signals interfere with aircraft systems. Neither found evidence that they do. Separate testing by Boeing and Airbus reached the same conclusion, and regulators elsewhere came up empty too: in the FAA's own review, the New Zealand Civil Aviation Authority reported only two potential instances of cell phone interference on aircraft, and neither was conclusively attributed to a phone. After all that searching, the scorecard was essentially zero confirmed cases of a passenger phone disrupting navigation or flight systems.

Meanwhile, the real-world experiment has been running the whole time. With forty percent of passengers skipping airplane mode and millions of flights operating every year, commercial aviation has effectively been subjected to the largest uncontrolled electromagnetic interference test in history — and the planes keep landing safely. It is hard to overstate how strong this evidence is. If passenger phones could bring down airliners, or even routinely scramble cockpit displays, it would have happened by now, in public, and it would be undeniable. It has not happened.

None of this means interference is literally impossible. Aviation experts tend to hedge with a phrase that shows up again and again in technical coverage: "I'm not going to say the odds [of cell phones interfering with that system] are zero, but they're next to zero." One small, real-world nuisance does crop up in reports: cellular signals can produce an annoying rhythmic buzz in pilot headset frequencies when a phone transmits at high power near older cockpit communications equipment. Passengers may have heard the same phenomenon themselves — the familiar staccato buzz a phone used to inject into nearby computer speakers or car stereos a moment before a call came through. That buzz is electromagnetic interference, technically speaking. But there is a canyon of difference between audible static in a headset and a crashed airplane, and no one has ever credibly connected passenger phones to a serious avionics failure, let alone a catastrophe.

## The Interference Scare That Was Real

Here is where the story takes a sharp turn, because there has been a genuine, serious conflict between wireless signals and aircraft systems in recent years — and it had nothing to do with passenger phones.

In early 2021, the FCC auctioned off radio spectrum in the C-band — frequencies between 3.7 and 3.98 gigahertz — for 5G networks. On January 19, 2022, 5G services launched in 46 U.S. markets using that spectrum. And the aviation industry panicked. The reason: radio altimeters, the instruments that tell pilots exactly how high they are above the ground during the most critical moments of takeoff and landing, operate just above that band, at 4.2 to 4.4 gigahertz. An RTCA risk assessment published in 2020 had warned of "a major risk that 5G telecommunications systems in the 3.7-3.98 GHz band will cause harmful interference to radar altimeters on all types of civil aircraft." The FAA's own assessment agreed, stating that "no information has been presented that shows radio altimeters are not susceptible to interference caused by C-Band emissions."

This was not a rumor about phones in pockets. This was a clash between ground-based cell towers transmitting at high power and a specific piece of safety-critical avionics operating on adjacent spectrum — exactly the kind of frequency adjacency that made the 1961 FM radio ban sensible. The FAA responded with emergency airworthiness directives restricting low-visibility landings where 5G C-band interference was possible, and a flurry of negotiations between airlines, wireless carriers, and regulators led to temporary buffer zones and power reductions around runways. Later directives required aircraft without upgraded, 5G-tolerant altimeters to avoid low-visibility operations, with a final compliance deadline of February 1, 2024 for U.S. transport and commuter aircraft. By early 2026, the FAA had moved from emergency stopgaps to a permanent rule: a January 2026 proposal would require operators to upgrade radio altimeters again to cope with the next wave of 5G expansion into the upper C-band, which sits even closer to the altimeter's frequencies.

The irony is thick. The one time a real electromagnetic emergency threatened the interface between wireless networks and airplanes, the culprits were the cell towers — the same towers the 1991 phone ban was designed to protect — not the phones in the cabin. And the fix was never "turn off your phone." It was engineering: harden the altimeters, manage the spectrum, and the two systems coexist.

There is also a telling detail in how the rest of the world handled the phone question. Since 2022, the European Union has permitted phone calls and data usage during flights using "picocells" — small onboard cells that connect passenger phones locally and relay the signal, so phones stop shouting at full power toward distant ground towers. The technology had existed for roughly two decades before regulators deployed it at scale. If cabin phones were a genuine avionics hazard, European regulators would not have blessed a system designed to encourage their use. They were solving the ground-network problem — the original 1991 concern — while accepting that the avionics problem was never really there.

## What Regulators Actually Say in 2026

Strip away the folklore and look at the rulebook as it stands. The FAA's official position today is carried in Information for Operators bulletin 13010, which states that operators "should continue to require passengers to place their PEDs in 'Airplane Mode' (cellular transmitters off) from the time the aircraft takes off until it lands." The FAA's public safety page puts it in plain language: "The FCC and FAA ban cell phones for airborne use because its signals could interfere with critical aircraft instruments. Devices must be used in airplane mode or with the cellular connection disabled."

But read the fine print and the picture gets softer. Under 14 CFR 91.21, the FAA does not actually ban all portable electronics outright; it prohibits their use on commercial flights unless the operator determines they will not interfere with navigation or communication systems — and it delegates that judgment to the airline. Airlines, in turn, allow gate-to-gate use of devices in airplane mode, plus Wi-Fi and Bluetooth once the aircraft's approved systems permit it. The FAA has also long allowed airlines to permit phone calls after the plane has landed and is taxiing to the gate. The framework is precautionary, not absolute — a managed risk system, not a finding that phones are dangerous.

The FCC's side of the fence has evolved too, though more slowly. The agency proposed modifying the 1991 ban in 2004, then withdrew the proposal after public comments poured in. In 2013, the FCC proposed a new framework that would let airlines allow commercial mobile spectrum use in flight, subject to transportation regulators' rules — an acknowledgment that its own rules were, in its words, outdated. But the 1991 rule stayed on the books. Meanwhile, the European path diverged: allow the phones, manage the signals with picocells, and let passengers use them. Two continents looked at the same evidence and reached different policies — a sign that this was never a matter of settled physics demanding a single answer.

## So Why Does the Rule Persist?

This is the question that actually matters, because it has a real answer — just not the one most passengers carry in their heads. The airplane mode requirement survives for several overlapping reasons, and flight safety, in the crash-the-plane sense, is the least supported of them.

The first is the headset buzz. A phone at altitude, straining to find a tower, transmits at high power — and regulators are honest enough to admit that a pilot should never have to wonder what that clicking in their headset is while taking instructions from air traffic control. Both the UK Civil Aviation Authority and the FAA keep the rule partly on these precautionary grounds. It is not that a phone will crash the plane. It is that aviation operates on a philosophy of eliminating avoidable unknowns, especially during takeoff and landing, and a cabin full of transmitters shouting at full power is an avoidable unknown. The U.S. Department of Transportation's own analysis framed it this way decades ago, recommending that carriers prohibit the operation of portable electronics during the critical phases of flight below 10,000 feet, where pilots need clear, uninterrupted communication most.

The second reason is the one that never went away: the ground. A phone hunting for signal from a metal tube at 500 miles per hour hops from tower to tower, overloading cell sites with connection requests. The 1991 FCC concern about terrestrial network congestion was a real engineering issue, and parts of it remain valid even today. In-flight Wi-Fi, by contrast, operates on approved frequencies through equipment that was specifically tested not to interfere with avionics — which is why airlines can sell you Wi-Fi while forbidding cellular. The distinction is not "wireless is fine" versus "wireless is dangerous"; it is that one system was certified and the other was never designed to operate at altitude.

The third reason is social, and it may be the most powerful of all. A cabin where 200 people can make voice calls is a cabin full of other people's conversations, for hours, in a sealed tube. Regulators and airlines are in no hurry to create that environment. The flight-attendant unions and airline industry have repeatedly flagged fears that unrestricted phone use would worsen passenger behavior at a time when in-flight incidents are already a problem — reported incidents of unruly passengers rose by roughly 300 percent between 2021 and 2023, with the FAA logging more than 10,000 cases of disruptive passengers. Nobody has proven that phone calls would add to that count, but nobody in the industry wants to run the experiment. Airplane mode, whatever its origins, now doubles as a social contract: the cabin stays quiet because nobody's phone is live.

There is also a quiet fourth reason, the one that affects you directly and never affects the aircraft: your battery. A phone that cannot find a signal turns its transmitter up to full power and keeps searching, draining itself far faster than it would on the ground. Flip on airplane mode and you will land with your phone still usable. It is the one guaranteed benefit of the ritual, delivered every single flight, with no exceptions.

## The Verdict

The claim that mobile phone signals can crash airplanes — and therefore that airplane mode is a life-or-death safety measure — is **Misleading**.

It is misleading because it takes a grain of truth — that radio transmissions can, in principle, interfere with radio-based avionics — and stretches it into a catastrophe story that the evidence does not support. Decades of formal studies, including the FAA's own 2006 and 2012 reviews and testing by Boeing and Airbus, found no confirmed cases of passenger phones disrupting navigation or flight systems. The most that researchers have documented is the possibility of audible interference in headsets — annoying, worth preventing, and a very long way from a crashed plane.

What is true, and worth remembering, is that the rule was never really about the plane in the first place. The 1991 FCC ban that underpins the modern requirement was written to protect ground-based cell networks from phones shouting at towers from 35,000 feet. The 1961 ban it grew out of concerned FM radio receivers, a different device on a different frequency. And when a genuine spectrum conflict between wireless networks and aircraft did arrive — the 5G C-band versus radio altimeter fight of the early 2020s — the threat came from the towers, not the phones, and it was solved by engineering, not by asking passengers to power down.

So should you still use airplane mode? Yes — because the rule exists, because it is backed by real regulators, because a pilot's headset should stay clear during takeoff and landing, because ground networks work better when your phone is not spamming them from the stratosphere, because the cabin stays civil, and because your battery will thank you. Just do not believe the story that your forgotten phone is one notification away from bringing down the aircraft. That claim had thirty years to produce evidence, and it produced none.

Next time the announcement comes on, switch your phone to airplane mode without resentment — and without fear. The plane was never in danger from your handset. The rule is a mix of engineering caution, network housekeeping, and hard-won cabin peace. And that, unlike the crash story, is something the evidence actually supports.
