The day after the September 11 attacks, a truck mechanic from Wisconsin went down to his basement and let two of the world's deadliest snakes bite him. Not by accident. On purpose. Tim Friede had decided, in the wreckage of that week, to do something extraordinary with his life: he would make himself immune to poison.

Over the next eighteen years, Friede allowed himself to be bitten more than 200 times and injected himself with snake venom over 650 times, working through the venoms of cobras, black mambas, taipans, rattlesnakes and kraits. Twice, the experiment nearly killed him. But in May 2025, scientists announced that antibodies isolated from his blood — combined with a small-molecule drug — fully protected mice from the venom of 13 of the 19 deadliest snakes on Earth, and partially protected them against the other six. His blood, researchers said, could be the foundation of the first truly universal antivenom.

It is one of the strangest true stories in modern science. And it raises a question that has fascinated people for more than 2,000 years: can you build immunity to poison by taking tiny doses of it, again and again, until your body learns to shrug it off?

The short answer is that the idea is real — but only in a much narrower sense than the legend suggests. For a small class of poisons, the kind your immune system can recognise and remember, gradual exposure can genuinely build protection. For most poisons, it cannot. Some will simply accumulate in your body until they kill you, and others will damage your organs while you mistake growing tolerance for growing safety. The king who supposedly poison-proofed himself, the snake handler who survived 200 bites, and the laboratory that turned one man's blood into medicine all point to the same conclusion: mithridatism works, except when it doesn't — and you cannot know in advance which kind of poison you are dealing with until it is too late.

## The King Who Could Not Poison Himself

The practice has a name, and the name is a king. Mithridates VI Eupator ruled Pontus, a kingdom on the southern shore of the Black Sea, from 120 to 63 BCE. He was Rome's most persistent enemy in the east, fighting three wars against the Republic over 26 years, and he lived in constant fear of assassination by poison — a reasonable fear, since poisoning was a standard political tool of the ancient world, and his own guardians had reportedly tried to poison him when he was a boy.

According to the ancient accounts, Mithridates responded the way a paranoid genius would: he decided to make himself unpoisonable. He studied every known toxin, experimented with antidotes — reportedly testing them on condemned prisoners — and compounded a daily preventive mixture that became known as mithridate, or mithridatium. The Roman physician Celsus, writing about a century after the king's death, recorded a version of the formula with thirty-six ingredients, mostly plant extracts mixed in honey with castor for scent. Pliny the Elder described a version with fifty-four ingredients, and he was openly sceptical of the whole business, asking, "Which of the gods, in the name of Truth, fixed these absurd proportions? No human brain could have been sharp enough."

The legend reached its climax in 63 BCE. Defeated by the Roman general Pompey, Mithridates fled to Panticapaeum, on the far side of the Black Sea in what is now Crimea, where his own son had rebelled against him and his allies had deserted him. Cornered, he decided to die by his own hand — by poison. The historian Appian tells the story in grim detail: the king took out the poison he always carried beside his sword and mixed it, but his two young daughters, Mithridates and Nysa, begged to drink it first, and he let them. The drug took effect on them at once. Upon Mithridates himself, Appian wrote, it had no effect at all, "because he had accustomed himself to other drugs by continually trying them as a means of protection against poisoners." He walked around rapidly to hasten the poison's action. Nothing happened. In the end, he asked his Gallic bodyguard and friend, Bituitus, to kill him with a sword.

It is a magnificent story, and it may be largely true in outline — Appian is a serious historian, and the failed suicide is reported by multiple ancient sources. What is much harder to establish is what, exactly, Mithridates had been taking. Some modern scholars have suggested that his daily mixture may have included minuscule amounts of arsenic, venoms and other poisons, gradually training his body to tolerate them. Others argue the whole thing was a deliberate piece of propaganda: a king who wanted his enemies to believe that poisoning him was pointless would have every reason to spread the story himself. Either way, the recipe itself is lost. Pompey reportedly found one antidote recipe among the king's papers — two dried walnuts, two figs and twenty rue leaves, crushed together and taken with a pinch of salt after fasting for a day — which sounds less like a miracle cure than a folk remedy.

What is certain is that the idea outlived the king by two millennia. Versions of mithridate, later called theriacs, remained a staple of pharmacology for close to 2,000 years, prescribed for everything from snakebite to plague. The word "mithridatism" entered the language to describe the practice itself: protecting yourself against a poison by gradually self-administering non-lethal amounts of it. The question is whether the practice actually works — and modern toxicology gives a much more careful answer than the legend does.

## What "Immunity" Actually Means

The first problem with the claim is the word "immunity" itself, because the body has more than one way of adapting to a poison, and only one of them deserves the name.

True immunity is an immune-system response. It works only against substances that are biologically complex enough for the immune system to recognise, remember and attack — typically large protein molecules. When the immune system meets such a molecule in a small, survivable dose, it can produce antibodies that bind to it and neutralise it. The next time a larger dose arrives, those antibodies are already circulating, ready to intercept the toxin before it does its damage. This is the same principle behind vaccines and allergy shots: a controlled, tiny exposure that teaches the immune system to recognise a threat.

The second kind of adaptation is metabolic tolerance, and it is a much rougher bargain. Some poisons are broken down by enzymes in the liver, and repeated exposure can train the liver to produce more of those enzymes. The most familiar example is alcohol. Heavy drinkers develop a tolerance to the effects of drinking because their livers become more efficient at metabolising ethanol, so it takes more alcohol to feel drunk. But this is not immunity, and it is not free. The breakdown of alcohol produces acetaldehyde, a compound that is itself toxic and accumulates in the liver, and it is a major contributor to fatty liver disease and cirrhosis. The drinker feels less affected while the damage quietly continues. Tolerance, in this case, masks harm rather than preventing it.

The third possibility is that nothing adapts at all. Many poisons work by interfering directly with chemical processes inside cells, bypassing the immune system entirely, and the body has no mechanism for learning to resist them. For those substances, taking small doses does not build protection. It builds up the poison itself.

That distinction — between poisons the immune system can learn from, poisons the liver can be trained to process, and poisons that simply accumulate — is the whole story. Whether mithridatism works depends entirely on which kind of poison you are talking about, and the legend's mistake is treating all poisons as if they were the same.

## The Poisons You Cannot Train Against

Start with the ones that cannot be beaten at all. Heavy metals are the clearest example. Lead, mercury and cadmium accumulate in the body: the body has no efficient way of getting rid of them, so taking multiple small doses eventually adds up to one big dose. Regularly ingesting tiny amounts of lead or mercury will not confer tolerance. It will damage the brain, the kidneys and the nervous system, slowly and irreversibly. The dose still makes the poison, as the old toxicology saying goes — it just makes it on an instalment plan.

Then there are the poisons that the body can process, but only within strict limits that no amount of training can meaningfully extend. Cyanide is the classic case. The liver carries an enzyme called rhodanese, which converts cyanide into thiocyanate, a far less toxic compound that the body can excrete. That is why humans can ingest the small amounts of cyanide found naturally in apple seeds, or inhale the traces present in cigarette smoke and fire smoke, and survive. But the process also requires large amounts of sulfur-containing compounds, and the supply of those is limited. You cannot effectively condition the liver against cyanide the way you can against alcohol, because the bottleneck is not the enzyme but the raw material it needs. Relatively larger amounts of cyanide remain highly lethal, no matter how many small doses you have taken before.

Arsenic sits in an awkward middle ground. Some populations have developed genetic adaptations that allow them to tolerate higher levels of arsenic exposure, and in theory a limited tolerance can be built through controlled exposure. But the margin between adaptation and poisoning is extremely narrow, and the practice is extraordinarily dangerous. Hydrofluoric acid, meanwhile, is simply destructive at any dose: it burns through tissue and binds calcium in the blood, and no gradual exposure programme can teach the body to withstand it.

The pattern is consistent. Where the body has a specific, expandable defence — antibodies for a protein toxin, or enzymes for a metabolisable one — gradual exposure can help. Where it does not, gradual exposure is just slow poisoning. And the cruel part is that the person doing the experimenting often cannot tell which situation they are in. A growing tolerance to alcohol feels like success, right up until the liver fails. A person microdosing a heavy metal feels fine, right up until the accumulated dose crosses the line into organ damage. The feedback the body gives is unreliable, and the price of misreading it can be death.

That is why toxicologists are unanimous on one point: nobody should attempt mithridatism at home. The ancient king had physicians, prisoners to test on, and a lifetime to refine his mixtures. The modern enthusiast has a search engine and a scale. The gap between those two situations is the gap between a legend and a funeral.

## The Animals That Were Born Immune

Before returning to humans, it is worth noting that nature solved this problem long ago — for other species. Several animals have evolved genuine, inherited resistance to snake venom. Opossums, mongooses, hedgehogs and honey badgers carry proteins in their blood that neutralise venom components, allowing them to hunt and eat venomous snakes with minimal risk. A honey badger can take a cobra bite, sleep it off, and go back for more.

These animals did not earn their resistance by dosing themselves. They inherited it, shaped by millions of years of evolution in which the animals that survived snake encounters passed on their genes and the ones that did not, did not. Their immunity is also specific: it protects against the venoms they evolved alongside, not against every venom on Earth. Even in nature, there is no such thing as universal poison immunity — only a set of narrow, hard-won defences against particular threats. Some venomous animals are stranger still — the so-called immortal jellyfish can reverse its own life cycle entirely, a trick that has nothing to do with venom resistance but everything to do with how little we still understand about these creatures (<a href="/blog/does-the-immortal-jellyfish-live-forever-fact-check/">does the immortal jellyfish live forever</a>).

Humans, by contrast, were born with no immunity to snake venom at all, which is why a bite from a cobra or a black mamba can kill a healthy adult in hours. But venom has one crucial property that makes it the perfect candidate for mithridatism: it is made of proteins, and proteins are exactly what the immune system is built to recognise. Expose a human to a sub-lethal dose of venom, and the immune system can learn to produce antibodies against it. Expose them again, and again, in slowly increasing doses, and the antibody response grows stronger. In principle, a person could build up a circulating stockpile of antibodies capable of neutralising a dose that would kill anyone else.

That principle is not theoretical. It is the basis of how antivenom has been made for about 125 years: small doses of snake venom are injected into animals such as horses, which produce antibodies that can then be extracted from their blood and used to treat bite victims. The horse is, in effect, a professional mithridatist. Snakes are not the only venomous animals people encounter, either — jellyfish stings deliver venom too, and the folk remedy for them has been tested in its own right (<a href="/blog/does-peeing-on-a-jellyfish-sting-help-fact-check/">does peeing on a jellyfish sting help</a>). The question that haunted researchers for decades was whether a human could do the same thing to themselves — and whether the result would be a curiosity, or a medicine.

## The Man Who Let Snakes Bite Him 200 Times

If Mithridates is the patron saint of this idea, Tim Friede is its modern heir. Friede, a former truck mechanic from Wisconsin with no university degree, spent nearly two decades deliberately exposing himself to the venom of some of the world's deadliest snakes. His project began in the early 2000s, inspired by the ancient concept of mithridatism, and it escalated from tiny injected doses to full bites from cobras, black mambas, taipans, kraits and rattlesnakes — sixteen species in all. By the time he stopped in 2018, he had logged 202 bites and 654 self-injections of venom.

The work was neither quick nor safe. Friede has said that it generally takes about 24 venom injections of increasing concentration, spread over four months, to build full immunity to a single toxin — and he was working through more than a dozen of them. In 2011, two cobra bites in quick succession nearly killed him. He has been frank about the risks ever since, saying of his survival: "I got lucky." His kidneys and liver could have failed; his immune system could have turned against him. The immunologist who eventually studied his blood put it more bluntly: "No one should ever attempt this."

Friede was not the first to try. In the 1960s, a US Army veterinarian named Herschel Flowers repeatedly injected himself with snake venom in an attempt to create a vaccine that would protect soldiers fighting in the snake-infested jungles of Vietnam, and he reported withstanding a cobra bite with no ill effects after repeated doses of various toxins. Bill Haast, the founder of the Miami Serpentarium, injected himself with diluted snake venom for decades and survived more than 170 venomous snake bites, donating his blood for research along the way. In Burma, the Pakokku Snake Clan is renowned for so-called protection tattoos, part of a tradition of deliberate venom exposure. The idea has always attracted a certain kind of person: part scientist, part daredevil, part true believer.

What set Friede apart was what happened next. In 2017, Jacob Glanville, an immunologist who had previously worked on universal vaccines, met Friede and asked a question that would change both of their lives: could researchers study his blood? Friede, who had long hoped his experiments would lead to medical advances, agreed immediately, donating samples of his blood to Glanville's team at Centivax, a biotechnology company in South San Francisco. Glanville later offered him a job there. Friede stopped injecting himself with venom in 2018, partly to protect the company from liability.

What Glanville's team found in that blood was extraordinary: billions of distinct antibodies, some of which could neutralise venom from several snake species at once. The researchers isolated the antibodies that reacted with neurotoxins from 19 of the World Health Organization's category 1 and 2 deadliest snakes — the elapid family, which includes cobras, mambas, kraits, taipans and coral snakes — and set about building a treatment from them.

## From One Man's Blood to a Universal Antivenom

The results, published in the journal Cell in May 2025, were the most broadly effective antivenom ever described. The cocktail the team assembled had three components: two antibodies isolated from Friede's blood, called LNX-D09 and SNX-B03, plus a small-molecule drug called varespladib, a known toxin inhibitor. Tested in mice, the combination fully protected the animals against lethal doses of venom from 13 of the 19 snake species, and partially protected them against the remaining six.

The numbers tell the story of how the cocktail was built. The first antibody, LNX-D09, protected mice from a lethal dose of whole venom from six of the species in the panel. Adding varespladib extended protection to three more. Adding the second antibody, SNX-B03, extended it across the full panel. "By the time we reached 3 components, we had a dramatically unparalleled breadth of full protection for 13 of the 19 species and then partial protection for the remaining that we looked at," Glanville said.

The stakes are enormous. According to the World Health Organization, snakes kill more than 100,000 people every year, mostly in poor rural regions where the right antivenom is often unavailable, unaffordable, or matched to the wrong species. Traditional antivenom is made by immunising horses, and it is specific to particular snakes in particular regions; a treatment that worked against a bite in India might be useless against one in sub-Saharan Africa. A broadly effective, ideally universal antivenom would change that arithmetic entirely — and Friede's blood, researchers believe, could be the foundation of one.

It is important to be clear about what has and has not been achieved. The cocktail has so far been tested in mice, not in people. It is an experimental treatment, not an approved drug, and a universal antivenom is still years away from adoption. The next steps are veterinary trials in Australia, where snakebites are a major threat to dogs, and then, if those succeed, human trials. Scientists are also using synthetic biology and artificial intelligence to replicate Friede's antibodies in the laboratory, so that no one else ever needs to repeat what he did. The goal, in the end, is a safe, lab-made version of his immunity — one that does not require anyone to be bitten 202 times to obtain it.

## Why Venom Works and Most Poisons Don't

The Friede story is so compelling that it is easy to draw the wrong lesson from it. If one man can become immune to the venom of sixteen deadly snakes, the reasoning goes, then surely the principle works for poisons in general — and surely a careful person could do the same with arsenic, cyanide or anything else. That reasoning is exactly backwards. Friede's case proves the rule by being the exception that fits it perfectly.

Snake venom works for mithridatism because it satisfies every condition the biology requires. It is a complex mixture of proteins, large enough for the immune system to recognise and remember. The same protein-based logic applies to other animal venoms — including the sting of a honeybee, whose venom is also a complex protein mixture, and whose sting carries its own famous consequence (<a href="/blog/do-bees-die-after-they-sting-fact-check/">do bees die after they sting</a>). It is delivered in doses that can be controlled and escalated gradually. The antibodies it provokes are specific and long-lasting. And crucially, the venom itself does not accumulate in the body the way a heavy metal does — once the immune system has dealt with it, it is gone.

Almost none of that is true of the poisons people usually imagine when they hear the legend. Arsenic and cyanide are small molecules, not proteins, and the immune system has nothing to grab onto. Lead and mercury accumulate. Alcohol can be metabolised faster with training, but the training itself damages the liver. The ancient king's fantasy — a single daily potion that protects against every poison — was never biologically plausible, because poisons do not share a single mechanism. There is no general "poison" for the body to learn to resist, only individual chemicals, each with its own chemistry, its own route through the body, and its own way of doing damage.

There is a second, subtler trap. Even where gradual exposure does build a genuine defence, it can create a new danger at the same time. Repeated exposure to a foreign protein can sensitise the immune system rather than tolerise it, so that a later dose triggers a catastrophic allergic reaction — anaphylactic shock — instead of being neutralised. This is a known risk of venom immunisation, and it is one of the reasons the practice is so dangerous outside a laboratory. Friede survived two near-death experiences and has said plainly that luck played a part. Bill Haast suffered serious health consequences from his decades of self-injection, including kidney damage. The line between building immunity and building an allergy is thin, and the person walking it has no way of knowing which side they are on until they fall.

This is also why the modern, medical version of the idea looks nothing like the legend. Nobody in the Cell study is suggesting that people should inject themselves with venom. The researchers took Friede's antibodies out of his body, identified the useful ones, and are working to manufacture them synthetically. The lesson of his experiment is not "try this at home" — it is that a single human immune system, pushed to an extreme over eighteen years, can produce molecules that laboratories can now copy and improve. The heroism, if there is any, belongs to the biology, not to the method.

## The Verdict

So, can you build immunity to poison? The claim, as it is usually made, is misleading — and the word "misleading" matters, because the claim is not simply false. There is a real phenomenon at its core. A king who dosed himself daily may genuinely have survived a poisoning attempt. A truck mechanic who let snakes bite him 200 times genuinely developed antibodies that now protect laboratory mice from some of the deadliest venoms on Earth. The mechanism is real, it is understood, and it has now produced a candidate medicine.

But the claim as people understand it — that you can make yourself broadly immune to poison by taking small doses — is false in almost every way that counts. It works only for biologically complex toxins, principally protein venoms, that the immune system can recognise and remember. It does not work for heavy metals, which accumulate. It does not work for cyanide, which the liver can only process in small amounts. It does not work for corrosive substances, which destroy tissue on contact. And where it does work, it works narrowly: Friede's antibodies protect against specific neurotoxins from specific snakes, not against poison in general, and even his protection came at the cost of two near-death experiences and eighteen years of risk.

The deeper problem is that the legend gets the direction of the lesson backwards. Mithridates' story is usually told as proof that the human body can be trained to defeat any poison. The evidence says the opposite: the body can be trained to defeat a few very specific poisons, under controlled conditions, at great risk — and the attempt to extend that training to other poisons is how people die. The most useful thing Friede's blood has produced is not a proof that self-immunisation works, but a set of antibodies that mean nobody else will have to try it.

If there is a single takeaway, it is this: immunity is not a general property you can acquire, like fitness. It is a collection of specific defences against specific threats, built by an immune system that only recognises certain kinds of molecules. The king who could not poison himself, if the story is true, was protected against the particular poisons he had trained against — and nothing else. The snake man who survived 200 bites was protected against sixteen species of snake — and nothing else. The rest is legend, and legends, unlike venom, do not come with antibodies.

## Timeline: Two Thousand Years of Poison-Proofing

- **120–63 BCE** — Mithridates VI rules Pontus and, according to ancient accounts, doses himself daily with antidotes and small amounts of poison to protect against assassination.
- **63 BCE** — Defeated by Pompey, Mithridates tries to kill himself with poison; his daughters die from the same dose, but it has no effect on him, and he dies by the sword instead.
- **1st century CE** — The Roman physician Celsus records a 36-ingredient version of the king's antidote; Pliny the Elder describes a 54-ingredient version and doubts its proportions.
- **For ~2,000 years** — Versions of the antidote, known as theriacs, remain a staple of medicine across Europe and the Middle East.
- **1960s** — US Army veterinarian Herschel Flowers injects himself with snake venom, aiming to protect soldiers in Vietnam, and reports surviving a cobra bite.
- **Mid-20th century** — Bill Haast, founder of the Miami Serpentarium, injects diluted venom for decades and survives more than 170 bites.

- **2017** — Immunologist Jacob Glanville meets Friede and begins studying his blood at Centivax.
- **2 May 2025** — Glanville's team publishes in Cell: a cocktail of two of Friede's antibodies plus varespladib fully protects mice against 13 of the 19 deadliest snake venoms, with partial protection against six more.
- **2025–present** — Veterinary trials in Australia are planned as the next step, with human trials to follow; researchers work to synthesise Friede's antibodies so nobody has to repeat his experiment.

The story that began with a king who could not die by poison ends, fittingly, not with a potion but with a blood sample. Mithridates wanted a universal antidote he could swallow every morning; what science actually found was something narrower, stranger and more useful — a set of antibodies, built by one man's immune system over eighteen years of deliberate risk, that may one day save the lives of strangers who will never know his name. The legend promised immunity to everything. The evidence delivered protection against a few things, at a price no sensible person should pay. That is the difference between a myth and a medicine, and it is why the claim, for all its grains of truth, earns its verdict: misleading.
