There is a pot on the stove in almost every culture's version of this story. A grandmother in Brooklyn, a mother in Seoul, a neighbour in Naples — when someone comes down with a cold, somebody ladles out a bowl of hot chicken soup, and somebody else says, with total confidence, that it will make you better. The soup has been called "Jewish penicillin," a nickname that captures both the affection and the exaggeration wrapped up in the claim. Affection, because the ritual is real and the comfort is real. Exaggeration, because a bowl of soup has never killed a virus.

So the question that actually matters is narrower than the folklore suggests. Does chicken soup help a cold — and if it does, what exactly does "help" mean? It turns out this is one of the rare home remedies where scientists bothered to check. The evidence is small, imperfect, and more interesting than either the believers or the sceptics usually admit: soup will not cure your cold, but in a few measurable ways it can make the days of having one noticeably easier. That is the difference between a myth and a mostly true claim, and it is worth getting right.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, is simple: chicken soup helps when you have a cold. In its strongest form, it goes further — that chicken soup has special healing powers, that it fights the infection itself, or even that it can shorten a cold by days. Social media posts and wellness blogs routinely present it that way, and the soup's reputation has been burnished by centuries of tradition. The medieval physician Maimonides recommended chicken soup for respiratory complaints in the twelfth century, and the advice has been handed down, largely unchallenged, ever since.

What makes this claim worth fact-checking, rather than simply enjoying, is that it sits exactly on the border between folklore and medicine. Unlike claims about detox teas or miracle cures, chicken soup has been studied in peer-reviewed journals. Three pieces of research in particular define what we actually know: a small experiment from 1978 that measured how fast mucus moves, a laboratory study from 2000 that became famous far beyond its actual findings, and a systematic review published in 2025 that gathered the scattered evidence together for the first time. Each one says something different, and each one has been misquoted.

Before looking at them, it helps to understand what a cold actually is, because the soup's real effects only make sense in that light.

## What a Cold Actually Is

A cold is a viral infection of the upper airways, most often caused by rhinoviruses. There are more than 200 viruses that can cause cold symptoms, which is one reason no cure exists and no vaccine is practical. Once the virus takes hold, your body does most of the work of getting rid of it, and that work takes time. The Mayo Clinic's guidance is blunt about the timeline: if you catch a cold, expect to be sick for one to two weeks.

Here is the part that matters for the soup question. Most of what makes a cold miserable — the blocked nose, the sore throat, the cough, the feeling of being flattened — is not caused directly by the virus. It is caused by your immune system's response to the virus. White blood cells, especially a type called neutrophils, rush to the infected airways and trigger inflammation. The inflammation swells the nasal passages, produces mucus, and irritates the throat. The virus is the intruder, but the inflammation is what you actually feel.

That distinction is the key to the whole story. Nothing you eat can kill a rhinovirus. But something you eat or drink might, in principle, calm the inflammation, help clear the mucus, keep you hydrated, or simply soothe irritated tissue while your immune system finishes the job. Chicken soup, it turns out, does a little of several of those things — and the research shows it does at least two of them measurably.

## The 1978 Study: Soup Versus Hot Water

The first serious test of chicken soup was published in 1978 in the journal *Chest*, and it is still the most direct experiment ever done on the question. Researchers led by K. Saketkhoo at the Mount Sinai Medical Center in Miami recruited fifteen healthy volunteers and measured how fast the mucus in their noses was moving — a measurement called nasal mucus velocity. Faster mucus movement means the nose is clearing germs and debris more efficiently. Then each volunteer drank hot chicken soup, hot water, or cold water, and the measurement was repeated.

The results were striking, and they have been quoted ever since. After hot chicken soup, nasal mucus velocity rose from about 6.9 to 9.2 millimetres per minute — roughly a one-third increase. Hot water did nearly as much, raising mucus velocity from about 6.2 to 8.4 millimetres per minute. Cold water did the opposite: mucus velocity fell from about 7.3 to 4.5 millimetres per minute. The researchers attributed soup's small edge over plain hot water to aromatic compounds in the soup stimulating the back of the nasal cavity, on top of the effect of the heat itself.

There are important limits to what this study proves. Fifteen people is a tiny sample. The volunteers were healthy, not sick with colds, so the study measured a mechanism, not a treatment outcome. And the effect was transient: mucus velocity was back to baseline within about thirty minutes. But that last point cuts both ways. A bowl of soup is not a single dose; when you are sick, you sip it continuously, and the cumulative clearing effect across an afternoon of sipping is plausibly real. The study's honest conclusion is modest and still useful: hot liquids help your nose clear itself, hot chicken soup helps slightly more than hot water, and cold drinks do the reverse.

## The 2000 Nebraska Study: The One the Headlines Love

The most famous piece of chicken soup research came twenty-two years later, and it is also the most misunderstood. In 2000, Dr. Stephen Rennard, a pulmonologist at the University of Nebraska Medical Center, took his wife's grandmother's chicken soup recipe into his laboratory and tested it on immune cells in a dish. The study, published in *Chest*, measured whether the soup slowed the movement of neutrophils — the white blood cells whose rush to the airways drives much of cold inflammation — using a device called a Boyden chemotaxis chamber.

The result was genuine: the soup significantly inhibited neutrophil chemotaxis, and the effect grew stronger as the soup concentration increased. Crucially, the active component survived filtering, which suggested the effect came from dissolved compounds in the broth rather than from chunks of chicken or vegetables. Rennard tested several soups, including commercial varieties, and found the effect varied — his wife's homemade recipe performed best, but it was not the only one that worked.

Then came the misquoting. Headlines turned a laboratory-dish finding into a medical fact: "chicken soup fights colds," "scientists prove grandma was right." The study's own authors were more careful than their headlines. Their caution, stated in the paper itself, was that this was a dish, not a person. Nobody has shown that the active ingredient survives digestion, enters the bloodstream, and reaches the airways in a concentration that matters. Researchers have proposed candidates for the active compound — including carnosine, an amino-acid compound found in chicken — but the identity of the active ingredient has never been firmly established, and no clinical trial has ever tested whether the laboratory effect translates into fewer symptoms in a real patient.

That does not make the study worthless. It makes it a mechanism, not a verdict. It shows that chicken soup contains something capable of damping down the exact inflammatory process that makes a cold feel terrible. Combined with the 1978 mucus study, it gives the folklore a plausible biological basis that most home remedies never get. But a plausible mechanism is not the same as proven relief, and the gap between the petri dish and the patient is where the claim starts to wobble.

## The 2025 Review: Gathering the Scraps

For decades, the evidence for chicken soup consisted of those two small studies and a great deal of tradition. That changed in 2025, when researchers published the first systematic review of soup as medicine for acute respiratory infections in the journal *Nutrients*. The review, by Lucas and colleagues, gathered four studies covering 342 people and asked a simple question: taken together, does soup actually help?

The answer was modest, and the authors were honest about its limits. Soups — usually chicken soup — gave a mild benefit for symptom relief and nudged some inflammation markers down. The certainty of that finding was low to moderate, by the authors' own assessment. Only one of the four studies even measured how long the cold lasted, and three of the four studies were done in Asia, which limits how far the results generalise. That single trial reported colds shortened by one to two and a half days, but its individual numbers mostly missed statistical significance, and the authors chose a narrative synthesis instead of pooling the numbers — a sign that the data were too thin and too varied to combine with confidence.

This is the most up-to-date summary of the evidence, and it is the best reason to rate the claim "mostly true" rather than "true." There is real evidence of symptom relief, but it is thin, the studies are small, and the strongest version of the claim — that soup shortens a cold — rests on a single trial whose results did not reach statistical significance. A responsible reading is that the soup probably helps you feel better, and probably does not make you better faster.

## What the Mayo Clinic Actually Says

If you want the mainstream medical position, the Mayo Clinic's guidance on cold remedies is the clearest statement available, and it is notably restrained. "Nothing can cure a cold," the clinic says. "But some remedies might help ease your symptoms and keep you from feeling so miserable."

On soup specifically, the Mayo Clinic lists warm liquids among the remedies that work, in the limited sense that they help with symptoms: "Taking in warm liquids, such as chicken soup, tea or warm apple juice, might be soothing and might ease congestion by increasing mucus flow." The clinic's advice on fluids in general is the same: water, juice, clear broth, or warm lemon water with honey helps loosen congestion and prevents dehydration. Honey, the clinic notes, may help coughs in adults and in children older than one year — and it warns never to give honey to children younger than one, because of the risk of infant botulism.

Notice what is missing from that endorsement. There is no claim that soup fights the virus, no claim that it shortens the illness, and no claim that chicken soup is better than any other warm liquid in any important way. The endorsement is for warmth, fluid, and steam — and soup happens to deliver all three at once, along with salt, calories, and comfort. That is the whole of the mainstream endorsement: a maybe, on the symptoms, not on the cold.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: Mostly True.**

Chicken soup does help a cold — in the specific, limited sense that matters to someone who is actually sick. Hot soup increases nasal mucus flow, helping the nose clear itself; laboratory evidence shows it can damp down the neutrophil-driven inflammation behind congestion; it hydrates you, replaces some lost salts, soothes a sore throat with warmth and steam, and delivers easy calories at a moment when your appetite has usually vanished. The 2025 systematic review found a mild benefit for symptom relief across the available trials. Those are real, measurable effects, and they are more than most home remedies can show.

What chicken soup does not do is equally clear, and it is where the folklore overreaches. It does not kill the virus, it does not cure a cold, and the evidence that it shortens an illness is weak — a single small trial, mostly not statistically significant. The famous Nebraska study was done in a dish, not in people. The mucus study involved fifteen healthy volunteers and an effect that faded within half an hour. If someone tells you soup will "knock out" a cold in a day, that version of the claim is false.

The honest summary is the one the Mayo Clinic gives: soup is a supportive measure, not a treatment. It makes the one to two weeks of a cold more bearable. For a bowl of broth that costs almost nothing and has almost no downside, that is a genuinely good result — just not the miraculous one the stories promise.

## How to Get the Most Out of It

If you are going to use chicken soup as a cold remedy, the evidence points to a few practical choices. First, eat it hot and sip it slowly. The mucus-clearing effect in the 1978 study came from heat and steam, and it faded within about thirty minutes — so a bowl nursed over time, or several small bowls across the day, makes more sense than one rushed serving. Second, favour a light, broth-based soup over a heavy, creamy one; the point is fluid, warmth and gentle nutrition, not a rich meal on an already unsettled stomach.

Third, watch the salt. This is the one exception nobody mentions when praising soup: many canned and restaurant soups are very salty, which is worth knowing if you are watching your sodium intake or already dehydrated. A homemade soup lets you control the salt, and a low-sodium canned soup is a reasonable compromise when you are too ill to cook. Fourth, do not rely on soup alone. Rest and fluids are the foundation of cold care, and the Mayo Clinic's advice puts them first for a reason: the longer you put off rest and hydration, the longer the cold will bother you.

Finally, know when soup is not enough. A cold that lasts more than ten days, a fever that climbs high or returns after fading, severe sinus pain, shortness of breath, or a cough that produces discoloured mucus and will not shift — these are signs to see a doctor rather than to make another pot of soup. Soup is comfort care, not a substitute for medical attention when something more serious is going on.

## What Actually Helps a Cold — and What Does Not

Chicken soup sits in the small group of cold remedies with real evidence behind them, but it is worth putting it in context, because most of the things people reach for when they catch a cold have far less support. Rest and fluids are the foundation, and they are the only measures every authority agrees on. A teaspoon of honey in warm tea may help a cough in adults — we examined that claim separately in <a href="/blog/does-honey-help-a-cough-fact-check/">Does Honey Help a Cough? Yes — Modestly, and Only for the Right Kind of Cough</a>. Salt-water gargles can temporarily soothe a sore throat. A cool-mist humidifier or a steamy shower can ease congestion in the same way soup's steam does.

By contrast, the supplement aisle offers mostly disappointment. The Mayo Clinic's review of the evidence is blunt: most studies do not support using vitamin C, echinacea, or zinc as cold remedies. Vitamin D is a more complicated story — we fact-checked whether supplements prevent respiratory infections in <a href="/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/">Do Vitamin D Supplements Prevent Colds and Flu? Fact-Checking the Viral Immunity Claim</a>. And if you are wondering whether a vaccine could be behind your symptoms instead, the answer is no — as we explained in <a href="/blog/flu-vaccine-gives-you-flu-fact-check/">The Flu Shot Can't Give You the Flu — So Why Did You Feel Sick for Two Days?</a>, the flu shot contains no live flu virus and cannot give you the flu.

That context is the real lesson of the chicken soup story. The remedies that work for a cold are almost boring: warmth, water, rest, and time. Soup is valuable precisely because it bundles several of them into something that also tastes like somebody cares about you. The science does not diminish that. It just draws a line, honestly, between feeling better and getting better — and chicken soup, the evidence says, is firmly on the feeling-better side of it.

## The Bottom Line

Grandma was mostly right, and the scientists who checked her work would probably say so too — with footnotes. Chicken soup helps a cold the way a good blanket helps a fever: not by defeating the illness, but by making the body that is fighting it more comfortable while it does. The mucus moves faster, the throat feels soothed, the inflammation is damped down a little, and you stay hydrated and fed through the worst of it. None of that is a cure, and none of it needs to be. A cold lasts one to two weeks whatever you do; the question is only how miserable those weeks feel. On that question, the evidence — thin, old, and imperfect as it is — comes down on the side of the soup.

So make the soup. Make it hot, make it light, sip it slowly, and rest while you do. Just do not expect it to do the immune system's job. That work belongs to your body, and it will take its time either way. The soup's job is simpler and older than medicine itself: to keep you going while you wait.
