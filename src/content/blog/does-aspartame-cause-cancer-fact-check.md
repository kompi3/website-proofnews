It is the sweetener in the diet soda on your desk, the sugar-free gum in your pocket, and the little pink or blue packet beside the coffee machine — and for more than fifty years it has also been one of the most argued-about ingredients on the planet. Type "does aspartame cause cancer" into a search engine today and you will find two completely different internets. On one side, viral videos and wellness influencers insist the sweetener is a proven carcinogen that regulators covered up. On the other, food-safety agencies say it is safe at the amounts people actually consume. Both sides point to the same event as proof: in July 2023, the World Health Organization's cancer agency classified aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic to humans."

That classification was real. What most of the posts sharing it never mention is what happened on the very same day: a second WHO body reviewed the identical evidence and left the safe daily intake unchanged, the US Food and Drug Administration publicly disagreed with the classification, and the WHO's own nutrition director said safety "is not a major concern at the doses which are commonly used." The claim that aspartame causes cancer takes one half of that story — the scary-sounding label — and presents it as the whole story. This is what the evidence actually shows, from the laboratory rats of the 1970s to the largest human studies ever run on the sweetener.

## The Claim

The claim, in its bluntest viral form, is simple: aspartame causes cancer. Sometimes it arrives as a warning — "stop drinking diet soda or you will get cancer." Sometimes it arrives as a revelation — "the WHO finally admitted aspartame causes cancer." Sometimes it arrives dressed up as history, with a tale of buried rat studies, a rigged approval process, and a sweetener that should never have reached the supermarket shelf.

The claim surged again in 2026. As one review of the diet-soda debate noted in March 2026, searches for diet soda jumped on the back of a new wave of viral posts recycling two genuine 2023 events: the WHO's advice against relying on non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, and its cancer agency's decision to label aspartame as possibly carcinogenic. The science, the same review observed, "did not move in 2026; the videos did." That is the pattern of this entire controversy: real events, stripped of their context, replayed years later as if they were new proof.

To judge the claim fairly, three questions have to be separated. First, what did the WHO actually say in 2023 — and what did it not say? Second, what have the biggest human studies found when they followed aspartame consumers for years? And third, how much aspartame would a person actually have to consume before any of the official safety limits were crossed? The answers to those three questions point in the same direction, and it is not the direction the viral posts suggest.

## A Sweetener Born by Accident

Aspartame's story starts not in a cancer laboratory but in a drug laboratory. It was discovered in 1965 by James M. Schlatter, a chemist at G.D. Searle & Company, while he was working on an anti-ulcer drug. According to a 2021 narrative review of aspartame safety published in the peer-reviewed journal Nutrients, Schlatter discovered the sweetness completely by accident, after licking the compound off his finger. What he had made was a dipeptide — two amino acids, phenylalanine and aspartic acid, joined together — that tasted about 200 times sweeter than table sugar, which meant a tiny amount could replace a spoonful of sugar with almost no calories.

That accident set off one of the longest regulatory sagas in food history. The FDA first issued a regulation for aspartame in 1974, allowing it as a tabletop sweetener and in chewing gum, cold breakfast cereals, and dry food bases. But objections followed almost immediately, driven by questions about the safety studies Searle had submitted — including studies in rats that critics said showed brain tumours. In 1980, the FDA convened a Public Board of Inquiry, a panel of three independent scientists, to examine the claimed link between aspartame and brain cancer. The board concluded that aspartame does not cause brain damage, but it recommended against approving the sweetener at that time, citing unanswered questions about cancer in laboratory rats.

The deadlock broke in 1981, when the FDA's new commissioner approved aspartame for use in dry goods, and it entered the market as NutraSweet. In 1983 the approval was extended to carbonated beverages — the use that would make aspartame a household name — and in 1996 the FDA removed all remaining restrictions, allowing it as a general-purpose sweetener. By then it was in diet drinks, chewing gum, gelatin, ice cream, yogurt, breakfast cereal, toothpaste, and even medicines such as cough drops and chewable vitamins. The WHO's 2023 release lists exactly that range of products, a reminder of how thoroughly the sweetener had woven itself into the modern diet before the cancer question ever reached an international verdict.

The cancer question never fully went away, though. In the 2000s, researchers at Italy's Ramazzini Institute published a series of long-term rat studies reporting higher rates of lymphomas, leukaemias, and other cancers in animals fed aspartame. The American Cancer Society's summary of the evidence notes that those studies "had some flaws that made their results hard to interpret," and regulators who re-examined them did not accept them as proof of a human risk. But the studies kept the controversy alive — and they are the reason that, in 2019, an international scientific advisory group gave aspartame high priority for review by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the WHO's cancer agency. That review, when it finally came in June 2023, produced the headline the whole world saw.

## What "Possibly Carcinogenic" Actually Means

On 14 July 2023, the WHO released the results of two separate evaluations of aspartame, conducted by two different bodies on the same evidence. The first was IARC, the International Agency for Research on Cancer. Citing "limited evidence" for carcinogenicity in humans, IARC classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic to humans — Group 2B. The evidence it pointed to was limited in a specific way: it concerned one type of cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, a form of liver cancer, and it came from just three observational studies of artificially sweetened drinks. There was also limited evidence from animal studies and limited evidence about a possible mechanism.

The second evaluation came from JECFA, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives — the body that actually sets safe intake levels. JECFA looked at the same data and concluded there was no sufficient reason to change the acceptable daily intake of 0–40 milligrams of aspartame per kilogram of body weight per day. In the words of Dr Moez Sanaa, the WHO's head of food safety standards, "the evidence of an association between aspartame consumption and cancer in humans is not convincing." The WHO's own release put the two findings side by side without apology, because they answer different questions.

That difference — between hazard and risk — is the single most misunderstood idea in this whole debate, and the WHO's release explains it in plain language. IARC's classifications "reflect the strength of scientific evidence as to whether an agent can cause cancer in humans, but they do not reflect the risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level." IARC asks whether something *can* cause cancer under some circumstances. JECFA and the FDA ask how likely it is to do so at the amounts people actually consume. Group 2B is the third-highest of IARC's four levels, and it is used when there is limited but not convincing evidence in humans. The category already contains some surprising company: whole-leaf aloe vera extract, traditional Asian pickled vegetables, and the radiofrequency fields from mobile phones all sit in Group 2B alongside aspartame. A Group 2B label is a flag that says "we cannot rule this out" — not a finding that normal use causes cancer.

Dr Francesco Branca, the WHO's director of nutrition and food safety, summed up the joint findings in a sentence that almost never makes it into the viral posts: "while safety is not a major concern at the doses which are commonly used, potential effects have been described that need to be investigated by more and better studies." That is not a cover-up. It is the normal, cautious language of a body that found a weak signal and wanted better data.

The FDA went further. Its response to the IARC decision states plainly that aspartame being labelled "possibly carcinogenic to humans" "does not mean that aspartame is actually linked to cancer," and that the agency "disagrees with IARC's conclusion that these studies support classifying aspartame as a possible carcinogen to humans." FDA scientists had reviewed the studies behind the IARC decision when they first became available in 2021 and identified what the agency called "significant shortcomings" in them. The FDA's position, repeated on its aspartame page, is that "scientific evidence has continued to support the FDA's conclusion that aspartame is safe for the general population when made under good manufacturing practices and used under the approved conditions of use." The European Food Safety Authority reached the same place in its full 2013 re-evaluation, concluding that aspartame and its breakdown products are safe at current levels of exposure, and in September 2026 it reconfirmed the daily limit of 40 milligrams per kilogram of body weight, saying the available evidence does not support a causal link between current dietary aspartame exposure and cancer.

So the scoreboard after the most consequential week in aspartame's history reads like this: one agency said "possibly, on limited evidence"; two others said "not convincing" and kept the sweetener approved. That split is not a scandal. It is what happens when the evidence is genuinely thin, and it is exactly why the claim that aspartame "causes cancer" overstates what any authority has actually concluded.

## What the Big Human Studies Actually Found

If the argument stopped at the 2023 headlines, the verdict would be a shrug: one agency worried, the others unconvinced. But the evidence base is much larger than one week in July 2023, and it is worth walking through study by study, because the pattern that emerges is consistent — small, inconsistent signals in observational data, and no consistent link when the studies are pooled together.

The study cited most often by people who believe aspartame causes cancer is France's NutriNet-Santé cohort, published in PLOS Medicine in 2022. It is a serious study, and its numbers deserve to be stated precisely. Researchers followed 102,865 French adults for a median of 7.8 years, using repeated dietary records that included brand names, and logged 3,358 cancers. Among people with the highest aspartame intake — above the median among sweetener users — the overall cancer rate was 15 percent higher than among people who used no artificial sweeteners at all, a hazard ratio of 1.15. The links were stronger for breast cancer, at 22 percent higher, and for obesity-related cancers as a group. Those numbers are real, and they are the strongest human evidence anyone has produced for a link.

But a hazard ratio of 1.15 in a single observational study is not proof of causation, and the study's own context explains why. People who consume large amounts of artificial sweeteners differ from people who consume none in many ways at once: they are more likely to be trying to lose weight, more likely to have diabetes or a family history of disease, and more likely to have other dietary patterns that independently track with cancer risk. Researchers call this confounding, and it is the central weakness of every observational study of sweeteners. The authors of the NutriNet-Santé study measured what people ate; they could not randomise who drank diet soda. The WHO's 2023 review of non-sugar sweeteners made the same point about this whole literature, noting that the associations could be distorted by reverse causation — people who are already gaining weight often switch to diet drinks, so the drink can end up statistically linked to the very problem people chose it to fight.

The counterweight to NutriNet-Santé is the sheer size of the studies that found nothing. The US National Cancer Institute's fact sheet on artificial sweeteners runs through them. The NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study — one of the largest diet-and-health cohorts ever assembled — found that higher consumption of aspartame-containing beverages was not associated with the development of lymphoma, leukaemia, or brain cancer during more than five years of follow-up. A 2013 review of the epidemiological evidence published between January 1990 and November 2012 found no consistent association between aspartame use and cancer risk. And the liver-cancer evidence behind IARC's 2023 classification itself was split down the middle: of the three studies IARC relied on, one found an association with liver cancer in the whole cohort, a second found it only among people with diabetes, and a third found no association at all. After the IARC panel met, a fourth study — published in JAMA Network Open in June 2026 — examined artificially sweetened drinks and liver cancer risk and also found no association after adjusting for differences between people.

When researchers pool the studies instead of cherry-picking them, the signal washes out further. A meta-analysis of 25 prospective studies covering 3,739,775 participants found no apparent overall association between artificial sweeteners and either cancer incidence or mortality. A separate review of 90 studies of non-sugar sweeteners, including aspartame, cited by the American Cancer Society, "did not find a consistent link to a risk of any cancer type." An umbrella review — a review of systematic reviews — that weighed the quality of the included studies found only weak evidence for an association between artificially sweetened beverages and either developing or dying from any cancer. The National Cancer Institute's verdict, after laying out all of this, is that the human studies "have not had consistent findings," and the American Cancer Society puts it even more plainly: "At this time, there is no consistent link between aspartame and cancer, although more research needs to be done."

That last clause matters. "More research needs to be done" is not a dodge; it is an honest description of a literature in which the positive findings are small, inconsistent, and confined to observational studies, while the null findings come from some of the largest cohorts ever run. The scientists who want better data are asking for the same things JECFA asked for in 2023: studies with longer follow-up, repeated dietary questionnaires in existing cohorts, and randomised controlled trials looking at the mechanisms that could plausibly connect a sweetener to cancer. Until those studies exist, the claim that aspartame causes cancer is a claim that outruns its evidence.

## The Numbers: How Much Would You Have to Drink?

Even if the hazard question were settled against aspartame, there would still be a second question that the viral posts almost never ask: how much would a person actually have to consume? This is where the claim collapses most completely, because the gap between ordinary use and the official safety limits is enormous.

The acceptable daily intake — the amount considered safe to consume every day over a lifetime — is 0–40 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day under JECFA and the European Food Safety Authority, and 50 milligrams per kilogram per day under the US FDA. Those are not targets to aim for; they are ceilings built with large safety margins, set far below the doses that caused any effect in animal studies. But they give a concrete sense of scale. A can of diet soft drink contains roughly 200 to 300 milligrams of aspartame. By the WHO's own arithmetic, an adult weighing 70 kilograms would need to drink more than 9 to 14 cans of diet soda every single day — with no aspartame from any other food — to exceed the JECFA limit. The FDA's illustration is even more vivid: a person weighing 60 kilograms would have to consume about 75 packets of tabletop aspartame in a day to reach its daily limit.

Almost nobody drinks 14 cans of diet soda a day. Surveys of actual consumption consistently find that even heavy consumers of artificially sweetened drinks sit far below the acceptable daily intake, which is why JECFA concluded in 2023 that dietary exposure "does not pose a health concern," and why the WHO's nutrition director could say that safety "is not a major concern at the doses which are commonly used" in the very same announcement that delivered the Group 2B classification. The two statements are not contradictory; they describe two different things. One says a hazard cannot be ruled out. The other says the exposure most people actually get is far below the level where anyone has shown harm.

There is a useful comparison here, and it is one the fact-checkers keep returning to. The same kind of reasoning that says "possibly carcinogenic, therefore dangerous" would condemn a long list of ordinary things. IARC's Group 2B contains aloe vera whole-leaf extract and traditional Asian pickled vegetables. Nobody has proposed banning pickles. The classification is a statement about the strength of the evidence, not a measurement of risk — and the measurement of risk, done by the bodies whose job is to measure it, keeps coming back below the level of concern.

That does not make aspartame a health food. In May 2023, two months before the IARC announcement, the WHO issued a separate guideline advising against using non-sugar sweeteners as a tool for weight control or for lowering the risk of diet-related disease, based on a systematic review that found the long-term weight benefits unconvincing and some observational links to type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. That guideline was a conditional recommendation — the WHO's language for advice where the evidence is suggestive rather than settled — and it was explicitly not a toxicological verdict on individual sweeteners. It does not change the ADI, and it does not say aspartame causes cancer. But it is a fair reason to be sceptical of the idea that switching from sugar to diet soda is a free health win. Water, as ever, is the better swap — a point the WHO's guideline makes without needing a cancer scare to justify it.

## Why the Headlines Sound Scarier Than the Science

If the evidence is this mixed, why does the claim feel so certain when you meet it online? Part of the answer is language. "Possibly carcinogenic to humans" sounds, in everyday English, like a polite way of saying "causes cancer." In IARC's system it means something much narrower: the evidence was strong enough to raise a possibility and too weak to establish causation. Headlines that drop the word "possibly" — and many did in July 2023 — are not summarising the finding; they are upgrading it. The WHO's release was careful to say the classification "does not reflect the risk of developing cancer at a given exposure level." Most of the posts that went viral that week were not careful at all.

Part of the answer is also that aspartame is not the first food ingredient to go through this cycle, and the pattern is worth recognising. Food scares follow a script: a genuine scientific development, a headline that strips out the qualifiers, and a social-media afterlife in which the stripped-down version gets repeated until it feels like consensus. Monosodium glutamate went through the same machinery — a 1968 letter about "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" turned a seasoning into a suspected toxin for decades, even as controlled trials failed to reproduce the symptoms, a story we traced in <a href="/blog/msg-dangerous-myth-fact-check/">Is MSG Dangerous? The Truth Behind the 'Chinese Restaurant Syndrome' Myth</a>. Seed oils are going through it now, with influencers calling them "toxic sludge" while the clinical evidence points elsewhere, as we found in <a href="/blog/seed-oils-toxic-claim-fact-check/">Are Seed Oils Toxic? What the Science Actually Says About the Viral Claim</a>. Aspartame is simply the longest-running entry in that genre: a real controversy from the 1970s, a real classification from 2023, and fifty years of retelling in between.

And part of the answer is timing. The 2026 resurgence of the claim is not driven by new science. No major regulator has changed its position since July 2023. The FDA still lists aspartame as approved and safe under its conditions of use, JECFA's daily intake still stands, and EFSA reconfirmed its limit in September 2026. What changed in 2026 was the feed: old clips, recut and reposted, finding a new audience that had never seen the original context. When a claim's evidence stays still for three years while its popularity surges, that is usually a sign that the surge is about the algorithm, not the science.

There is one more confusion worth clearing up, because it fuels a lot of the fear. Some posts conflate the WHO's May 2023 weight-control guideline with the July 2023 cancer classification, as if the WHO had issued two cancer warnings in two months. It did not. The May guideline was about whether sweeteners help people control weight — the WHO's systematic review found short-term trials showed small weight reductions when sweeteners replaced sugar, but the long-term observational data did not support using them for weight control, and the recommendation was explicitly conditional. It was dietary guidance, not a cancer finding, and the WHO said plainly that the guideline was not based on toxicological assessments of individual sweeteners and did not replace the daily intake limits. Treating it as a second cancer warning is a category error, and it is one of the most common errors in the viral versions of this claim.

## Who Should Actually Avoid Aspartame

For all the noise about cancer, there is exactly one group of people for whom aspartame is genuinely off-limits, and the reason has nothing to do with tumours. People with phenylketonuria, or PKU — a rare genetic disorder present from birth in which the body cannot break down phenylalanine, one of the amino acids aspartame is made from — must avoid or strictly limit it. That is why every product containing aspartame carries the warning "PHENYLKETONURICS: CONTAINS PHENYLALANINE," and why newborns are routinely tested for PKU with a heel-prick test before leaving hospital. The American Cancer Society's aspartame summary puts it bluntly: aspartame "hasn't been linked conclusively to any specific health problems except for people with phenylketonuria."

For everyone else, the practical question is not whether to fear a can of diet soda but whether the sweetener is doing anything useful. Aspartame is about 200 times sweeter than sugar, so it adds almost no calories and does not raise blood sugar — genuinely useful properties for someone managing diabetes or cutting calories. But the evidence that it helps with long-term weight control is weak, which is the substance of the WHO's 2023 guideline, and the broader question of sugar and sweeteners and metabolic health is still open — including the question of whether sugar itself causes diabetes, which we examined in <a href="/blog/does-sugar-cause-diabetes-fact-check/">Does Sugar Cause Diabetes? No — But Sugary Drinks Really Do Raise Your Risk</a>. The honest advice, from the agencies and from the evidence, is unglamorous: stay well below the daily intake limit (which almost everyone already does), read the label if you have PKU, and do not mistake a diet drink for a health drink. None of that requires believing aspartame causes cancer, and none of it is helped by believing that it does.

## The Verdict

Does aspartame cause cancer? The claim is **Misleading**.

The strongest statement any major authority has made is IARC's July 2023 classification of aspartame as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B), based on limited evidence for one type of liver cancer from three observational studies of artificially sweetened drinks. On the same day, JECFA reviewed the identical evidence, concluded that the evidence of an association "is not convincing," and kept the acceptable daily intake at 0–40 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day — a level a 70-kilogram adult would exceed only by drinking more than 9 to 14 cans of diet soda a day. The FDA disagrees with the IARC classification outright, citing shortcomings in the underlying studies, and maintains that aspartame is safe for the general population under approved conditions of use. The European Food Safety Authority concluded in 2013 that aspartame is safe at current levels of exposure and reconfirmed its daily limit in 2026.

The human studies point the same way: one large French cohort found a modest 15 percent higher overall cancer rate among the highest aspartame consumers, while the NIH-AARP cohort found no link with lymphoma, leukaemia, or brain cancer, a meta-analysis of 25 prospective studies covering more than 3.7 million people found no overall association with cancer, and a review of 90 studies found no consistent link to any cancer type. A "possibly carcinogenic" label is a statement about the strength of the evidence, not a measurement of risk — and every body whose job is to measure risk has concluded that normal consumption does not pose a cancer concern. More and better research is genuinely needed, and the weak signals deserve follow-up. But a claim that aspartame causes cancer takes a possibility, strips out the qualifiers, and sells it as a certainty. The evidence does not support that — and after sixty years of argument, that is the most useful thing anyone can say about it.

The next time a video tells you the WHO "admitted" aspartame causes cancer, remember the day it actually happened: 14 July 2023, two announcements, one classification, and a second body saying the evidence was not convincing and the limit stays. Remember the 9 to 14 cans. Remember that the agencies that measure risk, rather than the ones that classify hazards, have not moved in three years — and that the posts moved instead. Aspartame is not a health food, and the debate about sweeteners and weight is a real one. But "causes cancer" is a claim the evidence has spent half a century refusing to confirm, and no amount of reposting changes what the studies actually found.
