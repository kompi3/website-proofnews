Every parent has seen it, or thinks they have. A child eats a slice of birthday cake, downs a cup of soda, unwraps a handful of candy — and within minutes they are bouncing off the walls, shouting, running laps around the living room, utterly uncontainable. The diagnosis feels obvious: the sugar did it. Ask almost any parent whether sugar makes children hyperactive and you will get a confident yes, delivered with the authority of someone who has cleaned frosting off a ceiling.

It is one of the most durable beliefs in modern parenting, repeated in school newsletters, parenting books, and pediatric waiting rooms for half a century. It shapes real decisions: sugar-free birthday parties, banned candy at school, guilty parents wondering whether the cupcake they allowed caused the tantrum that followed. There is just one problem. When scientists actually tested the claim — carefully, repeatedly, in studies where neither the children, the parents, nor the researchers knew who had eaten sugar and who had not — the effect vanished. Again and again, across three decades, the sugar rush failed to show up in the data.

This is the story of a myth that survived its own debunking, and of the experiments that quietly proved one of parenting's most confident convictions wrong.

## The Claim Every Parent "Knows"

The claim is simple and specific: eating sugar makes children hyperactive — more active, more impulsive, less able to focus — within a short time of eating it. The supposed mechanism sounds plausible enough. Sugar enters the bloodstream quickly, blood glucose spikes, energy surges, and a small body with a developing nervous system cannot handle the rush. Parents describe it with the confidence of eyewitnesses, because they have, in a sense, witnessed it: children at birthday parties, after Halloween, on Christmas morning, are wild. And sugar is always there, right in the middle of the scene.

But eyewitnesses are unreliable, and that is especially true when the witness already believes what they are seeing. The history of this claim is a textbook case of how a plausible idea, born in a doctor's office in the 1970s, hardened into common sense before anyone had properly tested it — and then refused to die when the tests came back negative.

The belief is so widespread that it has been studied as a cultural phenomenon in its own right. Surveys in the United States and other countries have repeatedly found that large majorities of parents believe sugar causes hyperactivity in children. Teachers believe it too. The conviction crosses borders and generations. It is the kind of folk wisdom that feels too obvious to question — which is exactly why it needed questioning.

## Where the Sugar Rush Was Born

The modern sugar-hyperactivity theory traces back to 1975, when an allergist named Benjamin Feingold published a paper linking hyperactivity and learning difficulties in children to artificial food colors, flavors, and preservatives — and, in the public telling, to sugar as well. Feingold proposed that certain food additives triggered hyperactive behavior in sensitive children, and he developed a diet that eliminated them. Parents desperate for an explanation for their children's restlessness embraced the idea. The Feingold diet became a movement, and sugar — the most visible ingredient in the foods Feingold's diet restricted — became the villain in the popular version of the story.

There was a kernel of something real in Feingold's work, and we will come back to it. But the sugar part of the theory was, from the start, an extrapolation. Feingold's focus was additives, not sucrose. The public, the press, and eventually even some clinicians collapsed the distinction. By the 1980s, "sugar makes kids hyperactive" had become a fact of life, repeated so often that it no longer needed evidence. The only problem was that when researchers finally ran the experiments, the evidence refused to cooperate.

## The Meta-Analysis That Should Have Ended It

The single most important piece of evidence in this debate was published in 1995, in the Journal of the American Medical Association. Researchers led by Mark Wolraich of Vanderbilt University set out to settle the question by pooling every properly controlled study they could find. To be included, a study had to give children a known amount of sugar, include a placebo condition using an artificial sweetener, keep children, parents, and researchers blind to which condition each child was in, and report enough data to calculate effect sizes.

Sixteen reports met those criteria, representing 23 within-subject studies and more than 500 children, including children diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. The researchers sorted the results into 14 behavioral and cognitive constructs — activity level, attention, impulsivity, aggression, cognitive performance, academic performance, and more — and computed a pooled effect size for each one.

The result was unambiguous. Across all 14 constructs, every pooled 95 percent confidence interval included zero. The mean effect sizes ranged from minus 0.14 to plus 0.30, and the largest value in either direction was smaller than the conventional threshold for even a small effect. In plain language: sugar did not make children more hyperactive, less attentive, or cognitively worse than a placebo. The effect was, statistically speaking, nothing.

Wolraich and his colleagues were careful scientists, so they left one honest door open. They noted that a very small effect, or an effect in some unidentified subset of children, could not be completely ruled out. That caveat is worth remembering — it is the reason this article's verdict is False rather than a claim that sugar has been proven harmless in every conceivable circumstance. But the central finding was clear: the dramatic, obvious, bouncing-off-the-walls sugar rush that parents describe does not exist in the controlled data. If sugar caused hyperactivity the way the folklore says it does, 23 blinded experiments would have found it.

## Three Weeks of Sugar, Measured 39 Ways

A year before the meta-analysis, Wolraich's team had published an even more direct test in the New England Journal of Medicine. This study addressed a fair criticism of short laboratory challenges: perhaps a brief dose of sugar in a lab misses effects that only appear during ordinary life, over days or weeks of eating sugar every day. So the researchers designed a study to close that gap.

They recruited 25 preschool children and 23 school-age children whose parents already considered them sugar-sensitive — the very children, in other words, in whom an effect should be easiest to find. Each child cycled through three diets, three weeks each, in a randomized crossover design: a diet high in sucrose, a diet low in sucrose but sweetened with aspartame, and a diet low in sucrose sweetened with saccharin. The diets were run double-blind, so nobody involved knew which diet the child was on in any given period.

Behavior and cognitive performance were measured 39 ways. Across all 39 measures, the high-sugar diet produced no significant difference from either placebo diet. Not one. If three weeks of high sugar intake made children hyperactive, this study — in children selected precisely because their parents believed they were sugar-sensitive — should have detected it easily. It detected nothing.

That is a remarkable result, and it is worth pausing on. The children in this study were not a random sample; they were the children most likely to show an effect. Their parents had already concluded that sugar affected them. The researchers gave them three full weeks of high-sugar eating, measured them 39 different ways, and found nothing. The sugar rush did not merely fail to appear in a laboratory challenge. It failed to appear in the closest thing to a real-life test that science could construct.

## The Experiment That Removed Sugar Entirely

If the Wolraich studies are the strongest evidence against the sugar rush, the most elegant evidence comes from a 1994 experiment by Daniel Hoover and Richard Milich, published in the Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology. It is the study that explains why parents are so sure they have seen something that the data says is not there.

Hoover and Milich recruited 35 boys, aged 5 to 7, whose mothers reported that they were sensitive to sugar. Every child was given a drink sweetened with aspartame. No child received any sugar at all. But half the mothers were told — falsely — that their son had just had a large dose of sugar. The other half were told the truth.

Then the mothers watched their sons play, and rated their behavior. The mothers who believed their child had consumed sugar rated the child as significantly more hyperactive than the mothers who had been told the truth — even though every child in the study had received exactly the same sugar-free drink. The children had not changed. The mothers' expectations had.

This is what scientists call the expectancy effect, and it is devastating to the sugar-rush story. It shows that the belief itself creates the observation. A parent who expects a child to become wild after sugar will interpret normal energetic behavior as wildness, will notice the bouncing and ignore the quiet moments, and will remember the afternoon that confirms the belief while forgetting the afternoons that do not. The experiment did not merely fail to find an effect of sugar. It found the mechanism by which parents convince themselves the effect is real: the sugar was never in the drink. It was in the mother's mind.

## Why Birthday Parties Look Like Proof

If sugar does not cause hyperactivity, why does the evidence of every parent's eyes say otherwise? Because the occasions when children eat the most sugar are also the occasions when children are most excited, most stimulated, and least supervised — and it is nearly impossible, in real life, to separate the sugar from everything else happening at the same time.

Think about a birthday party. There are other children, often many of them. There is music, games, decorations, presents, the thrill of a special day, the disruption of routine, adults who are distracted by hosting, and permission — explicit or implied — to be louder and wilder than usual. A child at a birthday party is hyperactive because they are at a birthday party. The cake is incidental. The same is true of Halloween, Christmas morning, and the school fair: sugar is present, but so is excitement, novelty, social stimulation, and a loosening of the ordinary rules.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has made this point directly, noting that controlled research has not established sugar as a cause of hyperactivity, and suggesting that the stimulating circumstances surrounding occasions such as parties provide a better explanation for the behavior parents observe. The sugar is the most visible ingredient in the scene, so it gets the blame. The excitement is invisible, so it escapes notice.

There is also confirmation bias, the quiet engine that keeps the myth alive. A parent who believes sugar causes hyperactivity will notice and remember every wild afternoon that follows a sugary snack, and will forget or explain away every calm afternoon that follows one — and every wild afternoon that follows no sugar at all. Over years, this selective memory builds a mountain of personal evidence that feels irrefutable, because every piece of it was genuinely observed. It is just that the observations were selected by the belief, not by the sugar.

## Sugar and ADHD: A Separate Question

It is worth asking a related but distinct question: even if sugar does not cause a short-term rush, could it contribute to attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, the clinical condition? Here the evidence is more complicated, and honesty requires saying so.

Controlled trials — the kind that can establish cause and effect — have not found that sugar worsens ADHD symptoms. Children with ADHD were included in the Wolraich meta-analysis, and they showed no more response to sugar than other children. The 1994 New England Journal of Medicine trial included children whose parents considered them sugar-sensitive, and found no effect. On the direct causal question, the answer is the same for children with ADHD as for everyone else: no reliable effect.

Observational studies, which follow large groups of children over time and look for associations, tell a messier story. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis that combined seven observational studies with 25,945 participants reported a positive pooled association between sugar or sugar-sweetened beverage exposure and ADHD-related outcomes. A 2026 systematic review of dietary carbohydrates and ADHD symptoms found that sugar-related exposures were frequently associated with less favorable ADHD-related outcomes across recent studies — while also judging much of the observational evidence to have moderate or high risk of bias and describing the intervention evidence as variable.

That sounds contradictory, but it is not. Observational studies cannot tell whether sugar causes ADHD symptoms, or whether children with ADHD symptoms simply consume more sugary drinks — because impulsivity affects food choices, because families under stress reach for convenient foods, because screen time and sugary drinks travel together, and because dozens of other factors are tangled up in the same data. An association in an observational study is a clue, not a verdict. And when the studies that can actually test causation — the blinded, placebo-controlled trials — consistently find nothing, the clue points at something other than sugar itself.

There is one more piece of nuance. A 2019 birth cohort study that followed children from ages 6 to 11 found no association between sugar intake and the development of ADHD. And a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis found that diets high in refined sugar and saturated fat were associated with a greater likelihood of ADHD-like symptoms, though the authors described the evidence as weak. The honest summary is that overall diet quality matters for children's health in many ways, but the specific claim that sugar causes hyperactivity or ADHD has not survived controlled testing.

## The One Thing Feingold Got Right

Remember Benjamin Feingold, whose 1975 paper started all of this? It turns out his core idea — that something in food could affect children's behavior — was not entirely wrong. It was just not about sugar.

In 2007, a landmark randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in The Lancet tested artificial food colorings and the preservative sodium benzoate in 3-year-olds and 8- and 9-year-olds from the general population. The study, led by Jim Stevenson at the University of Southampton, found that certain artificial colors and additives did produce a measurable increase in hyperactive behavior in both age groups. The effect was modest, and it was about additives, not sucrose — but it was real, and it was found with the same rigorous methods that had failed to find any effect for sugar.

This matters for two reasons. First, it vindicates the methods: the same kind of study that found nothing for sugar did find something for food colorings, which means the sugar studies were not simply too insensitive to detect real effects. Second, it explains part of the confusion. Many of the foods that parents associate with sugar rushes — brightly colored candies, frosted cakes, neon sodas, fruit drinks — are also loaded with artificial colors and preservatives. When a child eats a bag of vividly colored candy, they are consuming sugar and additives together. If the additives nudge behavior even slightly, parents will blame the ingredient they can see and name: the sugar. The real culprit, if there is one, hides in the ingredient list.

That said, the Southampton finding should not be overstated. The effect was small, it applied to specific additives, and regulators responded proportionately — the European Union, for example, now requires warning labels on foods containing certain artificial colors. It does not rescue the sugar-rush myth. It refines it into something narrower and less dramatic: some additives, in some children, may slightly increase hyperactive behavior. Sugar itself, tested on its own, does not.

## What Sugar Actually Does to Children

Debunking the sugar rush does not mean sugar is harmless. That would be a false and irresponsible conclusion, and it is important to be clear about what the evidence does and does not say. Sugar does not make children hyperactive. It does do other things, and parents are right to limit it — for the right reasons.

The strongest evidence concerns teeth. Sugar feeds the bacteria that produce the acid responsible for tooth decay, and frequent sugary snacks and drinks are one of the clearest dietary risk factors for cavities in children. That is a settled matter, and it is a much better reason to limit candy than any fear of hyperactivity.

Excess sugar also contributes to excess calorie intake, and sugar-sweetened drinks in particular are associated with weight gain in children, because liquid calories do not satisfy hunger the way solid food does. A child who drinks several sodas a day is consuming a substantial number of calories that add nothing nutritionally. Over time, that pattern is linked to obesity and to the metabolic problems that follow it. For more on that angle, our fact-check on <a href="/blog/do-energy-drinks-actually-give-you-energy-fact-check/">do energy drinks actually give you energy</a> looks at how sugary, caffeinated drinks affect the body — and why the lift they promise is mostly an illusion.

The World Health Organization recommends that both adults and children keep free sugars — the sugars added to foods and drinks, plus the sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and fruit juices — below 10 percent of total daily energy intake, with a further reduction below 5 percent potentially providing additional benefits. That guidance is about dental health and weight, not behavior. It is worth following. It just has nothing to do with hyperactivity.

There is also the question of what replaces sugar. Some parents, convinced that sugar is the problem, switch their children to artificially sweetened drinks or sugar-free candy — and are then surprised when the behavior does not change. It does not change because sugar was never the cause. The party is still exciting, the other children are still there, and the expectation is still in the parent's mind.

## Why the Myth Refuses to Die

The sugar-rush myth has now survived more than thirty years of consistent negative evidence. The Wolraich meta-analysis was published in 1995. The New England Journal of Medicine trial was published in 1994. The expectancy experiment was published in 1994. A generation of parents has grown up, had children, and passed the belief on to their own children, all while the scientific literature said the opposite. Why?

Part of the answer is that the belief is useful. It gives parents an explanation for behavior they find difficult, and an explanation that points outward — to a food, to a birthday party, to a well-meaning grandparent with a candy dish — rather than inward, to the child's temperament or the parent's own exhaustion. It gives teachers a story about the child who cannot sit still after lunch. And it gives everyone a simple, actionable rule: cut the sugar, fix the behavior. Simple rules are comforting, even when they are wrong.

Part of the answer is that the belief is constantly refreshed by experience. Every birthday party, every Halloween, every holiday provides a new vivid confirmation. The controlled trials are abstract; the party is right there in front of you. It takes real discipline to notice that the child was already excited before the cake arrived, and to remember the quiet afternoons when the same child ate the same candy and did nothing remarkable at all.

And part of the answer is that debunking a belief is not the same as replacing it. When a parent learns that sugar does not cause hyperactivity, the natural next question is: then what does cause my child's wild afternoons? The honest answer — excitement, stimulation, tiredness, the social dynamics of a room full of children, and sometimes just being a child — is less satisfying than a villain in a candy wrapper. Myths persist when they are more useful than the truth.

There is also a commercial dimension. The market for sugar-free and low-sugar children's products is enormous, and it is marketed heavily to parents who worry about hyperactivity. Food companies have little incentive to correct a belief that sells their products. Parenting media, meanwhile, repeats the claim because it is popular, and it is popular because it is repeated. The cycle sustains itself.

## What Parents Should Do Instead

If sugar is not the cause, what should a parent do when their child seems wild after a party or a sugary snack? The evidence points to a few practical, unglamorous answers.

First, look at the context before the food. Was the child already excited? Had they slept badly? Were they in a stimulating environment with other children? Was the routine disrupted? In most cases, the answer to at least one of those questions is yes — and any one of them is a better explanation for the behavior than the cupcake.

Second, keep the sugar rules about the right things. Limit sugary foods and drinks because of teeth and weight, not because of behavior. That framing is both more honest and more effective, because it does not set parents up for disappointment when cutting sugar fails to produce a calmer child. A child who eats less sugar will have healthier teeth. They will not necessarily be quieter — and expecting them to be quieter is how the myth renews itself.

Third, if a child genuinely struggles with sustained hyperactivity, impulsivity, or inattention that interferes with school or daily life, that deserves a conversation with a pediatrician — not a dietary experiment. ADHD is a real neurodevelopmental condition with effective, evidence-based treatments. Attributing its symptoms to sugar delays proper assessment and support, and it burdens families with guilt about birthday cake that was never the problem. Children with ADHD were included in the controlled sugar trials, and sugar did not affect them either. The condition deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms.

Finally, notice the expectation effect in yourself. The Hoover and Milich experiment is a mirror as much as a study. Every parent has, at some point, watched their child through the lens of a belief rather than through clear eyes. Catching yourself doing it — noticing that you called a normal burst of energy a sugar rush because you expected a sugar rush — is the single most useful thing the science of this myth has to offer. It does not make parenting easier. It makes it more honest.

## The Verdict

**False.** Sugar does not make children hyperactive.

The evidence for this verdict is unusually strong and unusually consistent. A 1995 meta-analysis in JAMA pooled 23 blinded, placebo-controlled studies and found no effect of sugar on children's behavior or cognition across 14 measured constructs. A 1994 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine fed children high-sugar diets for three weeks at a time, measured them 39 ways, and found no difference from placebo diets — even in children whose parents believed they were sugar-sensitive. And a 1994 expectancy experiment showed that mothers who believed their child had consumed sugar rated the child as more hyperactive even though every child had received a placebo. The behavior parents observe is real. The cause they attribute it to is not.

The honest caveats are these. The JAMA authors noted that a very small effect, or an effect in an unidentified subset of children, could not be ruled out. Observational studies do find associations between sugary drinks and ADHD-related symptoms, though those associations are tangled with other factors and the controlled trials that test causation find nothing. And artificial food colors and the preservative sodium benzoate — not sugar — showed a modest effect on hyperactive behavior in a rigorous 2007 Lancet trial. None of this rescues the claim that sugar makes children hyperactive. It refines the edges of a verdict that is, at its core, settled.

Sugar should still be limited in children's diets — for their teeth, for their weight, and for their long-term metabolic health. The World Health Organization's guidance to keep free sugars below 10 percent of daily energy intake is worth following. But it should be followed for the right reasons. The birthday cake did not make your child bounce off the walls. The birthday party did. And the most honest thing a parent can do with that knowledge is to enjoy the party, brush the teeth afterward, and let the myth go.
