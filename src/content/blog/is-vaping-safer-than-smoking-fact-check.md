Walk into any vape shop and you will see the pitch written on the wall, the packaging, and the social media ads: a cleaner, safer alternative to cigarettes. Scroll through a parenting forum and you will find the opposite claim, delivered with equal confidence: vaping is just as dangerous as smoking, maybe worse, and anyone who tells you otherwise is repeating industry marketing. Both sides sound certain. Both sides cannot be right. And because more than 100 million people worldwide now vape, including at least 15 million adolescents aged 13 to 15, according to the World Health Organization's 2025 global tobacco report, the question of whether vaping is genuinely safer than smoking is no longer an academic debate. It is a public health question with real consequences for smokers trying to quit, teenagers picking up their first device, and parents trying to tell the difference between a harm-reduction tool and a new addiction dressed up as one.

The short answer is that the claim is mostly true, but only in a specific and easily misunderstood sense. For an adult who already smokes and switches completely to vaping, the evidence consistently shows a substantial reduction in harm. For a non-smoker, a teenager, or anyone who vapes alongside cigarettes instead of replacing them, the same claim falls apart. Vaping is safer than smoking in the way that jumping from the second floor is safer than jumping from the tenth: the comparison is real, the improvement is large, and neither option is safe.

## The Claim

The claim takes several forms, and the form matters. The version you hear most often in vape shops and harm-reduction advocacy is that vaping is 95 percent less harmful than smoking. The version you hear from the World Health Organization is nearly the opposite: e-cigarettes are harmful, there is no evidence of their net benefit for public health, and the products are fuelling a new wave of nicotine addiction among young people. The version you hear from the UK's National Health Service sits in between: vaping is not completely risk-free, but it poses a small fraction of the risk of smoking cigarettes.

All three statements can be defended, because they are answering different questions. The vape-shop version is a claim about relative harm for an individual smoker who switches. The WHO version is a claim about population-level effects, where millions of teenagers who would never have smoked are now inhaling nicotine. The NHS version is a claim about the same comparison the vape shop makes, stated with the caution of a public health agency. To fact-check "is vaping safer than smoking," we have to pin down which question we are actually asking, because the honest answer changes with the person asking it.

## Why This Question Is So Hard to Answer

Cigarette smoking has roughly seventy years of research behind it. Scientists know with precision what decades of smoking do to the human body, because they have followed millions of smokers for their entire lives. Vaping has about fifteen years of research behind it, most of it conducted on devices that no longer represent what people actually use today. The high-powered pod systems and disposables that dominate the market now run hotter, deliver nicotine more efficiently, and contain different flavour chemicals than the early cigalike devices studied in 2015 and 2016.

That time gap creates a genuine scientific problem. The diseases that make smoking so deadly — lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart disease — take decades to develop. A product that has only been widely used since around 2015 cannot yet have twenty-year outcome data, because twenty years have not passed. The NHS states this plainly: the long-term risks of vaping are not yet clear. That is not evasion. It is the honest limit of what the science can currently say, and any confident claim about the lifetime safety of vaping is pretending that limit does not exist. It is the same kind of long-term uncertainty that surrounds other widely repeated health claims, such as <a href="/blog/do-cell-phones-cause-brain-cancer-fact-check/">do cell phones cause brain cancer</a>, where decades of data were needed before researchers could give a confident answer.

There is a second problem. "Vaping" is not one product. It is thousands of devices, liquids, flavourings, nicotine strengths, and heating temperatures, plus an enormous illicit market of unregulated cartridges. A regulated nicotine vape bought from a licensed shop and an illicit THC cartridge bought from a stranger are both "vaping" in casual conversation, but they expose the lungs to very different chemicals. Any honest assessment has to keep that distinction in view, because the worst vaping disaster on record was caused not by ordinary nicotine vaping but by a specific additive in illicit products.

## What Is Actually in the Smoke — and in the Aerosol

The core of the harm-reduction argument is combustion. A cigarette burns tobacco at temperatures above 600 degrees Celsius. That burning produces smoke containing more than 7,000 chemicals, including tar and carbon monoxide and more than 70 known carcinogens. Tar coats the lungs and drives cancer and chronic lung disease. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin and starves the heart and other organs of oxygen. The smoke also contains formaldehyde, benzene, hydrogen cyanide, and a long list of other compounds that no one would willingly inhale.

A vape does not burn anything. It heats a liquid — usually propylene glycol, vegetable glycerine, flavourings, and nicotine — to between roughly 150 and 350 degrees Celsius, producing an aerosol. Because there is no combustion, there is no tar and no carbon monoxide. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that this is the main reason e-cigarettes are considered less harmful than combustible cigarettes: removing combustion removes a large share of smoking's most damaging byproducts.

But the aerosol is not clean air. It still delivers nicotine, which is powerfully addictive. It contains fine particles that can penetrate deep into the lungs. It contains flavouring chemicals that were approved for eating, not for heating and inhaling, and some of those chemicals break down into irritants at vaping temperatures. It can contain heavy metals such as nickel and chromium, shed from the metal heating coil inside the device. And because the market includes unregulated products, it can contain whatever an unscrupulous manufacturer decided to add. The difference between smoke and aerosol is therefore enormous in scale — thousands of toxicants versus dozens — but it is a difference of degree, not a difference between poison and purity.

## The 95% Figure: Where It Came From and What It Really Means

No number in the vaping debate is quoted more often, or more misleadingly, than "95 percent less harmful." It comes from a 2015 evidence review commissioned by Public Health England, which concluded that the best current estimate was that e-cigarettes were around 95 percent less harmful than tobacco cigarettes. The figure was later repeated in government evidence reviews and in Parliament, and it became the single most repeated statistic in the entire debate.

Here is what most people who quote it do not know: the number was an expert judgement, not a measurement from a clinical trial. It traced back to a 2014 multi-criteria analysis in which a panel of experts rated nicotine products against each other across a range of harm criteria. E-cigarettes scored about 4 percent of the maximum relative harm of cigarettes, and Public Health England rounded that into its famous "around 95 percent less harmful" headline. The authors themselves said their understanding of e-cigarette hazards was at a very early stage and based on limited evidence. The Lancet published an editorial questioning the foundation of the figure, and researchers have argued ever since that it overstates the confidence we can have, given how much long-term data is still missing.

The 95 percent figure is also routinely misread as a claim about individual diseases. It is not a claim that vaping cuts your cancer risk by exactly 95 percent, or your heart disease risk by exactly 95 percent, or any single risk by exactly 95 percent. No study has measured those numbers over decades, because vaping has not existed for decades. What the figure represents is a broad expert judgement that the overall harm profile of vaping is a small fraction of smoking's — a judgement that later evidence reviews have broadly supported in direction, while declining to endorse the precise number.

The Royal College of Physicians, in its landmark 2016 report, reached a similar conclusion in different words: the hazard from long-term vapour inhalation was unlikely to exceed 5 percent of the harm from smoking tobacco, while stressing that the long-term picture was still uncertain. The House of Commons Science and Technology Committee, in its 2018 inquiry, concluded there was clear evidence that e-cigarettes were substantially less harmful than conventional cigarettes, while adding that the available evidence did not allow a precise figure to be determined. The pattern is consistent: every serious review agrees on the direction and the rough scale, and none of them will commit to the exact percentage.

## What the Big Evidence Reviews Actually Say

The most comprehensive assessment ever conducted was the UK government's Office for Health Improvement and Disparities evidence review of 2022, the eighth and final annual independent review in the series. It concluded that vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking in the short and medium term. The Royal College of Physicians returned to the question in 2024 and concluded there is clear evidence that e-cigarettes cause less harm to health than combustible tobacco. The NHS, which recommends e-cigarettes as a stop-smoking aid, states that vaping poses a small fraction of the risk of smoking, and that it does not produce tar or carbon monoxide — two of the most harmful elements in tobacco smoke.

The evidence is not only about toxicant levels. Biomarker studies, which measure the actual chemicals inside people's bodies, tell the same story. A 2021 study published in the journal Tobacco Control compared smokers, exclusive vapers, and dual users. Exclusive vapers showed dramatically lower urinary levels of 1-OHP, a biomarker for polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons linked to lung cancer, and NNAL, a tobacco-specific nitrosamine carcinogen — roughly 95 percent lower and 90 percent lower respectively than smokers. But the same study found that exclusive vapers had higher levels of certain metal biomarkers, nickel and chromium, compared with non-smokers, likely from metal coil degradation in the devices. The improvement is real and large, and it is not total.

A 2024 study in the American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine examined airway biopsies from never-smokers, long-term vapers of five years or more, and long-term smokers. Vapers showed intermediate levels of airway inflammation: elevated inflammatory markers not seen in never-smokers, but substantially lower than in smokers. The lungs of a vaper, in other words, look better than the lungs of a smoker and worse than the lungs of someone who inhales nothing at all. That is exactly what the "substantially less harmful but not harmless" framing predicts.

There is also strong evidence that vaping helps smokers quit, which is a different question from whether it is safer, but a closely related one. A Cochrane systematic review — the most rigorous type of evidence synthesis in medicine — found high-certainty evidence that people who used nicotine e-cigarettes were more likely to quit smoking than those using other nicotine replacement products such as gum or patches. The 2025 update of that review, by Lindson and colleagues, reached the same conclusion. Public Health England's 2018 report noted that at least 20,000 smokers successfully quit each year with the help of e-cigarettes. For a smoker who has tried and failed with other methods, that is not a trivial finding.

## The Risks Vaping Still Carries

None of the above makes vaping safe, and the research on its risks has grown steadily more detailed. Nicotine itself, while not classified as a carcinogen, is not harmless. It raises heart rate and blood pressure, constricts blood vessels, and promotes platelet aggregation — the clumping of blood cells that contributes to clots. A 2023 meta-analysis in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology found that vapers had similar short-term cardiovascular endpoints to smokers, although long-term outcome data is not yet available. A systematic scoping review by Izquierdo-Condoy and colleagues in 2024 found that vaping is generally associated with a lower cardiovascular risk profile than conventional cigarettes, but that it can still acutely increase blood pressure, heart rate, and arterial stiffness.

The respiratory risks are also real. A systematic review by Mughis and colleagues in 2024, examining the toxicity mechanisms of electronic nicotine delivery systems on the respiratory system, found that vape aerosol can cause harm through inflammation, tissue hypoxia, stress on the cardiovascular system, and metabolic changes. Public Health Wales noted in its February 2026 position statement that in the short term, vaping may cause headache, cough, throat irritation, dizziness, and nausea in some users. These are not the symptoms of a harmless product. They are the symptoms of a product that irritates the airways of a meaningful minority of its users.

The flavouring chemicals deserve particular attention, because they are the least studied part of the aerosol. Many flavourings were approved as safe to eat, which says nothing about their safety when heated and inhaled. Diacetyl, a buttery flavouring, became notorious after it was linked to "popcorn lung," a serious and irreversible lung condition, in factory workers who inhaled it. Cinnamon and menthol flavourings have shown toxic effects on lung cells in laboratory studies. The regulatory picture varies by country, and illicit products are not tested at all, which means the person vaping cannot always know what they are inhaling.

## EVALI: The Outbreak That Changed the Conversation

In the summer of 2019, emergency departments across the United States began reporting an unusual cluster of severe lung injuries among people who reported vaping. The first cases were identified in July 2019 in Wisconsin and Illinois. By February 18, 2020, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention had counted 2,807 hospitalized cases of what it named EVALI — e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury — and 68 confirmed deaths across all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and two US territories. Case counts peaked in September 2019 and then declined steadily through early 2020.

The investigation that followed is a case study in how public health detective work is supposed to function. Researchers analysed lung fluid from patients and found vitamin E acetate — an oily additive used to dilute and thicken illicit THC vape cartridges — in the lung fluid of 48 out of 51 EVALI patients, and in none of a control group of 99 healthy people who vaped nicotine only, smoked only, or did neither. More than 80 percent of EVALI patients reported using THC-containing products, and about three quarters were under 35 years old. Animal studies confirmed that mice exposed to inhaled vitamin E acetate developed lung injury markers consistent with EVALI. The CDC concluded that vitamin E acetate was strongly linked to the outbreak, while noting that the evidence was not sufficient to rule out the contribution of other chemicals.

EVALI matters for this fact-check in two ways. First, it shows that vaping can cause severe, acute lung injury — not merely theoretical long-term risk, but hospitalisation and death within weeks of use. Second, it shows how important the distinction between regulated nicotine products and illicit products is. The outbreak was driven overwhelmingly by illicit THC cartridges containing a cutting agent that licensed nicotine products do not contain. People who cite EVALI as proof that all vaping is deadly are making the same error as people who cite it as irrelevant: they are treating "vaping" as a single product when the evidence says it is a category containing very different risks.

## The Youth Problem — Where the Claim Falls Apart

Everything above describes adult smokers. The picture changes completely when the person vaping has never smoked — and that is now a large and growing group. The World Health Organization's 2025 global tobacco report estimated that at least 15 million adolescents aged 13 to 15 use e-cigarettes, and that in the 63 countries with available data, adolescents vape at a rate on average nine times higher than adults. More than 100 million people worldwide now vape, including at least 86 million adults, mostly in high-income countries. The WHO's director-general, speaking at a ministerial roundtable in November 2025, put the agency's position bluntly: marketing addictive, toxic products to teenagers who are not already tobacco users with bright colours, sweet flavours, and social media influencers is not harm reduction, it is harm production.

The United States shows the same pattern in miniature. The CDC's National Youth Tobacco Survey found that about 8 percent of middle and high school students reported current tobacco use in the most recent cycle, with nearly three quarters of them vaping e-cigarettes, and about 89 percent of the students who vaped using flavoured products, with fruit, candy, and mint among the most common. Other recent federal data put current e-cigarette use at roughly one in ten high school students — down from a peak of about 27.5 percent in 2019, but still representing millions of adolescents.

The health concern for teenagers is not only about lungs. Nicotine does not act on a young brain the way it acts on an adult brain. The CDC states that nicotine can harm the parts of an adolescent's brain that control attention, learning, mood, and impulse control. It is a reminder that substances adults tolerate can affect developing brains differently — a theme that also appears in the question of <a href="/blog/does-alcohol-kill-brain-cells-fact-check/">does alcohol kill brain cells</a>. A 2025 review in Molecular Psychiatry argued that adolescence is a sensitive window for the acetylcholine and dopamine signalling systems that shape attention, motivation, and mood, and that nicotine exposure during that window can leave lasting changes, linked to a higher risk of substance abuse, anxiety and depression, and schizophrenia later in life. Stanford Medicine's Dr Bonnie Halpern-Felsher, a youth tobacco expert, has pointed out that some vapes and nicotine pouches contain double or triple the amount of nicotine present in a standard pack of cigarettes, and that young people are biologically more prone to addiction than adults. Nearly nine out of ten adult daily smokers started before the age of 18, according to the CDC — which is why the industry's interest in young users is not accidental.

For a teenager who has never smoked, vaping adds risk without removing any. The "safer than smoking" comparison is meaningless for someone who was never going to smoke, because the alternative to vaping for that person is not a cigarette — it is breathing clean air. This is the sense in which the WHO is right to say there is no evidence of vaping's net benefit for public health, even while the NHS is right to say that vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking for an individual smoker. Both statements are true, because they describe different populations.

## The Dual-Use Trap

There is one more group for whom the claim quietly fails: people who both vape and smoke. Dual use is common, and it is the worst of both worlds in one important respect — the person keeps inhaling cigarette smoke, which means they keep taking on the full harm of smoking, while adding the additional exposures of vaping. The health benefit of switching to vaping depends on switching completely. A smoker who vapes on weekdays and smokes at weekends has not meaningfully reduced their risk, and may have made quitting harder by keeping their nicotine intake high around the clock.

This is why public health agencies are careful with their language. The NHS supports e-cigarettes as a tool to help people stop smoking, not as a lifestyle product. The Royal College of Physicians frames vaping as harm reduction for people who cannot or will not stop using nicotine — not as a safe habit in its own right. England's Chief Medical Officer, Professor Sir Chris Whitty, summarised the official position in one sentence that is worth memorising: "if you smoke, vaping is much safer; if you don't smoke, don't vape."

## What About Secondhand Vapour?

A related claim often travels with the main one: that secondhand vapour is harmless, or at least nothing to worry about. The evidence here is thinner but points in a reassuring direction. The House of Commons Science and Technology Committee found that researchers had found it almost impossible to measure the risks from secondhand e-cigarette vapour, because any potentially harmful compounds released into the surrounding area were so negligible. That is not proof of zero risk — it is evidence that the risk to bystanders is far smaller than the risk from secondhand cigarette smoke, which is a known cause of lung cancer and heart disease in non-smokers. The honest statement is that secondhand vapour appears to pose minimal risk to bystanders, while noting that the research base is small and that vaping around children, pregnant people, or people with lung conditions is still discouraged. Pregnancy is a period when safety questions carry extra weight, as our fact-check on <a href="/blog/is-it-safe-to-drink-coffee-while-pregnant-fact-check/">is it safe to drink coffee while pregnant</a> also found — and vaping during pregnancy is not recommended by health authorities.

## The Verdict

So, is vaping safer than smoking? The evidence supports the claim — with qualifications that are not optional extras but part of the answer itself.

For an adult who already smokes and switches completely to vaping, yes. The UK's major evidence reviews — Public Health England in 2015, the Royal College of Physicians in 2016 and again in 2024, the Office for Health Improvement and Disparities in 2022, and the House of Commons Science and Technology Committee in 2018 — all reach the same conclusion: vaping is substantially less harmful than smoking, because it removes combustion, tar, and carbon monoxide. Biomarker studies confirm it inside the body: exclusive vapers carry dramatically lower levels of the carcinogens found in smokers. And Cochrane's systematic reviews show that nicotine e-cigarettes help more smokers quit than traditional nicotine replacement products. That is the sense in which the claim is true, and it is a large and well-supported sense.

But the famous "95 percent less harmful" figure should not be quoted as a precise measurement. It was an expert judgement built on a small panel exercise in 2014, when the evidence base was thin. Later reviews support the direction of the claim while declining to endorse the exact number. Anyone who tells you vaping cuts your risk of every smoking-related disease by exactly 95 percent is overstating what the science says.

And the claim is false — or at best meaningless — for the people it is most often aimed at today. For a non-smoker, a teenager, or anyone who vapes alongside cigarettes, vaping adds risk rather than reducing it. Vaping carries real risks of its own: nicotine addiction, raised blood pressure and heart rate, airway inflammation, exposure to flavouring chemicals and metals, and — as the EVALI outbreak showed with 2,807 hospitalisations and 68 deaths — the possibility of severe acute lung injury from unregulated products. The long-term effects of decades of vaping are genuinely unknown, because the decades have not happened yet.

The fairest summary is the one England's Chief Medical Officer gave: if you smoke, vaping is much safer; if you don't smoke, don't vape. The claim "vaping is safer than smoking" earns a verdict of Mostly True — true for the smokers it was designed to help, misleading when it is used to sell a nicotine habit to people who never smoked at all.

## The Bottom Line for Readers

If you smoke and you have tried to quit and failed, switching completely to vaping is likely to reduce your health risk substantially — and the evidence for that is stronger than for almost any other claim in the vaping debate. Talk to your doctor or a stop-smoking service, buy regulated products from licensed sellers, and treat vaping as a step away from nicotine, not a new permanent habit. If you have never smoked, there is no health argument for starting to vape: you would be taking on addiction, airway irritation, and unknown long-term risks in exchange for nothing. And if you are a parent, the most important number in this article is not 95 percent. It is 15 million — the number of 13- to 15-year-olds worldwide who now vape, most of whom never smoked a cigarette in their lives.

The tobacco industry spent a century selling cigarettes to teenagers. The vaping industry is selling a new nicotine product to teenagers with brighter packaging and sweeter flavours. The fact that vaping is safer than smoking for a 50-year-old smoker does not make it safe for a 15-year-old who was never going to smoke. Both facts are true at the same time, and any honest answer to the question has to hold them both.
