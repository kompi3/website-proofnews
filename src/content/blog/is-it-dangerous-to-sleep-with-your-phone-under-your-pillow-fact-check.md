It is one of the most ordinary bedtime routines in the world. You plug in your phone, you climb into bed, and the phone ends up somewhere near your head — on the mattress, beside the pillow, or tucked underneath it so the alarm will be close in the morning and the device will be fully charged when you wake up. Millions of people do it every night without thinking twice. And every so often, a warning goes viral telling them to stop, usually illustrated with a photograph of scorched bedding and a melted phone.

So which is it: a harmless habit, or a fire waiting to happen? The answer, as with so many viral warnings, is more specific than the version that gets shared. Sleeping with a phone under your pillow is genuinely dangerous in one particular set of circumstances — when the phone is charging, covered by bedding, and unable to shed heat. Outside those circumstances, the most dramatic version of the warning falls apart: an unplugged phone under a pillow is not silently dosing you with cancer-causing radiation, and a healthy modern phone does not spontaneously burst into flames just because it spent the night near your head. But the part of the warning that matters — the fire part — is backed by fire departments, by phone manufacturers' own safety manuals, and by real incidents in which bedding caught fire. That is why the verdict here is Mostly True, with an important condition attached: the danger is real, but it lives in the charging and the covering, not in the pillow itself.

## The warning that keeps going viral

The modern version of this warning traces back to June 2017, when the Newton, New Hampshire, Fire Department published a post urging parents to check where their children were charging their phones and tablets. The department's message was simple: devices charging on beds or beneath pillows could become dangerously hot because the heat had nowhere to go, and it shared photographs of damaged bedding to make the point. The post spread rapidly online and was picked up by news organizations across the United States.

The figure most often repeated from that warning is that 53 percent of children and teenagers charge their phones or tablets on their beds or under their pillows. That statistic has been quoted so many times that it now travels without its original source attached, and careful retellings of the story note that the research behind it is no longer clearly identified. The number itself should therefore be treated with caution. But the safety advice attached to it has been repeated independently by fire services on both sides of the Atlantic, which is a much stronger signal than any single viral statistic.

Surrey Fire and Rescue Service in the United Kingdom put it bluntly: "Charging your devices under bedding for extended periods of time can cause them to overheat and catch fire!" Its advice was equally direct — charge devices on a hard, flat surface, unplug them before going to bed, and replace chargers that are damaged or discoloured. In the United States, the West Monroe Fire Department in Louisiana gave the same guidance, saying phones should be charged on a firm surface rather than on soft furnishings such as beds, sofas, or beneath pillows, because of the risks associated with the lithium-ion batteries inside smartphones. When fire departments in different countries, with no connection to one another, keep issuing the same warning, it is worth listening — even if the viral post that started the conversation was imperfect.

There is also a less-discussed part of the warning. Fire services have noted that the risk is not only fire. In one unusual case reported in 2025, a young boy in Wichita was electrocuted while sleeping next to a phone that was charging. Cases like that are rare and depend on specific faults and circumstances, but they underline the same underlying point: a charging cable, a live electrical device, and a sleeping person in a bed full of soft, flammable material are a combination that deserves more respect than it usually gets.

## The Texas teenager whose phone melted under her pillow

The single most-cited incident behind this warning happened years before the Newton post, in Texas in 2014. Thirteen-year-old Ariel Tolfree fell asleep with her Samsung Galaxy S4 under her pillow. During the night she woke to the smell of something burning, thought little of it, and went back to sleep. When she woke again, the smell was stronger — and her phone had partially melted, scorching her pillow, her sheets, and her mattress. Her father later told a local television station that the battery appeared to have swollen, and photographs of the aftermath showed a handset so damaged it was barely recognizable as a phone.

Samsung's response added an important complication to the story. The company said the phone had been fitted with a third-party replacement battery rather than an original Samsung battery, and it blamed that incompatible battery for the incident. That detail matters, because it means the Texas case is not proof that any phone will catch fire under any pillow. It is proof of something narrower and arguably more useful: a phone with a compromised or incompatible battery, generating heat while covered by insulating material, can fail in a way that sets bedding alight. Nobody was hurt in the Texas incident, and Samsung replaced the phone and the bedding, but the photographs did their work — they made an abstract warning concrete.

The phone's own manual had, in fact, warned about exactly this scenario. Samsung's health and safety guidance stated that covering the device with bedding, your body, thick clothing, or any other materials that significantly affect air flow could affect the phone's performance and posed a possible risk of fire or explosion, which could lead to serious bodily injury or damage to property. That warning was sitting in the fine print all along. Most people never read it, which is precisely why fire departments ended up repeating it in public.

## Why a pillow turns a warm phone into a hazard

To understand why the pillow matters, it helps to understand what a phone battery does while it charges. Every lithium-ion battery generates some heat as it charges — that is normal physics, not a defect. On a hard nightstand in open air, that warmth drifts away and the phone stays within a safe temperature range. Modern phones also help themselves: they slow or stop charging when they get too hot, and features such as optimized charging hold the battery below full charge for much of the night to reduce stress on the cell.

A pillow reverses all of that. Bedding is an insulator. It traps the heat the phone produces instead of letting it escape, so the temperature around the battery climbs steadily through the night. If the phone is also working hard at the same time — downloading updates, playing video, running apps while it charges — it generates even more heat. If the battery is old, swollen, or damaged, or if the charger is a cheap counterfeit without proper voltage regulation, the margin for error shrinks further. Engineers call the worst-case outcome thermal runaway: a chain reaction in which rising temperature damages the battery's internal structure, which releases more heat, which damages it further, until the cell vents, smokes, or catches fire. It is rare, but it is a real, documented failure mode of lithium-ion batteries, and bedding provides both the insulation that encourages it and the fuel that makes it dangerous.

Age makes the risk worse, not better. An older battery holds less charge, is more likely to swell, and is more likely to run hot. A phone that has been dropped, repaired with unofficial parts, or charged for years with a frayed cable is a worse candidate for a night under a pillow than a brand-new device. And the timing makes everything worse still: these failures tend to develop while the household is asleep, when nobody is awake to smell smoke early or react quickly. That combination — a heat source, insulation, flammable material, and an unconscious household — is why firefighters treat this habit differently from, say, leaving a phone charging on a kitchen counter.

The scale of the underlying problem is larger than most people realize, though it needs to be stated carefully. The United States Consumer Product Safety Commission logged more than 25,000 reports of overheating or fire across more than 400 types of battery-powered consumer products between 2012 and 2017. That figure covers every kind of battery product, not phones under pillows specifically, so it does not prove that your phone will catch fire tonight. What it does prove is that battery overheating is not a freak event invented by viral posts. It is a known, counted phenomenon, and phones are among the most common battery-powered devices in the home. For a closer look at how charging itself affects a phone, see our earlier investigation, <a href="/blog/is-it-safe-to-use-your-phone-while-charging-fact-check/">Is It Safe to Use Your Phone While Charging?</a>, which found that the real risks concentrate in counterfeit chargers, damaged batteries, and trapped heat — the same three factors that meet under a pillow.

## What the phone makers themselves say

One of the strongest pieces of evidence for this warning is that it does not come only from firefighters. It comes from the companies that make the phones. Apple's safety guidance recommends charging devices on a firm, uncovered surface, keeping all charging components well ventilated, and avoiding placing a phone under bedding, trapping a cable beneath your body, or sleeping on top of a charging connector. The company warns that damaged cables or adapters, and charging in the presence of moisture, can result in fire, electric shock, injury, or damage to the device and surrounding property. It also advises users to stop using an iPhone or battery suspected of being damaged, because it could overheat or cause harm, and it notes that charging in ambient temperatures above 35 degrees Celsius can permanently reduce battery lifespan.

Samsung's guidance, as quoted above, carries the same message in different words: do not cover a device with materials that significantly restrict airflow, because of the possible risk of fire or explosion. When the manufacturer of the device and the fire department of your town agree, the argument that a warning is just internet hysteria becomes very hard to sustain.

It is worth being precise about what the makers are warning against, though, because precision is where the viral version of this story usually goes wrong. Apple is not telling people never to charge a phone overnight. Overnight charging on a nightstand, with a certified charger and a phone in open air, is a routine the makers design for — which is why modern phones include battery-management systems that stop charging at full capacity and software that paces the charge through the night. Our earlier look at the overnight question, <a href="/blog/overnight-phone-charging-battery-fact-check/">Does Charging Your Phone Overnight Really Destroy the Battery? We Investigated</a>, reached the same conclusion: the habit itself is not the problem; the surface and the equipment around the phone are. The warning is about charging under a pillow, not about charging while you sleep.

## What about the phone that is not charging?

This is where the claim splits in two, and where honest fact-checking has to part company with the scariest version of the story. Many of the posts that circulate this warning do not stop at fire. They imply, or state outright, that sleeping with a phone under your pillow exposes you to dangerous radiation all night, and that this radiation can cause cancer or other illness. That part is not supported by the evidence.

Phones emit radiofrequency energy, which is a form of non-ionizing radiation — the same broad category as radio waves, and fundamentally different from the ionizing radiation of X-rays, which carries enough energy to break chemical bonds and damage DNA. The United States Food and Drug Administration reviewed published literature from 2008 to 2018, covering roughly 125 animal studies and around 70 epidemiological studies, and concluded that there was insufficient evidence to support a causal association between radiofrequency exposure from cell phones and tumor development. Its broader summary, echoed by the Federal Communications Commission, is that to date there is no consistent or credible scientific evidence of health problems caused by exposure to radiofrequency energy from cell phones. Some individual studies have reported associations for heavy users, but reviewers have repeatedly found no clear, consistent pattern, and the studies involved have been subject to flaws including reliance on self-reported phone use.

That does not mean the science is finished or that every question has been answered; regulators themselves say longer-term studies remain valuable. But it does mean the radiation argument cannot carry the weight the viral warning puts on it. If you sleep with an unplugged phone under your pillow, the documented risks are different in kind: discomfort, a phone that overheats if it is working hard or faulty, and the sleep disruption discussed below — not a proven cancer risk. Anyone who tells you the pillow warning is really about radiation is attaching a real fire-safety message to a health claim the evidence does not support.

There is one more distinction worth making, because it explains why two sensible people can look at the same warning and disagree about it. A phone that is not charging, not covered, and in good condition is very unlikely to catch fire. A phone that is charging, covered by a pillow, and fitted with a damaged or counterfeit battery is in the worst plausible position short of an actual defect. Most real nights fall somewhere between those extremes, and the risk rises with each bad factor added: charging plus covering plus old equipment plus a phone running hot. The warning is best understood as advice about stacking the odds, not as a prediction that a specific phone will ignite.

## The sleep cost nobody photographs

Fire is the dramatic risk, but it is not the only cost of taking a phone to bed — and it is not the most common one. A phone under a pillow is a phone within arm's reach all night, and that proximity shapes sleep in ways that researchers have measured repeatedly.

The best-understood mechanism is light. Phones, tablets, and other LED-screen devices emit short-wavelength blue light, which activates photoreceptors in the eye called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells. Those cells signal the brain that it is daytime, which suppresses melatonin, the hormone that helps signal that it is time to sleep. A study from researchers at the University of Houston College of Optometry found that participants who wore short-wavelength-blocking glasses for three hours before bedtime for two weeks, while otherwise keeping their normal digital routine, saw their nighttime melatonin levels rise by about 58 percent, and reported falling asleep faster, sleeping better, and gaining about 24 minutes of sleep a night. The study was small — 22 participants — but it sits within a larger body of work pointing the same way.

A well-known experiment published in 2015 found that adults who read on a light-emitting tablet for four hours before bed over several evenings took longer to fall asleep, produced less melatonin, had a later body clock, got less REM sleep, and felt less alert the next morning than when they read a printed book. A more recent study of 33 male adolescents and 35 male young adults, published in 2024, found that reading on a smartphone without a blue-light filter for 90 minutes before bed significantly reduced melatonin levels in both groups, with adults still showing reduced melatonin at bedtime. And a large 2025 study of more than 45,000 students in Norway found that each extra hour of screen use after getting into bed was associated with about 24 minutes less sleep and higher odds of insomnia symptoms — with the amount of time, rather than the type of activity, mattering most.

None of this means a single glance at a phone destroys a night's sleep. Later analysis of the blue-light research has stressed that the biggest melatonin effects were measured under extreme laboratory conditions — four hours of bright tablet reading in a dark room — and that typical evening phone use with night mode and moderate brightness produces much smaller shifts for most people. Caffeine, stress, bedroom temperature, and the regularity of your sleep schedule all matter too. But the direction of the evidence is consistent, and it points away from the bed as a place for a phone. A device under your pillow is not just a fire question; it is an invitation to check it at 2 a.m., to be woken by a notification, and to push sleep later by one more scroll. Sleep researchers' advice is boring and consistent: charge the phone somewhere else, or at least out of reach, and let the bedroom belong to sleep.

## Why children and teenagers get a special warning

Fire departments have aimed this warning especially at parents, and for good reason. Teenagers are the most likely to sleep with a phone in the bed: the phone is their alarm, their social life, and their entertainment, and the bed is where all three happen. The Newton warning was addressed to parents precisely because children were the group most often charging devices on beds or under pillows.

The risks also compound for younger users. A teenager is more likely to be using a hand-me-down phone with an older battery, a borrowed charger of unknown origin, or a device with a cracked screen and a swollen cell that nobody has inspected. They are less likely to notice the early warning signs — a charger that runs unusually hot, a case that no longer sits flat because the battery beneath it has swollen, a faint chemical smell — and more likely to sleep through the first signs of trouble. Parents checking where devices charge at night is therefore not overprotective fussing; it is the single cheapest fire-prevention check in most homes. Move the charger to a hard surface, make sure the cable and adapter are certified and undamaged, and keep the phone out of the bed itself. That one change removes the insulation, the fuel, and the sleeping victim from the same square foot of mattress.

It is also worth separating this warning from its neighbours, because phone-safety warnings have a habit of blurring together. The claim that a phone can blow up a gas station, for example, is a different story with a different answer — as we showed in <a href="/blog/cell-phone-gas-station-fire-myth-fact-check/">No, Your Cell Phone Won't Blow Up the Gas Station — Inside the 25-Year-Old Email Hoax That Invented a Fire Hazard</a>, no fire caused by a cell phone at a gas pump has ever been documented by the industry body that tracks such incidents, and the real culprit in pump fires is static electricity. The pillow warning is not that kind of myth. It has real incidents, real manufacturer warnings, and a clear physical mechanism. Conflating the two — dismissing the pillow warning because the gas-station warning was false, or believing the gas-station warning because the pillow warning is true — is exactly how misinformation survives: by borrowing credibility from its neighbours.

## The Verdict

**Mostly True.** Sleeping with your phone under your pillow is dangerous when the phone is charging under bedding, and that is the scenario the viral warning is really about. Fire departments in the United States and the United Kingdom have warned against it repeatedly. Phone makers warn against it in their own safety manuals. A Texas teenager's phone melted under her pillow and scorched her bedding in 2014, in a case linked to a third-party battery and trapped heat. The physics is straightforward: charging generates heat, bedding traps it, and a damaged or aging battery under those conditions can enter thermal runaway while everyone in the house is asleep.

What is not true is the scarier, broader version of the claim. An unplugged phone under a pillow is not a proven cancer risk — the FDA's review of the literature found insufficient evidence for a causal link between cell-phone radiofrequency exposure and tumors — and a healthy modern phone on its own does not routinely burst into flames. Nor is overnight charging itself the enemy; charging on a hard, ventilated surface with a certified charger is what the makers design for. The danger is the combination: charging, covered, with compromised equipment, next to a sleeping person. Remove any one of those factors and the risk falls sharply. Keep the phone out of the bed and on the nightstand, and you keep the convenience of a charged phone and a bedside alarm without the hazard that made firefighters write the warning in the first place.

## A safer bedtime routine, in five moves

First, charge on a hard, flat surface — a nightstand, desk, or dresser — where air can circulate around the phone and the charger. Never on a mattress, sofa, or under a blanket or pillow, and never with the cable trapped beneath your body. Second, use the charger and cable made for your phone, or a certified equivalent, and replace anything frayed, cracked, bent, discoloured, or suspiciously cheap. Counterfeit and damaged charging gear is the multiplier that turns an unlikely failure into a plausible one. Third, watch for warning signs and act on them: a phone or charger that feels unusually hot, a battery that has swollen so the case no longer sits flat, a charger that buzzes, smells, or shows scorch marks. Stop using that device or charger and have it checked; do not just move it to a cooler spot and hope.

Fourth, keep the phone out of the bed even when it is not charging. If you need it as an alarm, the nightstand is close enough to hear and far enough to protect your sleep. If notifications wake you, switch on do-not-disturb or leave the phone face down across the room. And fifth, give yourself a screen curfew: power down an hour or two before bed, or at least switch on night mode and lower the brightness, so the light and the content are not the last things your brain processes before sleep. None of these steps costs anything, none of them requires giving up the phone, and together they remove every ingredient the fire warning is actually about.

The pillow is not cursed, and the phone is not a bomb. But a bed is a terrible charging station: soft, insulating, flammable, and occupied by someone who cannot react. Firefighters did not issue this warning because they enjoy spoiling a convenient habit. They issued it because they had seen what happens when an ordinary device, an ordinary charger, and an ordinary pillow meet in the worst possible combination — and because the fix is so simple it barely counts as a sacrifice. Move the phone six inches, from under the pillow to the nightstand, and the warning stops applying to you. That is a rare thing in the world of viral health scares: a danger that is real, a solution that is trivial, and a night's sleep that is better for both reasons.
