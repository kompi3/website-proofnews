It's the kind of line that slips into conversation without anyone asking for proof. A colleague survives a layoff and someone quips, "lightning never strikes the same place twice." A writer uses it to mean that freak coincidences don't repeat. Even scientists have been heard to borrow the phrase in casual conversation. The saying feels old, folksy, and vaguely scientific — as if somewhere along the way, an actual observation about electricity got folded into the language.

But as a claim about the natural world, it deserves scrutiny, because it makes a concrete prediction: once a spot has been hit by lightning, it won't be hit again. That is testable. And when you test it — with tall buildings, with lightning-mapping satellites, and with the career of a man whose ranger hat still sits in a museum with a lightning burn across the crown — the prediction collapses completely.

The claim fails so thoroughly that meteorologists treat it as a teaching example of how a catchy metaphor can survive for a century despite zero physical evidence behind it. Here is what the evidence actually says.

## Where the Saying Comes From

The phrase "lightning never strikes the same place twice" entered English-language idiom as a metaphor, not as a meteorological finding. Reference works on phrase origins trace its figurative use to the 19th century, when it was already being deployed to mean that freak misfortunes — a bankruptcy, a scandal, a freak accident — don't repeat themselves for the same person. It was comforting, memorable, and rhymed just well enough to stick. It was not, at any point, the conclusion of a study.

That distinction matters, because metaphors behave differently than claims. A metaphor earns its keep by being evocative; a factual claim earns its keep by being true. The problem is that this particular metaphor sounds like physics. It borrows the authority of nature — as if lightning itself had confirmed the saying — and people hearing it in childhood often file it away as a fact about the world rather than a figure of speech. Safety educators have found themselves repeatedly correcting it, which tells you how deep the confusion runs: the National Weather Service's lightning guidance treats the repeated-strike reality as settled, and weather services around the world have had to publish myth-versus-fact pages addressing this exact saying.

## What Lightning Actually Does: The Physics

To understand why the saying is wrong, it helps to know how a lightning bolt actually forms — and it turns out the process itself almost argues against the myth from first principles.

A lightning strike begins inside a thundercloud, where collisions between ice crystals, hailstones, and water droplets separate electrical charge: lighter particles carry positive charge to the cloud top, heavier ones carry negative charge to the cloud base. As that charge separation builds, the electric field between the cloud and the ground intensifies, eventually reaching tens of millions of volts. At that point, the air itself — normally a good insulator — begins to break down.

The breakdown starts with what physicists call a **stepped leader**: an invisible channel of negatively charged air that descends from the cloud in discrete jumps of about 50 meters each, which is what gives lightning its jagged appearance. When that leader gets within roughly 100 meters of the ground, something remarkable happens: the ground reaches up to meet it. Tall objects — trees, buildings, antenna towers, even people — send up faint channels of positive charge called **streamers**. Whichever streamer makes contact with the descending leader first completes the circuit.

Here's the part most people never learn: the brilliant flash we call the lightning bolt is not the downward descent. It's the **return stroke** — a massive surge of current racing *upward* from the ground to the cloud at up to a third the speed of light, heating the channel air to around 30,000°C (about five times hotter than the surface of the sun), which is why the air explodes outward and we hear thunder. Our eyes simply can't distinguish the direction.

And critically, the stroke doesn't end there. The channel the first return stroke carved through the air remains ionized — a path of least resistance. In roughly half of all cloud-to-ground flashes, leftover charge follows that same channel back down in what scientists call **dart leaders**, producing additional return strokes down the identical path. A single bolt can flicker through multiple strokes along the same route in less than a second.

Lightning, in other words, doesn't just strike the same place twice — a *single flash* can strike the same channel multiple times within a fraction of a second. The physics doesn't merely permit repetition; it favors it. Ionized air conducts far better than undisturbed air, so electricity keeps taking the easiest route, the way water keeps running down the same gully it cut yesterday.

The other half of the physics explains *which* places get hit: lightning has no memory, no preferences, and no superstitions. It simply follows the path of least resistance. Tall, isolated, or highly conductive objects reduce the distance a stepped leader must travel and make the upward streamer connection easier. That is why the same tower, the same hilltop tree, the same stretch of lakeshore gets hit over and over. It isn't coincidence or bad luck. It's conductivity and geometry, and both are stable properties of a location. A tower that was the easiest path to ground last Tuesday is still the easiest path to ground this Thursday.

## The Empire State Building: 25 Strikes a Year

If the myth were true, the Empire State Building would have been struck once — and then left alone for the rest of its long life. Instead, the building is one of the most frequently struck structures on Earth.

According to the National Weather Service, the Empire State Building is hit by lightning an average of **25 times per year**. One account of its lightning history notes that during a single storm, the building was struck **eight times in 24 minutes**. A lightning-safety guide published by AAA, drawing on the same data, describes the building as having been "once used as a lightning laboratory because it is struck nearly 25 times per year and has been hit up to a dozen times during a single storm."

Those numbers aren't a fluke of one bad year. The building stands 1,454 feet tall including its antenna, looms over its surroundings, and is packed with conductive metal — precisely the combination of height, isolation, and conductivity that the physics predicts will attract repeated strikes. Engineers long ago stopped fighting this tendency and started exploiting it: the building's lightning protection system gives the strikes a safe conductive path to the ground, the same principle as the lightning rod Benjamin Franklin proposed in the 18th century.

The Empire State Building isn't even the champion. The CN Tower in Toronto, at 1,815 feet, is reported to be struck more than 70 times per year. And these are just the famous examples. Broadcast towers, radio masts, and tall trees in open fields routinely absorb repeated strikes season after season — sometimes dozens over a lifetime, the scars growing a little deeper each time.

## Roy Sullivan: Struck Seven Times

Buildings are one thing; they can't choose where to stand. What about people? The myth has a particular sting when applied to individuals — the idea that surviving a strike somehow buys you immunity. The life of Roy Sullivan dismantles that idea with almost cruel thoroughness.

Roy Cleveland Sullivan was a park ranger at Shenandoah National Park in Virginia from 1936 onward. Between 1942 and 1977, Sullivan was struck by lightning on **seven separate occasions** — and survived every one of them. Guinness World Records recognizes him as the person struck by lightning more times than any other human, a record that carries the nickname he earned the hard way: the "Human Lightning Conductor," or "Spark Ranger."

The documented tally, drawn from Sullivan's own detailed accounts and contemporary reporting, reads like a summary of bad-luck statistics:

- **April 1942**: hiding in a fire lookout tower during a storm — a tower with no lightning rod — Sullivan fled when the building caught fire from a strike and was hit in the right leg, losing the nail on his big toe.
- **July 1969**: struck while driving his truck on a mountain road; knocked unconscious, eyebrows burned off, wristwatch destroyed.
- **July 1970**: struck in his own front yard, his left shoulder seared.
- **April 1972**: hit inside a ranger station, his hair set on fire — after which he began carrying a pitcher of water with him at all times.
- **August 1973**: a bolt hit him on the head, threw him out of his car, and set his hair on fire again; his legs were also seared.
- **June 1976**: struck in a campground, injuring his ankle. Contemporary reports said he saw the cloud, thought it was following him, tried to run, and was struck anyway.
- **June 25, 1977**: struck while fishing from a boat; hospitalized with burns on his chest and stomach.

The cruel detail is that the strikes weren't distributed evenly across his 71-year life — five of them came within an eight-year span. After the 1972 strike set his hair ablaze, Sullivan developed such a reputation that fellow rangers would reportedly move away from him when storm clouds gathered; one colleague, walking with the Chief Ranger when lightning struck at a distance, simply said, "I'll see you later," and left. Sullivan found the avoidance saddening. He died in 1983 at age 71, not from lightning but from a self-inflicted gunshot — a personal tragedy that has nothing to do with the physics, but which punctuates a life spent as a living counterargument to the saying.

Could all seven strikes have been mere coincidence? Roughly speaking, no — but the explanation isn't supernatural either. Sullivan worked outdoors in a national park in a state that sees 35 to 45 thunderstorm days a year, on mountain ridges and in open areas, for four decades. The Weather Network, reviewing his story, noted that the naïve odds of being struck seven times look astronomically small — over an 80-year lifetime the chance of a single strike is around 1 in 10,000 — but that "the mathematical odds don't really apply to Sullivan's situation because he was at a higher risk" due to his work and location. That is precisely the point the myth gets wrong: lightning risk isn't random in the way a lottery is random. It's conditional. Once you're the tall object on the exposed ridge, the conditions that struck you last time are still there, waiting for the next storm.

Two of Sullivan's ranger Stetsons, their crowns scarred by lightning, are on display in Guinness World Exhibit Halls — one in New York City, one in South Carolina. They're artifacts of a record nobody wants to break, and physical proof that lightning not only strikes the same place twice, but can strike the same person seven times.

## The Place Where Lightning Strikes Every Night

If Sullivan's story proves that people can be repeat targets, Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela proves that entire landscapes can be struck on an almost nightly schedule.

At the mouth of the Catatumbo River, where it flows into Lake Maracaibo in northwestern Venezuela, an atmospheric phenomenon known as the **Catatumbo Lightning** — the "everlasting storm" — produces one of the most persistent electrical displays on Earth. The numbers, confirmed by NASA satellite observations and summarized in a 2014 Guinness World Records entry, are staggering:

- The hotspot averages roughly **233 to 250 lightning flashes per square kilometer per year**, the highest concentration on the planet.
- That adds up to around **1.2 to 1.6 million lightning bolts annually** over the region.
- Storms rage on roughly **140 to 160 nights a year** (some counts say up to 300), often lasting **nine to ten hours per night**.
- At peak intensity, the area sees up to **280 flashes per hour** — about 28 strikes every minute.

The mechanism is a perfect storm of geography: the lake basin is surrounded on three sides by mountains — the Andes, the Perijá range, and the Mérida cordillera — while the open fourth side funnels warm, moist Caribbean air inland. As the sun sets, low-level winds accelerate, collide with the mountains, and force humid air upward into towering storm clouds that discharge electricity almost continuously through the night. The displays are so bright and so reliable that Caribbean sailors historically used them as a natural lighthouse, visible from over 400 kilometers away.

Here, "the same place" isn't struck twice — it's struck hundreds of thousands of times a year, in the same swampy delta, night after night, year after year. As a literal refutation of the saying, it is about as decisive as a natural phenomenon can be. Central Africa has its own runners-up: the Kabare region of the Democratic Republic of Congo ranks among the most lightning-dense spots on Earth, and Florida's Tampa-to-Titusville corridor holds the U.S. title of "Lightning Capital" — the same corridor where, notably, people still die each year from strikes that landed exactly where lightning has landed before.

## Why the Myth Persists Anyway

Given that the evidence is so one-sided, it's worth asking why the saying survives. The answer is part psychology, part language.

First, the saying does real emotional work. It reassures. After something terrible and random happens, people want to believe the universe has some kind of no-repeat policy. The phrase delivers that comfort in seven words. Research into human risk perception consistently shows that people are bad at reasoning about independent probabilities — we treat a rare event as "used up" once it happens, a fallacy closely related to the gambler's fallacy. The lightning saying is essentially that fallacy wearing a folksy disguise.

Second, there's a kernel of misleading intuition: a tree that has just been blasted by lightning *looks* like it would be protected — it's charred, split, maybe dead. But a damaged tree is, if anything, still a tall object in the same exposed location, and a damaged conductor is still a conductor. Some lightning-safety educators note that people shelter under trees that have been struck before, reasoning that the spot is now "safe" — a belief that has gotten people killed. The myth isn't merely wrong; it's actively dangerous.

Third, the metaphor keeps the phrase alive independent of the physics. Journalists, politicians, and novelists reach for "lightning doesn't strike twice" to describe everything from lottery wins to scandals, and each figurative use reinforces the literal-sounding claim. When a metaphor is vivid enough, people stop noticing it's a metaphor.

## What Lightning Safety Actually Requires

The practical consequence of the myth matters more than the poetry. Lightning-safety guidance from weather agencies assumes — correctly — that the spot you just saw struck is *more* likely, not less likely, to be struck again, because the features that attracted the bolt haven't changed. The standing advice:

- **Don't shelter under a tall isolated tree** — it's a prime streamer source, struck or not.
- **Get inside a substantial building or hard-topped vehicle** at the first sign of a storm, and stay there for 30 minutes after the last thunder.
- **Lightning rods don't attract lightning so much as they intercept it**, giving a repeat-prone tall structure a safe path to ground — which is exactly why the Empire State Building can be hit 25 times a year without incident.
- **If you're outdoors with nowhere to go**, make yourself small and minimize contact with the ground — but understand this is a last resort, not a strategy.

The old folk rule had it exactly backward: the safest assumption is that a place that drew lightning yesterday will draw it again today.

## The Verdict

**The claim that "lightning never strikes the same place twice" is False.**

The physics says otherwise: lightning follows the path of least resistance, and about half of all cloud-to-ground flashes send multiple return strokes down the *same ionized channel* within a single bolt. The records say otherwise: the Empire State Building absorbs roughly 25 strikes a year — eight in 24 minutes during one documented storm — and the CN Tower takes even more. The satellites say otherwise: Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela endures up to 1.6 million bolts a year in the same river delta, earning a Guinness World Record for the highest lightning concentration on Earth. And the most stubborn witness of all, park ranger Roy Sullivan, was struck seven times between 1942 and 1977 and lived to have his scorched ranger hats put in a museum.

The saying survives as a metaphor, and metaphors don't need to be true to be useful. But as a statement about the world, it has never been true, and believing it — especially during a storm — can be dangerous. Lightning doesn't remember where it struck. It just finds the easiest way down, and it will take that way as many times as the sky asks it to.
