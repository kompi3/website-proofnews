It starts, as so many modern myths do, with a video that looks too good to doubt. A hand pushes two metal rods into a raw potato, clips on a couple of wires, plugs in a phone cable — and the screen lights up. The caption promises free energy from your kitchen cupboard, no wall socket required, no power bank, no electricity bill. Millions of people have watched some version of this trick, and a fair number have walked away believing that a bag of potatoes could keep their phone alive in an emergency.

It is a wonderful story. It is also, as a practical claim, false. The potato battery is real science — it genuinely produces electricity, and it has been a classroom staple for decades. But the gap between "produces electricity" and "charges a phone" is enormous, and it is measured in volts, amps, and, quite literally, tons of potatoes. This is the story of a real experiment that got inflated into a false promise, and of the one genuinely remarkable potato discovery that almost nobody in the viral videos ever mentions.

## The viral claim

The claim comes in several flavours, and the differences matter. The mildest version says a potato can generate electricity, which is true. A stronger version says a potato can charge a phone, which is where things fall apart. The strongest version — the one that racks up the views — shows a phone screen switching on while connected to a potato, implying that the humble spud is a working off-grid charger.

Search results for the trick are full of confident how-to guides. Some are honest, labelling the exercise a science demonstration. Others are less careful, with titles that promise phone charging from "simple tips" and materials lists that stop at two or three potatoes, a couple of nails, and a USB cable. The implication is that anyone with a pantry and a few coins can bypass the power grid. Nobody in these videos shows the phone's battery percentage climbing, because that is the part that does not happen.

Part of the confusion is understandable. The potato battery is not a hoax. It is a genuine electrochemical cell, demonstrated in schools since at least the 1980s, and it really does produce a measurable voltage. When something is half true, the false half rides along for free. To separate them, you have to look at what a potato battery actually is — and what a phone actually demands.

## How a potato battery actually works

Here is the first surprise: the potato does not make electricity. The potato is the middleman. Inside every potato is a watery mix of acids — including phosphoric acid — and dissolved salts. That juice acts as an electrolyte, a liquid that lets charged particles move around. When you push two different metals into the potato, typically a zinc-coated galvanised nail and a piece of copper wire or a copper coin, the chemistry starts.

Zinc is the more reactive metal, so it gives up electrons — it oxidises. Those electrons travel through the external wire toward the copper, which acts as the positive electrode. That flow of electrons is an electric current. The potato's job is simply to complete the circuit, carrying ions between the two metals the way the acid paste inside a shop-bought battery does. Scientists call the potato a salt bridge. It is, in effect, nature's version of battery acid, not nature's version of a power station.

A single potato cell built this way produces roughly 0.5 to 1.1 volts, with most classroom measurements landing around 0.8 volts. That is in the same neighbourhood as a single AA battery's 1.5 volts, which is why the experiment feels so promising. But voltage is only half the story. The other half is current — how many electrons actually flow — and this is where the potato falls off a cliff.

A potato cell typically delivers current in the range of a few hundred microamps: roughly 0.1 to 0.5 milliamps. For comparison, a single small LED wants around 20 milliamps, dozens of times more. That is why a potato battery can, at best, make an LED glow dimly or run a tiny digital clock, and why guides recommend wiring two or three cells in series for an LED and four to six for a clock. The potato's internal resistance is high — measurements put it in the range of a few thousand ohms — so the moment you ask it to do real work, the voltage sags and the current collapses.

The potato is not even the best vegetable for the job. Lemons, with their higher acidity, typically produce a little more voltage and current than potatoes, and oranges and apples can do the same trick. Potatoes became the classroom favourite because they are cheap, firm enough to hold the electrodes, and available almost everywhere. They were chosen for convenience, not for power.

## The math: what a phone actually needs

Now put the two sides of the equation together. A phone charges over USB, which starts at 5 volts. Modern phones using USB Power Delivery ask for considerably more, with many models requiring at least 15 watts before their charging circuits even engage properly. Even at the most modest end, a basic 5-volt, 1-amp charger delivers 5 watts of power.

A single potato cell delivers about 0.8 volts and perhaps 0.3 milliamps. Multiply those together and you get about 0.00024 watts — roughly a quarter of a milliwatt. To reach 5 watts at that rate, you would need around 20,000 potato cells. And that is before accounting for losses: wiring thousands of cells together, keeping them all moist and fresh, and converting their ragged output into the clean, stable 5 volts a phone expects. Real circuits lose energy at every step, so the honest number is higher still.

Voltage alone is easier to fix than current. Wiring cells in series adds their voltages, so seven potatoes in series gets you past 5 volts. That is the part the videos show, sometimes with a multimeter reading to prove it. What they do not show is the current, because seven potatoes in series still deliver only a fraction of a milliamp — and current is what actually fills a battery. Wiring cells in parallel adds current, but you would need thousands of parallel strings to reach even one amp. A phone's charging circuit, presented with a few microamps, simply does nothing. It does not charge slowly. It does not register at all.

Think of it like water pressure versus water flow. Voltage is the pressure in the pipe; current is how much water actually comes out. A potato battery is a pipe with decent pressure and a pinhole opening. A phone needs a fire hose. No arrangement of pinholes that fits on a kitchen table will fill the tank.

## The 500-pound potato battery

If you think the maths above is just theory, someone has already run the experiment at absurd scale. An artist and engineer in Portland, Oregon, built a battery from 500 pounds of potatoes — a whole truckload, wired into series groups for voltage and parallel groups for current, and installed in the back of a van with a small sound system. The result is one of the most honest data points in this whole story.

Five hundred pounds of potatoes produced about 5 volts and 4 milliamps. That is 0.02 watts: enough to run a small, low-power sound system at modest volume, and about one two-hundred-and-fiftieth of what a basic phone charger delivers. Scale that up to the 5 watts a phone wants and you need roughly 250 times as much potato — around 125,000 pounds, or close to 57 metric tonnes, of potatoes, plus thousands of copper and zinc electrodes, plus the wiring, plus a warehouse to put it all in. And the whole contraption would start rotting within days, because a potato battery is a perishable chemical reaction, not a durable machine.

The Portland experiment is the perfect reality check because it removes every excuse. It was not a single potato, it was a quarter of a ton of them. It was not built by amateurs, it was carefully engineered with series and parallel wiring. And it still produced less than one percent of a phone charger's output. If 500 pounds cannot do it, the three potatoes in a viral video certainly cannot.

## The boiled-potato breakthrough the videos never mention

Here is the twist: buried underneath the false claim is a genuinely exciting piece of real science that the viral videos almost never talk about. Around 2010, researchers at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, led by agricultural science professor Haim Rabinowitch with student Alex Goldberg and Berkeley professor Boris Rubinsky, discovered that boiling a potato for eight minutes before using it increases its power output up to tenfold.

The reason is elegant. Boiling breaks down the potato's internal structure and lowers the resistance of the salt bridge between the electrodes, letting current flow far more freely. The team's design used small units made from a quarter-slice of boiled potato sandwiched between a copper cathode and a zinc anode, connected by wire. Their goal was not to charge phones at all: it was to provide LED lighting for rooms in areas without access to a power grid, with a single unit able to power an LED for as long as 40 days. Cost analyses found the treated potato battery could be five to fifty times cheaper than commercial D-cell batteries, and the light it produced was at least six times more economical than the kerosene lamps commonly used in the developing world.

That is a remarkable finding, and it is real. But notice what it does not say. Even a tenfold improvement on a quarter of a milliwatt is still a few milliwatts — enough for an LED, a universe away from a phone. The researchers themselves framed their work as a solution for low-power needs: light and, potentially, basic communication devices in off-grid regions. The boiled potato is a better battery, not a phone charger. The viral version of the story takes a real discovery about lighting a room and inflates it into a fantasy about charging a smartphone.

## Why the videos look so convincing

If the physics is this clear, why do the videos persuade so many people? There are a few tricks, some deliberate and some accidental.

First, a multimeter reading is easy to fake by accident. A meter measuring an unloaded potato battery shows its open-circuit voltage, which looks respectable — nearly a volt per potato, and several volts for a chain. But open-circuit voltage is measured when almost no current is flowing. The moment a real load is attached, the voltage collapses. A phone is a heavy load; a multimeter is not. Showing the meter and then cutting to a phone screen implies a connection that was never demonstrated.

Second, some videos show a phone screen lighting up when a cable is plugged in. A phone screen can wake for many reasons — a button press, a timer, a notification — and a brief flash of the display is not the same as charging. What the videos never show, because they cannot, is the battery percentage rising over time, or the phone sustaining itself on potato power for an hour. A charging icon that appears for a second and vanishes is not evidence; it is editing.

Third, the experiment itself is so satisfying that it short-circuits scepticism. You built something with your own hands, you saw a number on a meter, an LED lit up. The leap from "this makes electricity" to "this can charge anything" feels small, because both statements use the same word: electricity. But electricity is not one thing. A static shock from a doorknob is thousands of volts and utterly useless for charging a phone, because the current lasts a fraction of a second. The potato's problem is the same in slow motion: real voltage, negligible current, and a load that demands both.

Finally, there is a genuine emotional appeal. The idea of free, natural, off-grid energy taps into a desire for self-sufficiency, and it is especially attractive in emergencies — a blackout, a camping trip, a dead phone when you need it most. That is exactly when a false claim does the most harm: it sends people hunting for potatoes instead of conserving battery, finding a real charger, or carrying a power bank. ## What potatoes can actually power

None of this makes the potato battery worthless. It makes it honestly useful, in its proper lane. A few potato cells will light an LED, run a small digital clock, or register on a multimeter — and that is precisely why it survives in science classrooms. It is cheap, safe, and it makes an invisible principle visible: chemical energy becoming electrical energy, with your own hands on the electrodes. For a student meeting electrochemistry for the first time, that is worth more than any viral video.

The boiled-potato research points to a more serious lane. In regions without reliable electricity, a treated potato battery powering an LED for weeks at a fraction of the cost of disposable batteries is a meaningful improvement over a kerosene lamp — cleaner air, no fire risk, light for studying after dark. Potatoes are grown in around 130 countries, more than almost any other crop, and they keep well. The researchers' vision was never a phone charger; it was light, and light matters.

There is one practical warning worth repeating: never eat a potato after using it as a battery. The electrodes leach metal ions — zinc and copper compounds — into the flesh, and the potato is no longer food. The same goes for any fruit or vegetable used in the experiment. Science projects and dinner should stay in separate piles.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: False.** You cannot charge a phone with a potato — not with one potato, not with a bag of them, and not with the setup shown in the viral videos. A potato cell produces roughly 0.5 to 1.1 volts and only a fraction of a milliamp, while a phone needs a stable 5 volts and whole watts before its charging circuit even engages. The most ambitious real-world attempt, a 500-pound potato battery, produced just 4 milliamps at 5 volts — about one two-hundred-and-fiftieth of a basic phone charger's output. Reaching phone-charging power would take tens of tonnes of potatoes and thousands of electrodes, and the whole thing would rot within days.

What is true, and worth celebrating, is the science underneath. The potato battery is a real electrochemical cell, a superb teaching tool, and — in its boiled form — a genuinely clever low-cost way to power LED lighting where the grid does not reach. The claim only becomes false when it is inflated from "potatoes make a little electricity" into "potatoes can charge your phone." That gap, measured in milliamps, is the entire story.

So the next time a video promises to free your phone from the wall socket with a sack of spuds, enjoy the experiment for what it is. Wire up a potato, light an LED, and show a curious kid how a battery works. Then plug the phone into a real charger. The potato has earned its place in the science classroom and, perhaps one day, in an off-grid home's lamp. It has not earned a place in your charging cable — and no amount of clever editing will change the maths.
