It is one of the most repeated health warnings in the world: eat too much sugar and you will get diabetes. Parents say it to children reaching for a second slice of cake. Headlines imply it every time a new study links sweet drinks to disease. And for many people, the logic feels obvious — diabetes is a disease of high blood sugar, so surely eating sugar must cause it.

The logic feels obvious. It is also wrong — or at least, it is far too simple to be true. Diabetes is not a single disease with a single cause, and the relationship between the sugar in your food and the sugar in your blood is much more complicated than a straight line from the candy jar to the clinic. What the research actually shows is a split picture: sugar itself, eaten as part of an ordinary diet, does not directly cause diabetes, but one specific way of consuming it — sugary drinks, taken every day — is consistently tied to a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

That distinction matters. It matters because the myth scares people in the wrong direction, making them fear an occasional dessert while overlooking the daily soda habit that the evidence actually flags. And it matters because it hides the real drivers of the disease — weight, genetics, age, inactivity, and the slow build-up of insulin resistance — behind a single villain that is easier to blame than to understand.

So what does the evidence say, exactly? We looked at what the American Diabetes Association, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the largest meta-analyses of long-term studies actually report, and the answer is more interesting than the warning on the candy wrapper.

## The Claim

The claim comes in many forms. Sometimes it is a blunt warning — "sugar causes diabetes" — printed on a poster, repeated by a relative, or shared as a meme. Sometimes it is softer but just as sweeping: "if you keep eating sweets like that, you'll end up diabetic." And sometimes it arrives dressed up as science, in articles that report a new study linking sugar consumption to diabetes and leave readers with the impression that the case is closed.

The idea is so widespread that the American Diabetes Association has a dedicated page addressing diabetes facts and myths, and the question of whether sugar causes diabetes sits near the top of it. The association's answer is clear: eating sugar does not cause diabetes. But that answer rarely travels as far as the warning does, because a scary, simple claim spreads faster than a careful, qualified one.

Part of the confusion comes from the name of the disease itself. "Diabetes mellitus" comes from Greek and Latin words meaning, roughly, "sweet urine" — a reference to the fact that the urine of people with uncontrolled diabetes contains excess glucose. That historical naming accident has helped generations of people assume that sugar in the body must come directly from sugar in the diet. In reality, the glucose in a diabetic person's blood comes from all the carbohydrates they eat — bread, rice, pasta and potatoes included — and from glucose the liver itself produces, not only from the sugar bowl.

The claim also survives because it contains a grain of truth. People who consume a lot of sugar, especially in drinks, do tend to develop type 2 diabetes more often than people who consume little. That grain of truth is real. But a correlation is not a cause, and the full story of how that grain of truth works turns out to be very different from the simple version.

## What Diabetes Actually Is

Before judging whether sugar causes diabetes, it helps to be precise about what diabetes is — because there is no single disease here. There are several, with different causes, and sugar's role is different in each of them.

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition. The body's own immune system attacks and destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas, leaving the body unable to make insulin at all. Insulin is the hormone that lets glucose move from the bloodstream into cells, where it is used for energy. Without it, blood sugar climbs dangerously high. Type 1 accounts for roughly 5 to 10 percent of diagnosed diabetes cases, and it usually appears in childhood or young adulthood, though it can develop at any age. The CDC is blunt on the cause: diet and lifestyle habits do not cause type 1 diabetes. No amount of sugar, and no amount of avoiding sugar, can trigger or prevent an autoimmune attack on the pancreas. Genetics and environmental triggers that scientists are still working to identify are the suspects here, not dessert.

Type 2 diabetes is the common form, accounting for 90 to 95 percent of all diagnosed cases, according to the CDC. Here the pancreas still makes insulin, but the body's cells stop responding to it properly — a condition called insulin resistance — and over time the pancreas cannot make enough insulin to keep blood sugar in range. Type 2 develops gradually, often over years, and its causes are a tangle of factors: genes, age, excess body weight, physical inactivity, and diet all play a part. About 38 million Americans live with diabetes, and the overwhelming majority of them have type 2. Worldwide, the numbers are far larger, and they have been climbing for decades as diets and lifestyles have changed.

Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy in roughly 2 to 10 percent of pregnancies, according to the CDC, and usually resolves after birth — though it raises the later risk of type 2 diabetes for both mother and child.

There is also prediabetes, a state in which blood sugar is higher than normal but not yet high enough to be called diabetes. In the United States, nearly 98 million adults have prediabetes, most without knowing it. It is a warning stage, and it is also a reversible one: modest weight loss and regular physical activity can bring blood sugar back down, which is why the details of what raises risk matter so much.

Understanding these distinctions is the key to the whole question. When someone asks whether sugar causes diabetes, they usually mean type 2, the common, slow-developing form. And for type 2, the honest answer is that sugar is a contributor to risk through several indirect routes — but it is not, on its own, the cause.

## What the Evidence Says About Sugar Itself

The most surprising finding in the recent research is how weak the link is between total sugar intake and type 2 diabetes — when sugar is counted as a whole, across all foods. A large dose-response meta-analysis published in the journal Advances in Nutrition in 2025, led by researchers at Brigham Young University, pulled together 29 prospective cohort studies covering more than half a million people. It found that each additional daily serving of a sugar-sweetened beverage raised the risk of developing type 2 diabetes by 25 percent. Fruit juice raised it by 5 percent per daily serving. But total sugar intake — all the sugar in a person's diet, from every source — showed no increased risk at all. In fact, intakes of 20 grams a day of total sugar and of sucrose, ordinary table sugar, were associated with slightly lower risk, not higher.

That result surprised even the researchers. "This is the first study to draw clear dose-response relationships between different sugar sources and type 2 diabetes risk," said Karen Della Corte, the lead author and a professor of nutritional science at BYU, when the study was published. The pattern held even after the researchers adjusted for body mass index, total calorie intake, and other lifestyle factors.

A review published in early 2026 in Current Nutrition Reports reached a similar conclusion. Looking across epidemiological studies and meta-analyses, its authors found inconsistent associations between total sugar intake and type 2 diabetes risk. Sugar-sweetened beverages, however, were more consistently linked to increased risk, with estimates of a 13 to 30 percent rise per daily serving. Free sugars eaten in solid foods, at typical dietary levels, showed no clear harmful effect on diabetes risk.

Why would the form of sugar matter so much? The 2026 review points to a plausible mechanism. In solid food, sugar arrives packaged with fibre, water, protein or fat, which slow digestion and blunt the blood sugar response. The small intestine also plays a bigger role than most people realise: it metabolises much of the fructose in food before it ever reaches the liver, limiting its contribution to fat production in the liver — provided intake stays within normal dietary ranges. Liquid sugar, by contrast, is absorbed fast, delivers a large dose of calories with almost no feeling of fullness, and does not trigger the body to eat less later to compensate. The result is a pattern of excess energy intake that, day after day, adds up.

That is not the same as saying a spoonful of sugar in your tea is harmless in unlimited quantities, or that diet does not matter. It means the risk is not about sugar as a single ingredient. It is about how much sugar you take in, in what form, and what it does to your weight and your metabolism over years.

## The Exception That Matters: Sugary Drinks

If total sugar is a weak suspect, sugary drinks are the one part of the story where the evidence is strong, consistent, and hard to dismiss. This is the part of the claim that holds up — and it is important enough that health authorities treat it separately.

The evidence base is large. In 2010, researchers at Harvard pooled prospective studies covering more than 310,000 people and about 15,000 cases of type 2 diabetes. They found that people in the highest intake group — most often one to two sugary drinks a day — had a 26 percent higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes than people in the lowest intake group. They also had about a 20 percent higher risk of metabolic syndrome, the cluster of high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess belly fat and abnormal cholesterol that often precedes diabetes.

A 2015 meta-analysis in the BMJ, led by researchers at Cambridge, looked at the question again with 17 cohort studies. Each extra daily serving of a sugary drink was linked to an 18 percent higher incidence of type 2 diabetes. After adjusting for body weight and body fat, the link shrank but did not disappear: it remained at 13 percent per daily serving. That persistence matters, because it suggests the effect is not only about weight. Something about sugary drinks themselves — the rapid spike in blood sugar, the repeated demand on the pancreas, the way liquid sugar bypasses the body's satiety signals — appears to carry risk beyond the calories alone.

The 2025 BYU meta-analysis, the largest of its kind, put the figure at 25 percent higher risk per additional daily 12-ounce serving of sugar-sweetened beverages. To put that in perspective, the researchers noted that these are relative risks, not absolute ones. If an average person's baseline risk of developing type 2 diabetes is about 10 percent, four sodas a day could raise it to roughly 20 percent — a doubling of risk, but not a certainty.

These are observational studies, which means they show a strong and consistent association, not proof that a particular drink caused diabetes in a particular person. People who drink a lot of soda also tend to differ in other ways — they may exercise less, eat less fibre, or smoke more — and researchers can only partly adjust for those differences. But when study after study, in different countries, over different decades, points in the same direction, and when the size of the effect grows with the dose, the association becomes hard to wave away. That is why the American Diabetes Association recommends replacing sugar-sweetened beverages with water whenever possible, and why the CDC calls sugary drinks the number one source of added sugar in the American diet.

The scale of the exposure is striking. A single 12-ounce can of soda contains about 37 grams of sugar — nearly 10 teaspoons, according to US Department of Agriculture data cited by the CDC. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend that people aged two and older get less than 10 percent of their daily calories from added sugars. One can of soda can use up most of that allowance on its own. And unlike a dessert, which is usually eaten as a treat, sugary drinks are often consumed daily, sometimes several times a day, alongside meals that already contain plenty of calories.

It is also worth noting what counts as a sugary drink. The category is broader than soda: it includes fruit punch, sweetened fruit drinks, sports drinks, energy drinks, and sweet tea. The sugar in many of these is invisible in a way that the sugar in a cake is not. That invisibility is part of the risk, and it is one reason health communicators keep returning to drinks rather than desserts. ## How Sugar Raises Risk Without Causing the Disease

If sugar does not directly cause diabetes, how does it end up implicated at all? The answer runs through weight — and through the liver.

The chain works like this. A diet rich in added sugars, especially from drinks, makes it easy to take in more calories than the body burns. Liquid calories are the worst offenders, because they do not make you feel full the way solid food does. Over weeks and months, that surplus becomes stored fat. Excess fat, particularly fat around the abdomen and inside the liver, makes the body's cells less responsive to insulin. The pancreas compensates by producing more insulin, and for a while that works. But over years, the beta cells that make insulin can become exhausted, and blood sugar begins to climb. That is the road to type 2 diabetes — and sugar is one of the things that can set a person walking down it, even though it is not the road itself.

Dietitian Caroline Thomason, a registered dietitian and certified diabetes care and education specialist, put it plainly in an interview with EatingWell: "Type 2 diabetes can certainly be impacted by the amount of sugar intake in your diet. Sugar in your diet impacts blood sugar, so it makes sense that increased sugar intake is associated with diabetes risk. However, eating sugar alone isn't enough to cause diabetes."

The American Diabetes Association makes the same distinction in its guidance. While there is an association between increased intake of sugary beverages and type 2 diabetes, eating sugar does not cause diabetes. A diet rich in added sugars, saturated fats and excess energy intake is associated with an increased risk — but the key words are "rich in" and "excess". The risk comes from the pattern, not from a single food.

There is a second route that researchers have been investigating more closely in recent years: the liver. When fructose — the sweet half of table sugar and the main sugar in high-fructose corn syrup — arrives in large, rapid doses, as it does from a sugary drink, the liver converts much of it into fat. Over time, that can lead to nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, a condition in which fat builds up in the liver of people who drink little or no alcohol. Fatty liver is closely tied to insulin resistance, and it is increasingly seen as an early step on the path to type 2 diabetes. The 2026 review noted that the small intestine normally shields the liver from much of the fructose in solid food — but that shield is overwhelmed when fructose arrives quickly and in liquid form.

None of this makes sugar innocent. It makes sugar an accomplice. The distinction is not a technicality. It changes what a person should actually do: not ban sugar entirely, but break the patterns — daily sugary drinks, constant snacking on high-sugar foods, meals with little fibre — that push weight and insulin resistance upward over time.

That is also why crash diets and miracle fixes miss the point. Viral remedies that promise to "melt" belly fat or "detox" the body do nothing about the slow metabolic processes that actually matter. ## What Actually Raises Your Risk

If sugar alone is not the cause, what is? The list is longer and less dramatic than a single villain, which is exactly why it gets less attention.

Genetics comes first. Having a parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes substantially raises a person's risk, and researchers have identified dozens of gene variants that influence insulin production and insulin sensitivity. Genes are not destiny — lifestyle can still move risk up or down — but they set the starting point.

Weight is the largest modifiable factor. Excess body weight, and especially fat carried around the waist, is the strongest single predictor of type 2 diabetes that a person can change. The relationship is dose-dependent: the more excess weight, the higher the risk. The good news is that the relationship works in reverse, too. Research cited by the American Diabetes Association shows that even a modest weight loss of 3 to 7 percent can reduce the risk of developing diabetes — a finding that underpins the Diabetes Prevention Program, a landmark trial in which lifestyle changes cut the incidence of type 2 diabetes by 58 percent compared with a placebo group.

Age matters. The risk of type 2 diabetes rises after 45, partly because the body's ability to produce and use insulin declines with age, and partly because inactivity and weight gain accumulate over the decades. Ethnicity matters as well: people of African, Hispanic, South Asian, East Asian, Native American and Pacific Islander descent develop type 2 diabetes at higher rates than white populations, for reasons that involve both genetics and social and environmental factors.

Inactivity is its own risk factor, separate from weight. Muscles are the body's biggest consumers of glucose, and regular movement keeps them sensitive to insulin. A sedentary person can be at higher risk even at a normal weight. The ADA recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity such as brisk walking, cycling or dancing.

Other factors include a history of gestational diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol levels, and a history of heart disease or stroke. Smoking raises risk as well. And the timing of meals may play a small part: our check on <a href="/blog/does-eating-late-at-night-cause-weight-gain-fact-check/">Does Eating Late at Night Cause Weight Gain? What the Science Actually Says</a> found that late meals nudge hunger, calorie burning and blood sugar in directions that make weight control harder, even though the clock itself is not the main problem.

Notice what is missing from that list: any single food. No reputable health authority lists "eating sugar" among the primary causes of type 2 diabetes, because the evidence does not support it. Sugar is one thread in a web, not the spider.

## Why the Myth Refuses to Die

If the evidence is this clear, why does the claim survive? Part of the answer is human psychology. People prefer a single, simple cause for a frightening disease, and they prefer a cause they can control. "Don't eat sugar and you won't get diabetes" is a reassuringly simple rule — much simpler than "your risk depends on your genes, your weight, your activity level, your age, and the pattern of your whole diet over decades."

Part of the answer is that the grain of truth keeps the myth fed. Every new study linking sugary drinks to diabetes gets reported with a headline that drops the word "drinks" and keeps the word "sugar". Readers reasonably conclude that sugar itself is the problem, and the nuance — that the link is strongest for liquid sugar, that total sugar shows no consistent association — never reaches them.

And part of the answer is that the myth is, in a narrow sense, useful. People who believe sugar causes diabetes do tend to eat less sugar, which is not a bad outcome. But the myth also does real harm. It makes people with diabetes feel blamed for their condition, as though they brought it on themselves with a sweet tooth — when in fact many people who develop type 2 diabetes eat no more sugar than their neighbours, and many heavy sugar eaters never develop it. It makes people without diabetes complacent, believing that skipping dessert is enough protection while they sit all day and drink soda with lunch. And it directs attention away from the interventions that actually work: sustained, modest weight loss, regular movement, and cutting back on sugary drinks.

The myth also survives because it is almost true in the one context that matters most to public health. If everyone in a population stopped drinking sugary beverages every day, the incidence of type 2 diabetes would fall measurably — researchers have estimated that sugary drinks account for roughly one in twelve new cases of type 2 diabetes in the United States. That is a large, preventable share. But it is a statement about a habit, not about a molecule. Sugar in a drink is dangerous in the way that a daily habit is dangerous: through repetition, dose, and the company it keeps.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Sugar does not cause diabetes — not type 1, which is an autoimmune disease untouched by diet, and not type 2, where the evidence shows no consistent link between total sugar intake and risk. What the evidence does show, clearly and repeatedly, is that regularly drinking sugar-sweetened beverages raises the risk of type 2 diabetes by roughly a fifth to a quarter for each daily serving, and that a diet high in added sugars contributes to weight gain, liver fat and insulin resistance — the real engines of the disease.

The claim is misleading rather than false because it points at a real pattern and then mislabels it. The people who eat the most sugar do develop diabetes more often. But the reason is not the sugar molecule itself. It is the weight, the liquid calories, the daily soda habit, and the metabolic strain that comes with them — combined with genes and age that no diet can change. Blaming sugar alone is like blaming the match for a house fire while ignoring the gas leak, the dry timber and the broken smoke alarm.

## What to Do Instead

The evidence points to a short list of actions that genuinely move the needle on diabetes risk — and none of them requires swearing off sugar forever.

First, cut the drinks. This is the single most effective dietary change the research supports. Replace soda, sweet tea, fruit punch, sports drinks and energy drinks with water, sparkling water, or unsweetened tea. The American Diabetes Association recommends exactly this, and the dose-response data suggest the benefit grows with every serving dropped.

Second, watch the pattern, not the ingredient. A diet built mostly on whole foods — vegetables, whole grains, beans, nuts, lean protein — leaves little room for the excess calories that drive insulin resistance, whatever the sugar content of an occasional dessert. The World Health Organization recommends keeping free sugars below 10 percent of total energy intake, a target that is easy to meet when drinks are out of the picture.

Third, move. At least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity — brisk walking counts — keeps muscles sensitive to insulin and helps hold weight steady. It is one of the few interventions with evidence as strong as the evidence against sugary drinks.

Fourth, know your numbers. If you are over 45, carry excess weight, or have a family history of diabetes, ask for a blood sugar test. Prediabetes usually has no symptoms, and catching it early is the difference between a warning and a diagnosis. Losing even 3 to 7 percent of body weight can cut the risk of progressing to diabetes substantially.

And finally, drop the guilt — yours and other people's. Diabetes is not a punishment for enjoying sweet food. It is a complex, largely preventable condition with many causes, and understanding it properly is the first step toward preventing it. The sugar bowl was never the villain of this story. The daily soda was, and it still is — and that is a much easier enemy to beat.

## The Bottom Line

The next time someone warns you that sugar causes diabetes, you can answer with confidence: no, it does not — but the sugary drink in their hand, finished every day, is a different matter entirely. The science has moved past the simple warning. It is time the warning caught up.
