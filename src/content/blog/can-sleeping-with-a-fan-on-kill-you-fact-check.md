It is one of the most persistent bedroom fears in the world, and it comes with its own hardware. In South Korea, electric fans have for decades been sold with built-in timers that switch the machine off after a set number of hours. The timer is not there as a convenience feature. It is there because millions of people grew up believing that falling asleep in a closed room with a fan running could kill you before morning.

The belief has a name: fan death. It says that a fan left running all night in a sealed room can suffocate the sleeper, drain the oxygen from the air, chill the body into hypothermia, or cook it into heatstroke. Versions of the story have circulated for a century, and they still travel today in social media posts, expatriate anecdotes, and worried questions from travellers who notice the timer on a hotel-room fan for the first time. So the question is worth asking plainly, and answering with physics, medicine, and the historical record rather than with ridicule. A belief held by millions of people deserves a real investigation, not a joke.

The short answer is reassuring. There is no credible evidence that an ordinary electric fan can kill a healthy person sleeping in a closed room, and the mechanisms proposed for fan death do not survive contact with how fans, rooms, and human bodies actually work. That does not mean a fan is entirely harmless overnight. A fan blowing on your face all night can dry out your nose and throat, stir up dust, worsen allergies, and leave you stiff in the morning. In a severe heatwave, a fan can also give a false sense of safety while your core temperature keeps climbing. Those are real effects, and they matter. But they are a long way from the claim at the centre of this article, which is that the fan itself can kill you in your sleep. On that claim, the evidence points one way.

## Where the belief comes from

Fears about electric fans in Korea date back to the technology's introduction, with stories warning of nausea, asphyxiation, and even facial paralysis from the new machines circulating in the 1920s and 1930s. A 1927 article reportedly warned of the "strange harm" that could come from electric fans, which means the belief predates the modern era of air conditioning, social media, and viral health panics by many decades. This is not a TikTok invention. It is an old folk belief that survived into the age of the internet.

One popular conspiracy theory holds that the South Korean government created or encouraged the myth during the 1970s energy crisis to persuade households to use less electricity. The theory is neat, and it explains why a government-funded consumer body once treated fan asphyxiation as a serious summer hazard. But the chronology works against it: warnings about fans appear decades before the energy crisis, so the crisis cannot be the origin of a belief that already existed. A later explanation can amplify an old fear without having invented it, and that seems to be what happened here. Official warnings, news reports of alleged cases, and the timers built into Korean fans all reinforced one another, turning a folk worry into something that looked like settled public-health advice.

Another proposed origin is more domestic and more plausible as a contributing factor. Traditional Korean homes were heated in ways that could fill a room with smoke or combustion gases if ventilation failed, and a fan that changed the airflow in such a room could plausibly have made a bad situation worse. That is a long way from proving that a fan alone can kill a sleeper in a modern bedroom, but it helps explain why the idea felt intuitive to people whose grandparents lived with open flames and poor ventilation. Folk beliefs often start with a genuine hazard, then attach themselves to the wrong object as homes and technology change.

What kept the belief alive was not just folklore. It was repetition in print. News outlets in South Korea reported alleged fan deaths from time to time, sometimes with few details about the victim's health, the room, or any other cause. A 1997 article in the Korea Herald reportedly claimed at least ten such deaths, and the Korea Consumer Protection Board, a government-funded body, reported twenty cases of asphyxiation attributed to fans and air conditioners between 2003 and 2005, listing fan asphyxiation among the leading causes of summer accidents. Those reports matter, because they gave the belief an official-looking paper trail. But a report that a person died in a room where a fan was running is not the same as proof that the fan caused the death, and that distinction is where the whole story falls apart.

## The four ways a fan is supposed to kill you

The fan death story is unusual because it does not settle on a single mechanism. Depending on who tells it, the fan kills by suffocation, by oxygen theft, by chilling, or by heating. Each version deserves its own test, because they cannot all be true at once, and most of them contradict each other.

The first and most common version says the fan's rotating blades create a vacuum or a vortex in front of the machine, chopping up the air and starving the sleeper's lungs of oxygen. This sounds dramatic, but it misunderstands what a fan does. A household fan does not remove air from a room. It moves air that is already there from one place to another, creating a breeze. It does not seal the room, it does not pump air out of the building, and it cannot create a vacuum in an ordinary bedroom. A vacuum strong enough to prevent breathing would require industrial equipment and a sealed chamber, not a plastic-bladed fan on a nightstand. The breeze from a fan is, by design, a gentle movement of air across the skin. It is not a force capable of overpowering the diaphragm and the muscles that draw breath hundreds of times a night.

The second version says the fan uses up the room's oxygen or fills the room with carbon dioxide. Again, the basic physics points the other way. An electric fan is powered by electricity, not by burning fuel, so its motor does not consume oxygen the way a flame does. The oxygen in a closed room does slowly fall and carbon dioxide does slowly rise while a person sleeps there, because breathing itself does that. But that happens whether the fan is on or off, and a running fan does not meaningfully worsen it. If anything, a fan mixes the air in the room, preventing pockets of exhaled air from lingering around a sleeper's face. That mixing effect is not a minor detail. It is the reason a later study, discussed below, found that fan use was associated with a lower risk of sudden infant death, on the theory that moving air disperses exhaled carbon dioxide rather than concentrating it.

The third version blames hypothermia: hours of circulating air gradually lower the sleeper's body temperature until it reaches a fatal level. A fan does cool the skin, because moving air speeds up the evaporation of sweat and carries heat away from the body. But a fan does not cool the room itself, and it cannot drive a healthy adult's core temperature down to a dangerous level in an ordinary bedroom. For hypothermia to become life-threatening, the room would already have to be very cold, and the sleeper would wake up shivering and uncomfortable long before reaching danger. The human body is not a passive object that cools silently to zero. It shivers, it wakes, it pulls up blankets, and it complains. A fan cannot override those defences in a normal room.

The fourth version is the mirror image of the third, and it is the one that reveals the story's internal contradiction. Some versions of fan death say the fan kills by hyperthermia: on a hot night, the fan circulates hot air and raises the sleeper's body temperature until heatstroke sets in. This version contains a grain of truth, but it misplaces the blame. Extreme heat itself can indeed kill, and a fan cannot save someone from a severe heatwave. But the killer in that scenario is the heat, not the fan. Blaming the fan for a heatwave death is like blaming a thermometer for a fever. The fan failed to prevent the death; it did not cause it. And as the heat guidance below shows, health agencies warn about fans in extreme heat precisely because fans stop helping, not because fans become weapons.

## What the autopsies and the experiments actually found

The strongest evidence against fan death comes from the very cases that were supposed to prove it. Dr. Lee Yoon-song, a professor at Seoul National University's medical school, performed autopsies on some of the people reported in the media as fan death victims. What he found was not a mysterious new mechanism of death. He found elderly people, many with severe heart disease or alcoholism, whose deaths had ordinary medical explanations. The fan was present, but presence is not causation. A person who dies of a heart condition in a room with a television running has not been killed by the television, and the same logic applies here. Dr. Lee reportedly compared the claim to saying that a person could suffocate from a television or a radio, a comparison that captures the absurdity of treating a background appliance as a lethal agent.

Other medical voices have been blunter. Dr. Suh Hee-sun has said that not a single death caused by an electric fan has been reported anywhere in the world, a statement that, if taken literally, would mean the global death toll from the supposed mechanism is zero despite billions of fan-nights every year. That is a striking fact to set against the story's popularity. Fans are among the most widely used appliances on Earth. They run all night in homes across Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the southern United States, in rooms that are often closed against mosquitoes, noise, or cold. If fans could routinely kill sleepers in closed rooms, the phenomenon would not be confined to anecdotes from one country. It would be a visible global pattern in mortality statistics, hospital records, and coroners' reports. It is not.

There is also a direct experiment, small but telling. Professor Rim Chun-taek of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology reportedly sat for two hours in a closed room with a fan running and showed no change in his vital signs. Two hours is not a full night, and one volunteer is not a clinical trial, so this experiment alone does not settle the question. But it sits comfortably alongside the physics: a fan does not measurably alter the air a person breathes in a closed room over a short period, and there is no plausible mechanism by which extra hours would transform a harmless breeze into a lethal one. The burden of proof lies with the extraordinary claim, and the claim has never produced a verified mechanism, a controlled study, or a case in which other causes were excluded and the fan remained as the only explanation.

Sceptics have also pointed to the more ordinary explanations for the deaths that were attributed to fans. Carbon monoxide poisoning from faulty heating, undiagnosed heart disease, alcohol-related collapse, heatstroke during a heatwave, and electrocution from a damaged appliance can all kill a person in a bedroom, and all of them are more plausible than suffocation by breeze. When a death is investigated properly, one of these explanations usually emerges. When it is not investigated properly, the fan, sitting visibly in the corner, becomes the easiest story to tell. That is how a correlation becomes a legend: the fan is always there because fans are everywhere, and the real cause is invisible because it requires an autopsy, a toxicology screen, or an inspection of the wiring to find.

## The twist: fans may actually help babies breathe

Here the story takes an unexpected turn, and it is the part that most coverage of fan death misses. A fan in a baby's room is not merely harmless. In one well-known study, it was associated with a lower risk of death.

In 2008, researchers led by Kimberly Coleman-Phox at Kaiser Permanente's Division of Research published a case-control study in the Archives of Pediatrics and Adolescent Medicine. They compared the sleep environments of 185 infants who had died of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) with those of 312 randomly selected infants from the same counties, matched by maternal race and ethnicity and infant age. Having a fan on during sleep was associated with a 72 percent lower risk of SIDS compared with sleeping in a room without a fan, and the association was even stronger, a 94 percent lower risk, in rooms warmer than 21 degrees Celsius (about 69 degrees Fahrenheit). The effect was most pronounced in infants in adverse sleep environments, such as those sleeping on their sides or stomachs, sharing a bed with someone other than a parent, or not using a pacifier.

The researchers' proposed explanation fits the physics discussed above. A fan keeps air moving, which may prevent exhaled carbon dioxide from pooling around an infant's face, particularly when bedding or positioning limits airflow. An open window showed a similar but statistically weaker association, which is consistent with the same idea: ventilation, not suffocation, is what protects a sleeping baby.

This study needs careful handling, and honest reporting requires its caveats. It was an observational case-control study, not a randomised trial, and the authors acknowledged that recall bias could have played a role, since mothers of healthy infants reported sleep habits closer to the time of the interview than mothers who had lost a child. The finding has not been confirmed by a prospective trial, and it would be unethical and impractical to run one at the scale required. The study does not prove that fans prevent SIDS, and no responsible article should tell parents that a fan replaces the established safe-sleep guidance: babies on their backs, on a firm surface, without loose bedding, and without overheating. But the study does something important for this fact-check. It shows that the scientific literature, when it looks at fans and sleeping infants, finds an association in the opposite direction from fan death. If fans were suffocating sleepers, the last place one would expect to find a protective association is in the most vulnerable sleepers of all. The study does not prove fan death false on its own, but it makes the claim much harder to sustain.

## What a fan can actually do to you overnight

Debunking fan death should not become a claim that fans are harmless in every way. The honest picture is mixed, and the mixed picture is more useful than a simple yes or no. Sleeping with a fan on all night does have documented downsides, and people who wake up feeling worse after a night with a fan are not imagining things. They are just misattributing the cause.

The most common effect is dryness. Continuous airflow accelerates evaporation from the skin, the eyes, and the mucous membranes lining the nose and throat. Those membranes are part of the body's first line of defence: they trap particles, and tiny hair-like cilia sweep mucus and trapped material out of the nose. When they dry out, they work less well, the body responds by producing more mucus, and the sleeper can wake up congested, with a sore throat, a stuffy nose, or a headache that feels like the beginning of a cold. Research on low humidity and influenza transmission has found that dry air impairs this barrier function, which helps explain why a fan night can feel like a sick morning even though no virus was involved. The fan did not infect you. It dried out the defences that normally keep infections at bay, and it made the symptoms of an existing cold or allergy feel worse.

The second effect is allergen circulation. A fan does not filter air. It moves it, and with it, whatever is floating in it or sitting on its blades. Dust, pollen, pet dander, and mould spores that had settled on surfaces get stirred up and redistributed around the room, and a dusty fan grille can launch weeks of accumulated grime into the air the moment it is switched on. For people with allergies or asthma, that is a real problem. Doctors quoted in sleep and health coverage have warned that allergy sufferers may find their symptoms aggravated by a fan, with sneezing, itching, coughing, and wheezing as the typical complaints, and that children with asthma are particularly likely to notice the difference. Cleaning the blades regularly, keeping the fan at a distance, and using an oscillating setting rather than a fixed blast at the face all reduce this effect, but they do not eliminate it.

The third effect is muscular. A steady stream of cool air on one part of the body all night can cause muscles to tighten, and sleepers sometimes wake up with a stiff neck, an aching shoulder, or cramps that they did not have at bedtime. This is the same principle behind waking up sore after sleeping under an air-conditioning vent. It is uncomfortable, it can ruin a morning, and it is not dangerous in the way the fan death story claims. It is also easily avoided by pointing the fan at a wall, using oscillation, or moving it further from the bed.

There is also a benefit that balances these downsides, and it is one of the reasons so many people swear by their bedroom fan. The steady sound of a fan acts as white noise, masking traffic, snoring, and household sounds that would otherwise fragment sleep. For light sleepers, that masking effect can genuinely improve sleep quality, even if the airflow itself is a nuisance. The trade-off, then, is not between safety and danger. It is between better-masked sleep on one side and drier airways, more allergens, and possible stiffness on the other. Most people can have the white noise without the worst of the airflow by pointing the fan away from the bed, running it on a timer, or using a dedicated white-noise machine instead.

## The one situation where a fan genuinely becomes dangerous

There is one scenario in which a fan can contribute to a fatal outcome, and getting it right matters because it is the scenario most often confused with fan death. That scenario is extreme heat, and the danger is the heat, not the fan's breeze.

A fan does not cool a room. It cools a person, and only by helping sweat evaporate from the skin. Once the air is hotter than the skin, or so hot and dry that sweat evaporates too fast, that mechanism breaks down. The United States Environmental Protection Agency advises people not to use electric fans for cooling when the room temperature is in the mid-90s Fahrenheit or higher, warning that fans will not prevent heat-related illness at those temperatures. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention draws the line lower, advising fan use only when indoor temperatures are below 90 degrees Fahrenheit and warning that above that level a fan can increase body temperature. The World Health Organization advises using electric fans only below 40 degrees Celsius, about 104 degrees Fahrenheit, and warns that above 35 degrees Celsius, about 95 degrees Fahrenheit, fans may not prevent heat-related illness. The exact threshold varies by agency and by humidity, but the message is consistent: a fan is not a substitute for air conditioning in a severe heatwave, and trusting one as if it were can be fatal.

Research has added nuance without overturning that warning. A study from the University of Sydney, published in the Annals of Internal Medicine, tested healthy young men in hot conditions and found that fans remained helpful in hot and humid air, where sweat struggles to evaporate, even at temperatures around 104 degrees Fahrenheit with 50 percent humidity. In hot and dry air, by contrast, fans increased core temperature and cardiovascular strain, at around 116.6 degrees Fahrenheit with 10 percent humidity. The lesson is that temperature alone does not decide whether a fan helps or harms in extreme heat. Humidity matters, and so does the health of the person. Older adults, infants, and people with heart disease are more vulnerable to heat strain, and for them a fan as the sole source of cooling in temperatures above 95 degrees Fahrenheit can fail to reduce core temperature while creating a false sense of comfort. That is a genuine, documented risk, and it deserves more attention than the fan death myth. But it is a risk of relying on a fan instead of real cooling during a heatwave, not a risk of a fan suffocating a sleeper on an ordinary night.

The same distinction applies to the other genuine fan hazards, which are mundane and preventable. A damaged cord, a faulty motor, or a fan used near water can cause a fire or an electric shock, just as any electrical appliance can. A fan with unguarded blades can injure a curious child's fingers. A fan that has never been cleaned can aggravate asthma. None of these hazards requires a new theory of physics. They require the same care that any household appliance deserves: intact wiring, a stable position, regular cleaning, and replacement when the machine is damaged or recalled. If a fan ever does feature in a real bedroom tragedy, it is far more likely to be one of these ordinary failures than the legendary closed-room suffocation.

## Why the myth refuses to die

If the evidence is so one-sided, why does fan death persist? Part of the answer is cultural momentum. A belief that is built into the appliances themselves, in the form of a timer, teaches each new generation that there must be something to fear. A child who grows up watching a parent set the fan timer, crack open a door, and warn against sleeping with the fan on will inherit the behaviour long before encountering the physics. The behaviour then feels like common sense, and common sense is notoriously resistant to correction.

Part of the answer is also psychological. The fan death story is vivid, specific, and frightening in a way that "you might wake up with a dry throat" is not. A closed room, a humming machine, and a sleeper who never wakes up is a horror story with a clear villain, and horror stories travel further than physiology lessons. The story also offers a comforting illusion of control: set the timer, open the door, and you are safe. Real health advice is messier. It involves humidity, allergies, heatwaves, and individual vulnerability, and it rarely offers a single switch that guarantees safety. The timer feels like that switch, so people keep using it even after they learn the science, just in case.

There is also a pattern that runs through many of the myths this site has examined. A genuine observation gets attached to the wrong cause. People did die in rooms where fans were running, just as people who catch colds often have wet hair, and people who feel warm after drinking alcohol are experiencing a real sensation. In each case the observation is real and the explanation is wrong, and the wrong explanation survives because it is simpler than the truth. Readers who enjoy this kind of detective work may recognise the pattern from our earlier look at whether <a href="/blog/wet-hair-cold-weather-causes-colds-fact-check/">going out with wet hair in cold weather causes colds</a>, from the myth that <a href="/blog/lose-most-body-heat-through-head-myth-fact-check/">you lose most of your body heat through your head</a>, and from the question of whether <a href="/blog/does-mouth-taping-help-you-sleep-fact-check/">mouth taping helps you sleep</a>. In all of these cases, a kernel of real experience sits inside a claim that the evidence does not support.

Finally, the myth persists because it is rarely corrected by the institutions that once endorsed it. When a government consumer body and news outlets report alleged cases without follow-up, the public record fills up with uncorrected stories, and later debunking has to fight against years of accumulated anecdotes. Fact-checkers, including Snopes, have debunked fan death, and medical authorities have said plainly that it does not happen. But a debunking article rarely travels as far as the original scary story, and a timer on a fan keeps whispering the old warning every night, long after the article has been forgotten.

## How to sleep with a fan safely

The practical advice that follows from the evidence is simple, and it does not require fear. If you like sleeping with a fan on, you can keep doing so, with a few adjustments that address the real downsides rather than the imaginary one.

First, do not aim the fan directly at your face all night. Point it at a wall, use the oscillating setting, or place it across the room so the air circulates without blasting one spot. This reduces dryness, stiffness, and the direct drying of your eyes and airways, while preserving the white-noise benefit and the general air movement.

Second, clean the fan regularly. Wipe the blades and the grille, especially if you have allergies, asthma, or pets. A clean fan stirs up far less dust than a dirty one, and the difference is often noticeable within a night or two.

Third, keep some distance and use a timer if you like, not because the fan will kill you without one, but because a few hours of airflow is often enough to fall asleep comfortably, and switching off later in the night reduces morning dryness. The timer is a comfort tool, not a life-saving device, and there is no harm in using it as one.

Fourth, treat extreme heat as a separate problem. If the room is in the mid-90s Fahrenheit or hotter, do not rely on a fan alone, especially for older adults, infants, or anyone with heart disease. Seek air conditioning, a cooling centre, or at least a cooler room, and watch for the signs of heat-related illness: dizziness, nausea, confusion, a rapid pulse, and hot, dry skin. The fan is not the enemy in a heatwave, but it is not the rescue either.

Fifth, keep the basics of electrical safety in mind. Do not use a fan with a frayed cord or a damaged plug, keep it away from water, make sure it stands stably where it cannot fall onto bedding, and keep small fingers away from the blades. These are the precautions that actually prevent fan-related injuries, and they have nothing to do with closed doors or sealed rooms.

## The Verdict

Can sleeping with a fan on kill you? The claim that an ordinary electric fan can suffocate a healthy sleeper in a closed room is False. No verified mechanism exists, no credible case has survived investigation, and the deaths attributed to fans turn out, on examination, to have ordinary medical explanations such as heart disease, alcohol-related collapse, or heatstroke. A fan moves air; it does not remove oxygen, create a vacuum, or chill a healthy adult into hypothermia in a normal bedroom. The belief's long history, its official-looking reports, and the timers built into Korean fans explain why it feels authoritative, but none of those things constitutes evidence that the mechanism is real.

What is true is narrower and more useful. A fan can dry your airways, stir up allergens, worsen congestion and asthma, and leave your muscles stiff. It can also mask noise and help you sleep, and in one observational study it was associated with a lower risk of sudden infant death, probably because moving air disperses exhaled carbon dioxide. And in extreme heat, a fan cannot protect you on its own, and trusting it as if it could is genuinely dangerous. Those are the real trade-offs of a bedroom fan. They deserve attention, they can be managed with simple habits, and they have nothing to do with the closed-room suffocation story.

So set the timer if it helps you sleep, clean the blades, point the fan away from your face, and crack a window if you like the fresh air. Do all of that for comfort, not for survival. The fan on your nightstand is not a killer. It is a machine that moves air, and after a century of fear, the air it moves is finally allowed to be just air.

## What the history teaches us about health scares

Fan death is worth studying beyond the bedroom because it shows how a health scare is built. It starts with a plausible worry, gains authority from official reports that mistake correlation for causation, gets embedded in product design, and then survives on the strength of habit and vivid storytelling. Each step feels reasonable on its own. Together they create a belief that can outlive every debunking, because the belief no longer depends on evidence. It depends on the timer, the parental warning, and the news story that nobody followed up.

The antidote is not mockery. It is the habit of asking, for any scary claim, what the mechanism would have to be, whether that mechanism has ever been observed, and what else could explain the cases cited in its favour. Applied to fan death, those questions dissolve the story quickly. The mechanism would require a household fan to do something no household fan can do. The cases, when investigated, have other causes. And the alternative explanations, from heart disease to heatstroke to faulty wiring, are not exotic. They are the ordinary tragedies that happen in bedrooms every night, with or without a fan running.

That habit is also what makes the rest of the story worth keeping. Fans are useful, cheap, and widely available, and in a warming world they will remain part of how billions of people cope with hot nights. Understanding what they can and cannot do is not a trivial matter. It determines whether people in heatwaves seek real cooling or settle for a breeze, whether allergy sufferers blame a virus for what a dusty grille caused, and whether parents worry about the wrong danger while missing the real ones. A myth that makes people afraid of their fan is not harmless, even if the fan itself is. It distracts from the heat, the wiring, and the dust that actually matter, and it teaches a lesson that fear, repeated often enough, can feel like knowledge.

The next time you notice a timer on a fan, or hear someone warn you not to sleep with one running in a closed room, you will know the history behind the warning and the evidence behind the answer. The timer is a cultural artefact, not a safety device. The fan is a breeze, not a threat. And the sleeper, on an ordinary night in an ordinary room, is safe to let it run.

## A note on sources and limits

This article draws on the published history of the fan death belief, on the physics of how electric fans move air, on medical commentary from physicians who examined reported cases, on the 2008 Coleman-Phox case-control study of fan use and sudden infant death syndrome, and on heat guidance from the United States Environmental Protection Agency, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the World Health Organization, together with the University of Sydney research on fans in hot and humid versus hot and dry conditions. Where sources disagree on an exact temperature threshold for fan use in extreme heat, this article reports the range rather than pretending to a single number, because humidity, age, and health change the answer.

No article can substitute for medical advice about an individual person. If you have asthma, sleep apnoea, heart disease, or another condition that affects breathing or temperature regulation, or if a child in your care has breathing difficulties, speak to a clinician about your sleep environment rather than relying on a general fact-check. What this article can say, and what the evidence supports, is that the general claim at its centre, that sleeping with a fan on in a closed room can kill a healthy person, is false, and that the real risks of bedroom fans are smaller, duller, and much easier to manage than the legend suggests.

## The bottom line for tonight

If you are reading this before bed, wondering whether to switch the fan on, the answer is simple. Switch it on if it helps you sleep. Point it away from your face, make sure it is clean and its cord is intact, and do not expect it to cool a dangerously hot room on its own. You will probably wake up with a slightly dry throat, and you may need a glass of water. You will not wake up, or fail to wake up, because the fan stole the air from your room. A century of fear has not produced a single verified case of that happening, and a century of fans running in bedrooms around the world has produced something much more ordinary: cooler skin, masked noise, drier air, and, for most people, a better night's sleep.

That is the verdict, and it is a liberating one. The fan is not your enemy. The heatwave might be, the dusty grille might be, and the frayed cord certainly could be, but the breeze itself is innocent. Sleep well, and let the fan do what it was built to do.
