It is one of the oldest pieces of drinking advice in the English language, and almost everyone has heard it at a party: "Beer before liquor, never been sicker; liquor before beer, you're in the clear." The rhyme is short, it is catchy, and it sounds like the kind of folk wisdom that must have survived for a reason. Generations of drinkers have used it to plan their evenings, starting with spirits and switching to beer, convinced that the sequence itself would protect them from a brutal morning.

The claim feels plausible because most people can point to a night that seems to prove it. They started on beer, moved on to shots, and woke up feeling terrible. The order looks like the culprit. But a feeling is not evidence, and when researchers finally put the rhyme to a proper test — giving volunteers beer and wine in both orders, in matched doses, under controlled conditions — the order made no difference at all. What mattered was how much alcohol people drank, how drunk they felt, and whether they vomited. The rhyme, in other words, is a story we tell about a bad morning, not a rule the body follows.

## The Rhyme Everyone Knows

The saying exists in several versions, and they do not even agree with each other. The best-known English version warns against beer first: "Beer before liquor, never been sicker; liquor before beer, you're in the clear." A companion rhyme deals with wine: "Wine before beer, you'll feel queer; beer before wine, you'll feel fine." And there is a third, stricter rule that bans mixing altogether: "Grape or grain, but never the twain." Between them, these rhymes cover almost every possible sequence, which should already make us suspicious. If every order is wrong in some version of the saying, the saying cannot be a reliable guide to any of them.

The idea behind the rhyme is that the body somehow reacts badly to a specific sequence — that beer first "primes" the stomach or the liver in a way that makes the hard liquor that follows hit harder. Some versions of the folk theory claim that beer dilutes the stomach's defences, or that switching drinks confuses the liver, or that carbonation in beer speeds up the absorption of whatever comes next. None of these mechanisms has ever been demonstrated. The liver, for its part, does not know or care what order the ethanol arrived in. It processes alcohol at a roughly steady rate — about one standard drink per hour for most adults — regardless of whether that alcohol came from a pint glass or a shot glass, and regardless of which came first.

What the rhyme really describes is a pattern of behaviour, not a pattern of chemistry. Beer is usually drunk faster and in larger volumes than spirits, and a night that starts with beer often ends with someone drinking more total alcohol than they planned. The rhyme then gets the credit — or the blame — for a result that the total dose actually caused. That distinction matters, because it changes the advice completely. If the order were the problem, you could drink as much as you liked as long as you sequenced it correctly. If the dose is the problem, no sequence can save you.

## What the Experiment Actually Found

For years, the rhyme survived untested. That changed in 2019, when a team at Witten/Herdecke University in Germany decided to test it properly. The study, led by Jöran Köchling and published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* under the title "Grape or grain but never the twain?", was designed specifically to answer the question drinkers had been arguing about for generations: does the combination and order of beer and wine change how bad your hangover is?

The design was unusually careful. Ninety volunteers aged 19 to 40, with a mean age of 23.9 and an even split of men and women, were matched into triplets according to age, gender, body composition, drinking habits, and how often they got hangovers. One group drank beer first, up to a breath alcohol concentration of at least 0.05 percent, and then switched to white wine until they reached 0.11 percent. A second group did the same thing in reverse: wine first, then beer. A control group drank only beer or only wine, all the way to the same target. Then, at least a week later, everyone came back and did it again with the order swapped, so each person served as their own comparison. Hangover severity the next morning was measured with a standard tool, the Acute Hangover Scale.

The result was unambiguous. The researchers reported that neither the type nor the order of the alcoholic beverages significantly affected hangover intensity. Whether volunteers drank beer then wine, wine then beer, or stuck to a single drink, their hangovers were statistically indistinguishable once the total amount of alcohol was matched. The study's own summary put it plainly: "Neither type nor order of consumed alcoholic beverages significantly affected hangover intensity." The strongest predictors of a bad morning were not what people drank, or in what order, but how drunk they felt and whether they vomited.

The trial was registered in advance with the Witten/Herdecke University ethics committee and retrospectively with the German Clinical Trials Register, and its conclusion has been widely reported since, including by Harvard's health publications. It tested beer and wine rather than beer and spirits, which is a fair limitation to note — nobody in the study was doing shots of vodka after pints of lager. But the underlying physiology is the same: ethanol is ethanol, and the body clears it at a fixed rate no matter which bottle it came from. Nothing in the pharmacology of alcohol suggests that swapping wine for whisky would resurrect an effect that beer and wine failed to show.

The United States National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism reaches the same conclusion in its public guidance on hangovers. Its position is that the more alcohol a person drinks, the worse the hangover will be, and that this holds whether the person drinks beer, wine, distilled spirits, or a combination of them. The institute treats drink order as folklore, not as a factor in hangover severity. When the country's leading alcohol research body and a controlled trial agree, the rhyme is in serious trouble.

## Why the Myth Feels True

If the order does not matter, why do so many people swear it does? The answer is that the rhyme captures something real about how drinking sessions unfold, and then mislabels it. The mechanism is pace, not sequence, and it is easy to see once you look at the numbers.

A typical beer is around 4 to 5 percent alcohol by volume. A typical spirit is around 40 percent — roughly ten times stronger. Dr. Rueben Gonzales, a professor of pharmacology and toxicology at the University of Texas, has explained the trap this creates: if you start the evening drinking beer at a comfortable pace, and then carry that same pace over to mixed drinks or shots, it is like driving slowly and then suddenly stepping on the gas. Your mouth may not notice the change, but your body does. The same number of sips now delivers far more alcohol per minute, and your blood alcohol level climbs much faster than it did during the beer phase.

The reverse sequence hides this trap. If you start with hard liquor, you tend to drink more slowly, because the drink is stronger and you feel its effects sooner. By the time you switch to beer, you are already feeling drunk, and you are likely to drink less of it, more slowly. The total amount of alcohol ends up lower, and the morning feels better — not because the order protected you, but because the order changed how much you drank. As Dr. Roshini Rajapaksa of New York University has put it, it is the total quantity of alcohol consumed, not the combination, that influences intoxication and sickness. Sandra Arévalo, a spokesperson for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, made the same point after reviewing the evidence: what really matters is the amount of alcohol that you consume.

There is also a smaller, genuine piece of chemistry that gets folded into the myth. A small 2007 study from the Universities of Manchester and Lancashire, with 21 volunteers, found that diluted alcohol is absorbed faster than concentrated alcohol, and that vodka mixed with carbonated water entered the bloodstream faster than vodka mixed with still water. Carbonation and dilution do affect absorption speed. But that finding cuts both ways: a beer, being dilute and carbonated, is absorbed quickly on its own, and a shot of neat spirits is absorbed more slowly than a mixed drink of the same alcohol content. None of it adds up to a rule about which drink should come first. It adds up to a warning about drinking quickly, whatever you are drinking.

Expectation plays a role too. Research on alcohol and behaviour has repeatedly found that what people expect a drink to do shapes how impaired they feel. A study by Dr. Maria Testa at the University at Buffalo found substantial evidence that those who expect the least impairment are the least impaired, and those who expect the most impairment are the most impaired, under the same dose. If you go into an evening convinced that beer before liquor will make you sick, you are primed to notice every wave of nausea and to blame the sequence. The rhyme becomes self-confirming: you remember the nights that fit it, and you forget the nights that did not.

## What Really Decides How Drunk You Get

If the order is irrelevant, what actually determines whether you wake up with a headache or sail through the morning? The evidence points to a short list of factors, and none of them is the sequence of your drinks.

The first and most important is the total dose. Blood alcohol concentration is determined by how much alcohol you consume over a period of time, adjusted for your weight, sex, and metabolism. The more ethanol in your system, the worse the hangover, and the relationship is steady and predictable. This is why the 2019 trial matched total alcohol across all its groups: once dose was held constant, the order effect vanished. It is also why "mixing" so often looks guilty. People who mix drinks tend to drink more overall, because each new type of drink resets the sense of how much they have had. A shot of tequila goes down far faster than a pint of beer, even though both contain roughly the same amount of alcohol, so a round of shots can add up to several pints' worth of ethanol in a few minutes.

The second factor is speed. Alcohol begins to be absorbed into your bloodstream as soon as it reaches your stomach, and peak concentrations are typically reached within 30 to 90 minutes. Higher-concentration drinks produce a higher peak blood alcohol level and reach that peak faster. Drinking quickly also overwhelms the liver's steady processing rate, so more alcohol circulates in the blood for longer. Eating before and during drinking slows absorption by keeping alcohol in the stomach longer, which is why drinking on an empty stomach hits so much harder — food delays the effect but does not reduce the total dose.

The third factor is what kind of drink you choose, because not all alcohol is the same. Fermentation produces byproducts called congeners — chemical compounds that travel alongside the ethanol and amplify hangover symptoms. Darker, more complex drinks contain far more of them. A Brown University study published in *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research* tested this directly: around 95 volunteers drank a high dose of either bourbon or vodka on separate nights, with total ethanol held constant at roughly the equivalent of six standard drinks. Bourbon produced significantly worse hangover symptom scores than vodka. Red wine carries its own extra penalty: a 2023 study in *Nature Scientific Reports* found that quercetin glucuronide, a compound in red wine, can block ALDH2, the enzyme that clears acetaldehyde — the toxic compound the liver produces when it breaks down alcohol. When ALDH2 is blocked, acetaldehyde builds up faster, and the headache and nausea get worse.

Individual biology matters too. Around a quarter of people who drink alcohol report that they never experience hangovers, whatever they drink. Genetics, sex, body weight, and drinking history all shape how quickly someone metabolises alcohol and how strongly they feel its after-effects. Women, for example, generally have lower levels of the stomach enzyme that begins breaking down alcohol, and less body water to dilute it, so the same number of drinks produces a higher blood alcohol concentration. None of this has anything to do with the order of drinks either — it is a reminder that the same evening can produce very different mornings for two people sitting side by side.

Sleep disruption and dehydration complete the picture. Alcohol suppresses the hormone that helps the kidneys conserve water, so drinkers lose more fluid than they take in — the body can expel several times as much water as the volume of alcohol consumed. It also fragments sleep, so even a long night in bed after drinking is less restorative than it looks. Both effects contribute to the classic hangover symptoms of headache, thirst, fatigue, and nausea. Drinking water between alcoholic drinks helps with the dehydration, but it does nothing about the acetaldehyde, the congeners, or the broken sleep. That is why water is useful and also why it is not a cure.

It is worth noting what does not work, either. A 2005 review that examined hangover remedies from multivitamins to chasers found that virtually none of them had good evidence behind them. The morning-after industry is built on the same kind of folk reasoning as the rhyme itself: a plausible mechanism, a satisfying story, and no controlled data. The only reliable way to avoid a hangover is to drink less, drink more slowly, and give your liver time to do its job.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: False.**

The claim that drinking beer before liquor makes you drunker — or sicker — than drinking them in the opposite order is false. A controlled trial of 90 people, published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* in 2019, tested beer-first and wine-first drinking in both orders at matched doses and found no difference in hangover severity. The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism states the same conclusion: how bad a hangover is depends on how much alcohol you drink, not on the order or the combination. What feels like an effect of sequence is really an effect of pace and total dose — beer first tends to mean drinking faster and drinking more, and it is the extra alcohol, not the order, that punishes you in the morning.

That does not mean the rhyme is useless. It survives because it accidentally encodes good advice: slow down when you switch to stronger drinks, and pay attention to how much you are actually drinking. But as a rule about the body, it is wrong, and believing it can be dangerous. Anyone who trusts the sequence to protect them — drinking hard liquor first and then assuming the beer that follows is harmless — is relying on a shield that does not exist. The liver keeps the same score regardless of the order in which the drinks arrive.

## How to Actually Feel Better Tomorrow

Since no sequence can protect you, the practical advice is simpler and less rhyming. Count your drinks, and count them honestly: a standard drink is roughly a 12-ounce beer at 5 percent alcohol, a 5-ounce glass of wine at 12 percent, or a 1.5-ounce shot of spirits at 40 percent. Those three contain about the same amount of pure alcohol, which is why a round of shots can equal several pints before you have noticed. Pace yourself at no more than about one standard drink per hour, which is roughly the rate the average liver can clear alcohol. Eat a proper meal before you start, and keep eating if the night goes on. Alternate alcoholic drinks with water or another non-alcoholic drink, and stop drinking well before you go to bed so your body has time to clear some of the alcohol before you sleep.

If you do wake up hungover, the unglamorous remedies are the ones with the most support: water, food, rest, and a painkiller such as ibuprofen if you can safely take one. Coffee will not sober you up, as we have explained in our earlier fact-check <a href="/blog/does-coffee-sober-you-up-fact-check/">Does Coffee Sober You Up? No — It Only Makes a Drunk Person Feel Awake</a> — caffeine can make a drunk person feel more alert, but it does not lower blood alcohol or restore judgement. A morning drink will not help either; our fact-check <a href="/blog/hair-of-the-dog-hangover-cure-fact-check/">Hair of the Dog Won't Cure Your Hangover — It Just Hits Snooze on One</a> found that it only postpones the hangover rather than curing it. And if you have ever wondered about the other famous cold-weather drinking claim, see <a href="/blog/drinking-alcohol-warm-you-up-myth-fact-check/">No, a Drink Won't Warm You Up — the Winter Myth That Makes You Colder</a>: alcohol sends warm blood to your skin and makes you feel warmer while your core temperature actually falls. Drinking myths cluster together because they share a structure — a vivid sensation, a simple story, and no controlled evidence — and they fall apart in the same way when the evidence arrives.

## Why Rhymes Outlive Evidence

There is a final reason the beer-before-liquor rhyme has survived for so long, and it has nothing to do with alcohol. Rhymes are memorable, and memorable claims feel true. Psychologists call this the fluency effect: the easier a statement is to process, the more likely we are to believe it. "Beer before liquor, never been sicker" scans perfectly, and its partner line resolves it neatly. The sentence feels finished, and a finished sentence feels like a fact. A journal article titled "Grape or grain but never the twain? A randomized controlled multiarm matched-triplet crossover trial of beer and wine" does not scan at all, and it will never be quoted at a bar.

The rhyme also benefits from a selection effect. Everyone remembers the night the rhyme seemed to work, because that night hurt. The nights when the rhyme failed — beer first, no hangover; liquor first, terrible morning — are forgotten, because they do not fit the story. The German researchers who ran the 2019 trial understood this: that is why they matched their volunteers, controlled the doses, and made everyone repeat the experiment in the opposite order. Memory is a biased witness. A crossover trial is not.

The same pattern shows up across the whole family of drinking folklore. The idea that mixing drinks is inherently dangerous, that coffee sobers you up, that alcohol warms you up in cold weather, that a morning drink cures a hangover — each of these claims has the same shape, and each of them has failed when tested properly. Alcohol is one of the most studied drugs in the world, and its rules are now well understood: dose, speed, body size, food, sleep, and the chemical company the ethanol keeps. The order of the bottles on the table is not on the list, and no amount of rhyming will put it there.

The next time someone at a party recites the rhyme with total confidence, you can smile, order whatever you like, and count your drinks instead of sequencing them. The rhyme will still be there tomorrow — rhymes always are. But the evidence is in, and the evidence says the order never mattered. What matters is how much, how fast, and whether you gave your body a fighting chance. Drink less, drink slower, eat something, and let the liver do its work. Your morning self will thank you, whatever order the glasses arrived in.
