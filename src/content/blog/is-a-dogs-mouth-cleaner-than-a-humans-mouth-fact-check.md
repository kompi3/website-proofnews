Your dog has just licked your face, thoroughly and with great enthusiasm, and someone in the room laughs and says it: "Don't worry — a dog's mouth is cleaner than a human's." It sounds reassuring. It sounds like one of those folk wisdoms that must have some truth behind it, because everybody seems to know it, and nobody seems to question it. Dog owners repeat it at the park, parents repeat it when the family Labrador plants a kiss on a toddler, and it has been repeated for so long that it feels less like a claim and more like a fact of life.

It is not a fact. It is a myth — one with a genuinely fascinating history, a grain of real science at its core, and a conclusion that modern microbiology has settled fairly decisively. A dog's mouth is not cleaner than a human's mouth. It is not dirtier, either, in any simple sense. The honest answer is stranger and more interesting than either version of the slogan: the two mouths are different ecosystems, full of different microbes, and "cleaner" is simply the wrong word for comparing them. What matters is not which mouth wins a cleanliness contest, but which germs can actually make you sick — and there the story has a few surprises.

## The Claim Everyone Has Heard

The claim comes in a few different wordings, but the core is always the same: a dog's mouth contains fewer germs, or less dangerous germs, than a human mouth. Sometimes it is stated as a comparison — cleaner than yours or mine. Sometimes it is stated as a prescription — let the dog lick a cut, because dog saliva heals wounds. Sometimes it is stated as reassurance — a dog licking a baby's face is fine, because dogs are naturally clean animals.

Dog myths are stubborn things. People also believe that <a href="/blog/one-dog-year-seven-human-years-myth-fact-check/">one dog year equals seven human years</a>, a conversion that veterinarians abandoned long ago, and the mouth myth survives in the same way: it is simple, it flatters our affection for our pets, and it seems to explain something we have all observed — dogs lick their wounds, and their wounds usually heal. The leap from that observation to "cleaner than a human mouth" feels small. It is not small at all, and it took modern DNA sequencing to show exactly why.

Before we look at the evidence, it is worth asking what "cleaner" could even mean. A mouth is not a kitchen counter. It cannot be wiped down and declared germ-free. Every healthy mouth — dog or human — is a thriving habitat for hundreds of species of bacteria, along with fungi and viruses. Counting them is possible. Ranking them by cleanliness is not, because the species that live in a dog are mostly not the species that live in a human, and a germ that is harmless in one host can be dangerous in another. The question, as veterinary dentists keep pointing out, is built on a category error. It is like asking whether a rainforest is cleaner than a coral reef.

That does not mean the answer is a shrug. It means the answer has two parts. First: no, a dog's mouth is not cleaner than a human's, by any measurement scientists have tried. Second: the reasons people believe it anyway reveal something real about how different our two microbiomes are — and about how the human body reacts to foreign germs.

## Where the Myth Comes From

The idea did not appear out of nowhere, and it did not start with modern pet culture. It is one of the oldest medical folk beliefs in the Western world, with roots that reach back to the ancient Mediterranean.

The Egyptians were among the earliest believers. Dogs held a sacred place in Egyptian religion — Anubis, the god of the afterlife, had the head of a jackal — and Egyptians kept dogs as hunters, guards, and companions, even mummifying some of them. They believed that having a dog lick a wound would speed healing, and they kept dogs in temples where the sick could come to be licked. In an age without antibiotics, a treatment that visibly cleaned a wound and seemed to help it close would have been worth believing in.

The Greeks picked the belief up from the Egyptians and built it into their medicine. The healing temples of Asclepius, the god of medicine, kept sacred dogs that were trained to lick the wounds of patients who slept in the temple overnight. The practice was taken seriously enough to become part of religious ritual, and the idea of the healing dog tongue travelled with Greek medicine into Rome and then into medieval Europe. A thirteenth-century writer, Gerald of Wales, stated it flatly in his book *The Journey Through Wales*: "A dog's tongue has healing powers." He added that a hurt dog could heal itself by licking its wounds, and that if a dog could not reach a wound with its tongue, it would transfer the healing properties to the wound with its hind foot. Gerald was a priest, not a doctor, and the business about the hind foot is plainly nonsense — but the core belief he recorded was already centuries old by his time.

The story even appears in the Bible. In the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, the beggar Lazarus lies at the rich man's gate covered in sores, and "the dogs came and licked his sores." The image stuck. Later, Saint Roch, a fourteenth-century French saint who tended plague victims, was said to have been nursed back to health in the forest by a dog that licked his plague sores. By the time folk-belief collectors in twentieth-century America wrote down the sayings of rural Illinois, the instruction had become a numbered entry in a list: "Let a dog lick a wound to heal it."

None of this proves the claim, but it explains its staying power. For thousands of years, people watched dogs lick wounds — their own and their owners' — and watched the wounds get better. In a world where most wounds did heal on their own, the licking looked like the cause. The belief was passed down as folk medicine long before anyone could see a bacterium, let alone sequence one.

## Two Mouths, Two Ecosystems

The first thing modern science did to the myth was count. And the counting, it turns out, is the easy part: both mouths are crowded.

According to the American Kennel Club, a dog's mouth carries more than 600 different types of bacteria. A 2020 study cited by veterinary site PetMD put the figure more precisely at 714 types of bacteria in a dog's mouth. Human mouths, by the same kind of measurement, contain around 700 types, according to the National Institutes of Health, while other estimates from Harvard researchers put the human figure at roughly 615 species. Whichever estimate you prefer, the numbers are in the same league. Neither mouth is remotely close to sterile, and neither one is dramatically less populated than the other. If cleanliness were a head count, the contest would be a draw.

But a head count is the wrong measurement, and this is where the story gets interesting. When researchers used genomic sequencing to compare the two microbiomes directly — most famously in a study published in the journal *PLOS ONE* by bacterial geneticist Floyd Dewhirst and colleagues — they found that only about 15 percent of the bacterial species in a dog's mouth are also found in a human mouth. Another summary of the same research puts the overlap at 16.4 percent. Either way, more than four out of every five bacterial species in your dog's mouth do not live in yours, and vice versa.

That is the central fact of this whole question, and it explains almost everything else. The two mouths are not two versions of the same thing, one cleaner and one dirtier. They are two different habitats, shaped by millions of years of separate evolution and by very different diets and behaviours. Humans carry mostly gram-positive bacteria suited to our diet and our oral environment. Dogs carry a more diverse spectrum, including gram-negative organisms associated with soil, faeces, and decaying material — which makes sense when you remember what dogs do with their mouths. A dog explores the world by putting it in its mouth. It sniffs garbage, licks its own rear end, eats things off the ground, and in many households raids the cat's litter box. Its mouth is a sampling device for the entire environment, and its microbiome reflects that.

The species each mouth carries also differ in the diseases they cause. Dogs carry their own version of the gum-disease bacterium *Porphyromonas* — *Porphyromonas gulae* — while humans carry its cousin *Porphyromonas gingivalis*. Both cause periodontal disease in their own host. Both mouths, in other words, carry bacteria that rot teeth and inflame gums. Neither is winning that contest either.

Dr. Colin Harvey, a professor of surgery and dentistry at the University of Pennsylvania's School of Veterinary Medicine and executive secretary of the American Veterinary Dental College, has summed up the comparison in the phrase everyone now quotes: comparing a dog's mouth to a human's mouth is "like comparing apples to oranges." Veterinary dental specialists and veterinary microbiologists who have weighed in through the American Kennel Club and the Diamond Pet Foods veterinary team have been even blunter, calling the idea that a dog's mouth is cleaner an emphatic no. The mouths are, in the words of one veterinary summary, "really dirty apples and really filthy oranges."

## The Kids Who Tested It in a Lab

One of the charming things about this myth is that it has repeatedly been tested by schoolchildren — and the children, working with real laboratories, produced results that professional scientists still cite.

The best-known test came from an eighth-grader named Abby, who tackled the question for a science project and won a Young Naturalists Award from the American Museum of Natural History in 2011. Working with a mentor at the State Hygienic Laboratory at the University of Iowa, she swabbed the tongues of dogs and humans, cultured the samples on agar plates, and counted what grew. Her hypothesis was that human tongues would be cleaner. The results were more complicated than that. Humans, she found, carried more bacteria in total — most of the humans had a "moderate" number of bacteria, while most of the dogs had "few." But the dogs carried more *types* of bacteria: an average of about 5.7 different bacterial colonies per dog, against about 4.1 per human. Her conclusion, in her own words, was that "dogs' mouths are cleaner than humans' in some ways, and dirtier in other ways." Asked whether she would let her dog keep licking her, she answered yes.

Two earlier California State Science Fair projects had come to different conclusions, which tells you something about how sensitive this kind of test is to method. In 2005, Lauren Palmer swabbed 25 dogs and 25 humans, diluted the saliva, and incubated the samples on blood agar plates for 72 hours. The dogs had consistently lower bacterial growth than the humans; 28 percent of the dogs had no growth at all on day three, while every human sample grew, and most dogs had 100 colonies or fewer where most humans had at least 250. Her conclusion: "the mouth of man's best friend was cleaner than his master's." Five years later, Morgan Ard ran a similar experiment with 15 dogs and 13 humans and got the opposite result: the dog saliva grew faster, produced many more colonies, and showed more colours — pink, orange, white, grey, and black — while the human samples produced fewer colonies in fewer colours, and the number of colonies in the human samples actually fell after 75 hours. Her conclusion was that there were many more bacterial colonies in the dog samples than in the human or control samples.

What should we make of three school experiments that point in different directions? Exactly what the scientists say: total colony counts on a plate are a crude measure. They depend on when the samples were taken, whether the humans had brushed their teeth, what the dogs had eaten, how the samples were diluted, and which bacteria happen to grow well on that particular agar. None of the experiments measured the thing that actually matters for health, which is not how many colonies grow on a plate but which species are present and whether they can infect a human. That question needed DNA sequencing, and sequencing gave the answer we have already seen: different ecosystems, about 15 percent overlap, no winner.

The school experiments are still worth remembering, though, because they capture the myth's appeal. The question is testable, it is fun to test, and it produces an answer that feels satisfying — until you realise the test was measuring the wrong thing. Abby's own balanced conclusion is probably the wisest sentence ever written on the subject: cleaner in some ways, dirtier in others. That is not a verdict of "cleaner." It is a verdict of "it depends what you mean," and the claim as usually stated — a dog's mouth *is* cleaner than a human's — is not what she found.

## What Dog Saliva Can Actually Do

If the myth is false, why did so many generations of people believe it, and why do wounds licked by dogs often seem to heal? Because there is a real grain of truth in it — just not the one the slogan claims.

Saliva, in dogs and in humans, contains compounds with genuine antibacterial and wound-healing properties. The best studied are proteins called histatins, identified by researchers at the University of Amsterdam, which can fight off bacteria and help cells move across a wound surface to close it. Dog saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that breaks down bacterial cell walls, along with other antimicrobial peptides. And the physical act of licking does something useful on its own: it removes dirt, debris, and dead tissue from a wound, which lowers the risk of infection and clears the way for healing. That is why animals of almost every species lick their wounds, and why humans have the same instinct — you have probably licked a paper cut without thinking about it.

There is even evidence that licked wounds can heal faster than unlicked ones, in animals at least. The effect is real enough that scientists have studied it seriously rather than dismissing it outright. But two important qualifications follow. First, human saliva has the same properties. The histatins that help wounds were first studied in human saliva. Whatever healing power dog saliva has, your own saliva has it too, so the comparison does not favour the dog. Second, the benefit applies to the dog's own wounds, on the dog's own body, where the bacteria in its saliva are bacteria its immune system already knows. Transferring that saliva to a human wound is a different proposition entirely, because the bacteria that are harmless in the dog may not be harmless in you.

This is the honest version of the folk belief. Dogs licking their own wounds helps the dogs, for real and measurable reasons. The leap from there to "a dog's mouth is cleaner than a human's" was never justified, and the leap from there to "let a dog lick your wound" is actively dangerous, for reasons we are about to see.

## When a Dog's Mouth Can Make You Sick

Here is the part of the story that the slogan hides. A dog's mouth carries bacteria that are harmless to dogs and dangerous to people, and when those bacteria get into a human body through the wrong route, the consequences can be severe.

The most feared is *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, a bacterium that lives harmlessly in the mouths of a large share of healthy dogs — studies have found it in anywhere from 16 to 41 percent of them. In dogs it causes no illness at all. In humans it can cause sepsis, meningitis, and organ failure, sometimes within 24 to 72 hours of exposure, and it has led to limb loss and death. The people at greatest risk are those with weakened immune systems, people who have had their spleen removed, and people with heavy alcohol use, but severe cases have also struck apparently healthy adults. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that *Capnocytophaga* germs can spread from dogs and cats to people through bites, scratches, or close contact, and that while infection is rare, it can be devastating. It is rare — that matters, and we should not exaggerate it — but it is real, and it is a direct consequence of the fact that a dog's mouth is full of germs your body has never met.

More common is *Pasteurella*, the bacterium found in more than half of infected dog-bite wounds. *Pasteurella canis* and *Pasteurella multocida* cause painful, red, rapidly spreading skin infections, sometimes within 12 to 24 hours of a bite. Then there is the sheer scale of the problem. According to the CDC, about 4.5 million dog bites occur every year in the United States, and about 800,000 of them are serious enough to need medical attention. Roughly one in five dog bites becomes infected — estimates range from 15 to 20 percent — and a single bite can introduce more than 60 species of bacteria into a wound, which is why bite infections are so often polymicrobial, a mixture of many organisms at once. Dogs also account for 80 to 90 percent of all animal bites seen in American emergency departments.

The risks are not limited to bites. Dogs fed raw meat diets can carry salmonella in their mouths, and that salmonella can be passed to a person if the dog licks them after eating contaminated food. Dogs can also carry parasites such as roundworms, which can be transferred through saliva, and bacteria such as *E. coli* and *Staphylococcus*. Owners who let dogs lick their faces, particularly around the mouth, may be exposing themselves to organisms that have no business in a human body. The risk to a healthy adult from an occasional lick on intact skin is low, and the evidence supports that reassurance. But the risk is not zero, and it rises sharply when the saliva meets an open wound, a mucous membrane, or a person whose immune system is compromised.

It is worth comparing this with the human side of the equation, because the myth has a companion claim: that human bites are more dangerous than dog bites. That, too, was once taught as fact. The older literature made human bites look extraordinarily dangerous, but later research showed the picture was biased by its focus on human bites to the hand that arrived late and already infected. A 1988 study in the *Annals of Emergency Medicine* and a 1995 study in the *Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology* both concluded that human bites outside the hand carry about the same infection risk as dog bites, and that overall only around 10 to 15 percent of human bites become infected. The human mouth is dangerous in its own way — it carries about 190 species of anaerobic bacteria alone, including organisms related to those that cause tetanus and botulism — but it is not uniquely dangerous, and it is not more dangerous than a dog's mouth in any general sense. Both mouths can hurt you. They just hurt you differently.

## Why Dog Kisses Usually Don't Make You Sick

If a dog's mouth is so full of unfamiliar germs, why doesn't every dog kiss end in illness? Because of the same fact that kills the myth: the two microbiomes barely overlap.

Most of the bacteria in your dog's mouth are not zoonotic, which means they cannot cause disease in humans. They are adapted to life in a dog, at a dog's body temperature, in a dog's oral environment, and they simply cannot establish themselves in a human body. That is why kissing your dog is, in practical terms, less risky than kissing another human. When you kiss another person, you swap germs that are perfectly evolved to thrive in a human body, including the cold viruses, streptococci, and other organisms that actually make people ill. When your dog licks you, you receive a large dose of germs that mostly cannot hurt you — and a small dose of ones that can.

This is the paradox at the heart of the myth, and it is the reason the myth feels true. Dog owners notice, correctly, that they do not get sick from their dogs' kisses. They conclude, incorrectly, that the dog's mouth must be clean. The correct conclusion is that the dog's mouth is full of germs that are incompatible with them. The American Kennel Club makes this point explicitly: most of the bacteria in a dog's mouth aren't zoonotic, so you probably won't catch a disease from a big slobbery kiss — but there are exceptions, and the exceptions matter. A dog that eats a raw diet is at increased risk of carrying salmonella. A dog that has just been scavenging, or raiding the litter box, is carrying whatever it just ate. And a lick on broken skin is a different event from a lick on an intact cheek.

Dogs also have another advantage over humans that has nothing to do with bacteria: they do not get cavities from sugar, they do not smoke, and they do not go days without brushing in quite the way some humans do. But their dental health is often worse than their owners realise. Most dogs over the age of three have some degree of periodontal disease, and the cavities in a dog's teeth can contain hundreds of different bacterial species. A mouth that smells bad — and many dog owners will recognise the smell — is not a clean mouth. It is a mouth full of bacteria digesting food debris and gum tissue, exactly like a human mouth with gum disease.

## The Verdict

**False.** A dog's mouth is not cleaner than a human's mouth.

The evidence is consistent across every line of inquiry. Both mouths carry hundreds of bacterial species in roughly similar numbers — more than 600 types in dogs, roughly 615 to 700 in humans. Only about 15 percent of those species are shared, which means the two mouths are different ecosystems, not ranked versions of the same thing. Veterinary dentists and microbiologists, from the University of Pennsylvania's School of Veterinary Medicine to the American Kennel Club, agree that the comparison itself is misguided: it is, in Dr. Colin Harvey's phrase, like comparing apples to oranges.

The myth survives because it contains a distorted version of a truth. Dog saliva does contain antibacterial compounds, and dogs licking their own wounds does help them heal. And most dog germs cannot infect humans, which is why dog kisses rarely make healthy people ill. But none of that makes a dog's mouth cleaner. A dog's mouth also carries *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, which can cause fatal sepsis in vulnerable people; *Pasteurella*, which infects more than half of infected dog-bite wounds; and, in raw-fed dogs, salmonella. About 4.5 million Americans are bitten by dogs each year, and about one in five of those bites becomes infected. No honest definition of "cleaner" survives those numbers.

The school science-fair experiments capture the ambiguity best. One found dogs cleaner by colony count, one found humans cleaner, and the most careful one concluded that dogs' mouths are cleaner in some ways and dirtier in others. The professional verdict, with the benefit of DNA sequencing, is simpler: different, not cleaner. The claim as it is usually stated is false.

## What to Do Instead

None of this means you should stop loving your dog, or even stop letting it lick you — it means you should do it with your eyes open. A few practical rules follow directly from the evidence.

First, keep dog licks away from open wounds, cuts, and scrapes, and away from your mouth, nose, and eyes. The risk from a lick on intact skin is low, but a lick on broken skin is how *Capnocytophaga* and *Pasteurella* get in. If your dog does lick a wound, wash it promptly with soap and water — the same first aid you would give any animal bite or scratch.

Second, be extra careful if you are in a higher-risk group. People with weakened immune systems, people without a spleen, people with heavy alcohol use, pregnant women, and very young children should avoid letting dogs lick their faces, and should be particularly careful around any bite or scratch, however small. If you develop fever, spreading redness, or severe pain after a dog bite, seek medical care quickly — *Pasteurella* infections can worsen within hours.

Third, take your dog's dental health seriously. Regular brushing with dog toothpaste, dental chews approved by the Veterinary Oral Health Council, and professional cleaning when your vet recommends it will reduce the bacterial load in your dog's mouth — and reduce the smell that tells you it is there. And think about diet: dogs fed raw meat are more likely to carry salmonella, which is one more reason many vets advise against raw feeding.

Finally, if your dog bites someone, or you are bitten, treat it as a medical event, not a trivial scratch. Wash the wound, apply pressure to stop bleeding, and get medical advice — especially for bites to the hands, face, or joints, and especially for children and older adults. Roughly 800,000 Americans seek medical care for dog bites each year. There is no shame in being one of them; there is only sense.

## The Last Word

The myth of the cleaner dog mouth has survived for thousands of years, from Egyptian temples to Greek healing shrines to medieval chronicles to the dog park, because it flatters a real emotion: the feeling that our dogs are pure in a way we are not. They are loyal, they are affectionate, and they love us without conditions. It is tempting to extend that purity to their biology.

But biology does not work that way. A dog's mouth is a wild, crowded, ancient ecosystem — full of organisms that keep the dog healthy and can make us ill, shaped by a diet and a lifestyle no human shares. It is not cleaner than yours. It is not dirtier than yours, either. It is different, and that difference is worth understanding on its own terms, because it is the difference between a kiss that is harmless and a lick on an open cut that is not. Your dog's love is unconditional. Its mouth is not. Treat both accordingly.

*Dogs do remarkable things with their noses — researchers have even studied whether <a href="/blog/can-dogs-smell-cancer-fact-check/">can dogs smell cancer</a> — and their mouths are just as specialised. The science is clear: a dog's mouth is not cleaner than a human's, only different. And while we are correcting the record on dogs, <a href="/blog/do-dogs-see-in-black-and-white-fact-check/">do dogs see in black and white</a>? That is another myth worth checking.*
