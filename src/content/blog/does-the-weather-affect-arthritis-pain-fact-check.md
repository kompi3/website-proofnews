Every family seems to have someone who can do it. A grandmother who rubs her knee at breakfast and announces that rain is coming. An uncle whose old football injury starts throbbing the day before a storm. A neighbour who swears the cold snap last winter was worse for her hands than any flare-up in years. For generations, this kind of weather forecasting by joint has been treated as folk wisdom — charming, probably exaggerated, and impossible to prove. Doctors, for the most part, were sceptical. Patients, for the most part, were certain.

Then researchers at the University of Manchester decided to settle the argument properly. Instead of asking a few dozen patients to remember how they felt last month, they handed smartphones to more than 13,000 people across the United Kingdom and tracked their pain, day by day, against the actual weather outside their front doors. The results, published in 2019, were the largest and most careful test the claim had ever received. And they landed somewhere neither side expected: the folk wisdom was real, but it was much smaller — and much more specific — than anyone had claimed.

So does the weather affect arthritis pain? The short answer is yes, for many people, a little. The longer answer is where it gets interesting, because the evidence splits in ways that explain why your grandmother and your doctor can both be right at the same time.

## The Claim That Would Not Go Away

The idea that weather changes joint pain is one of the oldest beliefs in medicine. Hippocrates wrote about it more than 2,000 years ago, noting that changes in the seasons seemed to bring changes in joint complaints. In the centuries since, the belief has survived every wave of medical scepticism, largely because the people who hold it are so consistent about it. Survey after survey has found that a majority of people with arthritis — often around two in three — say their symptoms change with the weather. Cold and damp are the usual villains. Falling pressure before a storm is the classic trigger. Warm, dry, settled weather is supposed to bring relief.

For a long time, the scientific literature could not keep up with that confidence. Studies were small, often involving a few dozen patients followed for a few weeks. They relied on people remembering how much pain they had been in, which is a notoriously unreliable way to measure anything. Some found links with temperature, some with pressure, some with humidity, and some found nothing at all. A doctor could look at that pile of contradictory results and reasonably conclude that the whole thing was a trick of memory: people remember the rainy days when their knees hurt and forget the rainy days when they did not.

That explanation — that the effect is a cognitive illusion — was the respectable sceptical position for decades. It was also, it turns out, partly wrong. The problem was never that the effect did not exist. The problem was that the studies were too small and too crude to see an effect that is real but modest, and that only shows up clearly when you watch thousands of people, every day, for months.

## The Smartphone Study That Changed the Conversation

The project that finally gave the question a fair hearing was called Cloudy with a Chance of Pain, run by researchers at the University of Manchester and funded by the charity Versus Arthritis. It launched in January 2016 as the world's first smartphone-based study of weather and pain, and it recruited more than 13,000 people from all 124 postcode areas of the United Kingdom, from Orkney in the far north to the Isles of Scilly in the far south. Participants had conditions including arthritis, fibromyalgia, migraine, and neuropathic pain.

The design was the key to its power. Using a smartphone app developed by the healthcare software company uMotif, participants recorded their symptoms every day, while the phone's GPS location was matched to local weather data. That removed the two biggest weaknesses of earlier research: nobody had to remember anything, and nobody had to guess what the weather had been. The study ran for 15 months and generated millions of data points.

The main analysis looked at 2,658 people who provided daily data on most days for around six months. The headline finding was clear. People with long-term pain were about 20 per cent more likely to suffer pain on days that were humid and windy with low atmospheric pressure. Humid days were the most likely to be painful; dry days were the least likely. Low pressure and higher wind speed were also linked to more painful days, though to a lesser extent than humidity. The combination — a damp, windy day with low pressure — was the worst of all.

Just as striking was what the study did not find. Despite many people believing that temperature drives their pain, there was no association with temperature when the figures were averaged across the whole population. Cold days that were also damp and windy could be more painful, but cold alone was not the culprit. Rainfall itself was not associated with pain either. That distinction matters beyond joints, too — cold weather has its own separate reputation for causing illness, and as we found in our fact-check of <a href="/blog/does-cold-weather-make-you-sick-fact-check/">Does Cold Weather Make You Sick?</a>, the evidence there is similarly mixed. It was the humidity, the pressure, and the wind that mattered — not the thermometer, and not whether it actually rained.

The study's lead author, Professor Will Dixon, put the size of the effect in plain terms that cut through the percentages. "This would mean that, if your chances of a painful day on an average weather day were 5 in 100, they would increase to 6 in 100 on a damp and windy day," he said. That is a real effect. It is also, by any honest measure, a small one — a nudge, not a shove.

Crucially, the researchers also tested the obvious alternative explanations. Mood has a strong association with pain, and bad weather can lower mood; bad weather can also keep people indoors and less active, and inactivity can stiffen joints. But the association between weather and pain remained even after mood and physical activity were taken into account. Whatever is going on, it is not simply that grey days make people sad and sedentary.

The findings were published in the journal npj Digital Medicine in 2019, and they have since become the reference point for the whole debate — the study everyone else has to argue with.

## The Jet Stream Told the Same Story

A follow-up analysis of the same mountain of data, led by Professor David Schultz from the University of Manchester's Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences and published in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, looked at the question the way a meteorologist would. Instead of testing one weather variable at a time, the team ranked every day in the study by the percentage of participants who reported a pain increase, then averaged the weather on the most painful days and compared it with the least painful days.

The contrast was dramatic. On the most painful days, 23 per cent of participants reported an increase in pain. On the least painful days, only 10 per cent did. The team then took the 45 days at the top of the ranking — the worst 10 per cent of all study days — and examined the weather patterns behind them.

On the most painful days, the jet stream was aimed directly at the United Kingdom, with below-normal pressure over the country. Humidity and the precipitation rate were both above normal, and winds were stronger. On the least painful days, the jet stream tended to blow north of the UK, bringing above-normal pressure, below-normal humidity and precipitation, and weaker winds. It was the first time anyone had shown the large-scale weather patterns behind painful days versus pain-free days — and the patterns matched the original findings exactly: damp, blustery, low-pressure weather, not cold weather as such.

The work was strong enough that Professor Schultz received the European Meteorological Society's Tromp Foundation award for outstanding achievement in biometeorology in 2020. It also mattered because it answered a subtle objection to the original study. If the effect had been an artefact of how the data was analysed, a completely different analytical approach — ranking days and averaging weather maps — would be unlikely to reproduce it so cleanly. It did.

## What Happens When You Pool Every Study Together

One big study, however impressive, is still one study. The next question is what happens when you combine all the research on osteoarthritis and weather into a single analysis. That is what a team did in a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Annals of Medicine in 2023, searching the Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science for every study up to September 2022.

Fourteen studies made it into the qualitative review, and the consistency was striking: 13 of the 14 reported that weather factors in general were associated with osteoarthritis pain. When the researchers pooled the numbers, barometric pressure was positively related to pain, with a summary correlation of 0.35; relative humidity was also positively related, though more weakly, at 0.086; and temperature was negatively related, at minus 0.36 — meaning colder conditions were associated with more pain. That last result is interesting because it partly conflicts with the Manchester study, which found no temperature effect when averaged across its population. The difference is a reminder that "arthritis" is not one condition and "temperature" does not act alone: in many of the smaller studies, cold arrived together with damp and low pressure, and the pooled result may be capturing that combination.

A narrative review published in the journal Medicina in 2026, synthesising 15 studies from 2010 to May 2024, reached a similar middle ground. Temperature showed the most consistent pattern across the literature — colder and more variable conditions were linked to increased pain and stiffness, while warmer, stable conditions favoured mobility. Humidity and barometric pressure fluctuations showed modest effects, particularly in cool and damp conditions. Precipitation, sunlight, and wind had minimal or inconsistent associations on their own. The review's conclusion was carefully worded and worth quoting in spirit: weather acts as a contextual modifier rather than a primary determinant of osteoarthritis symptoms. It turns the volume up or down. It does not write the song.

That framing helps explain why the debate has run so hot for so long. If you study a hundred people with osteoarthritis and ask whether the weather matters, the honest answer is that it matters a bit, for some of them, some of the time — which is exactly the kind of answer that small, short studies will sometimes detect and sometimes miss.

## The Evidence That Pushes Back

But the story does not end with a comfortable consensus, because some of the most rigorous research points the other way — and a fair fact-check has to give that evidence its full weight.

The strongest challenge came from a systematic review with meta-analysis of case-crossover studies, published in Seminars in Arthritis and Rheumatism in 2024. Case-crossover studies are a particularly tough test: instead of comparing different people with each other, they compare the same person on days when they had a pain flare with days when they did not, which strips out all the differences between individuals that can muddy the results. The review pooled 11 such studies covering 15,315 participants, 28,010 pain events, and more than 100,000 control periods, across seven musculoskeletal conditions.

The result was a flat no. The pooled analyses showed no association between relative humidity, air pressure, temperature, or precipitation and the risk of rheumatoid arthritis, knee pain, or low back pain. The authors' interpretation was blunt: despite anecdotal reports from patients, changes in weather factors do not seem to be risk factors for rheumatoid arthritis, knee, hip, or low back pain.

There was one exception, and it was a large one. High temperatures combined with low humidity were associated with increased pain, redness, and joint swelling in people with gout, with an odds ratio of 2.04 — roughly a doubling of the risk of a flare. That makes biological sense, because heat and low humidity promote dehydration, and dehydration is a known trigger for the uric acid crystals that cause gout. But it is the opposite weather pattern from the one arthritis patients usually blame: hot and dry, not cold and damp.

A second sceptical strand comes from research on rheumatoid arthritis specifically. A systematic review by Smedslund and Hagen, published in 2011 under the telling title "Does rain really cause pain?", pooled nine studies and found no relationship at all between weather and reported pain when the data were combined across patients. At the group level, the effect vanished entirely. But when two of the included studies analysed people individually, a minority did look sensitive: 16 per cent and 11 per cent of participants had increased pain with temperature changes, and 6 per cent and 11 per cent were sensitive to changes in relative humidity. Some of those people felt worse when it got warmer; others felt worse when it got colder. Averaged together, they cancelled each other out.

That cancelling-out effect may be the single most important clue in the whole debate. If a tenth of patients are worse in the cold, a tenth are worse in the heat, and the rest are unaffected, then a study that averages everyone together will conclude that the weather does nothing — while every one of those sensitive patients is telling the truth about their own joints.

## Why Some People Feel It and Others Do Not

The pattern that keeps emerging is that weather sensitivity is real, individual, and unevenly distributed. A few smaller studies illustrate that unevenness vividly.

In 1990, researchers Guedj and Weinberger followed 62 rheumatic patients for a month — 16 with rheumatoid arthritis, 24 with osteoarthritis, 11 with inflammatory arthritis, and 11 with fibromyalgia — comparing daily symptoms with daily weather. In most patients, weather changes increased symptoms, but the details differed by condition: pain was affected by barometric pressure and temperature in rheumatoid arthritis, by temperature, rain, and barometric pressure in osteoarthritis, and by barometric pressure in fibromyalgia. Women were more sensitive than men, 62 per cent versus 37 per cent.

A 2012 study of 53 patients with end-stage osteoarthritis of the hip, published in International Orthopaedics, asked participants to rate their pain on a visual scale every day for a month. Pain levels increased as a function of the absolute change in atmospheric pressure from one day to the next — not whether pressure was high or low, but how much it had moved. Precipitation and temperature showed no influence at all. That finding points to an important nuance: for some joints, it may be the change in the weather, rather than the weather itself, that hurts.

An earlier study from 2007, tracking 200 people with osteoarthritis of the knee for three months, found that barometric pressure and temperature changes were each independently associated with pain severity. Three months, 200 people — modest by modern standards, but consistent with the bigger picture: pressure moves, pain follows, and the effect is measurable even in small groups when you measure daily.

None of these studies is decisive on its own. Together, they sketch a plausible portrait. A minority of people with arthritis are genuinely weather-sensitive. Their sensitivity is personal — one person flares when pressure falls, another when it rises, another in cold, another in heat. The sensitive minority is large enough to show up clearly in a study of 13,000 people, but small enough to disappear when a study averages a hundred people together, especially if the sensitive ones pull in opposite directions.

There is also a humbling possibility that runs through the literature: part of what people feel may not be the weather acting on their joints at all, but the weather acting on everything around their joints. Cold, damp days change behaviour — people move less, sit longer, sleep worse, and tense up against the chill. The Manchester researchers controlled for mood and physical activity and the effect survived, which argues against that explanation being the whole story. But in everyday life, the weather, the mood, the inactivity, and the pain all arrive together, and separating them is the work of statistics, not of feeling.

## Why Would the Weather Hurt a Joint at All?

If the effect is real, what causes it? Here the science is honest about its limits: nobody knows for certain. The leading hypothesis is the one your grandmother probably offered without knowing it was a hypothesis. When barometric pressure falls before a storm, the atmosphere presses less hard against the body, and the tissues in and around a joint can expand very slightly. In a healthy joint, that expansion means nothing. In a joint where cartilage has worn thin and the surrounding tissue is already inflamed and crowded, that tiny expansion could press on nearby nerves and register as an ache. It is a neat, mechanical explanation — and, as the science writers who cover it are careful to add, it remains unproven.

Other mechanisms are plausible but equally unconfirmed. Cold may make the fluid inside joints thicker and less lubricating, the way cold oil thickens in an engine. Nerve endings in damaged joints may simply be more excitable, so any environmental change registers more strongly. Changes in humidity could affect the tissues' water content. None of these ideas has been tested in a way that would settle the matter, because testing them would require measuring pressure, temperature, and humidity inside a living human joint while the weather changes outside — an experiment nobody has figured out how to run.

The honest summary is that the epidemiology is ahead of the physiology. We have large-scale evidence that humid, windy, low-pressure days coincide with more pain, and we do not yet have a proven chain of cause and effect to explain it. That gap is normal in medicine — aspirin was used for decades before anyone understood how it worked — but it is also the reason a responsible doctor will say the effect is "associated with" the weather rather than "caused by" it. Meanwhile, some of the therapies marketed for joint pain have been tested far more rigorously than the weather has — including <a href="/blog/does-red-light-therapy-actually-work-fact-check/">Does Red Light Therapy Actually Work?</a>, where 204 trials gave a more modest answer than the advertisements.

## What the Claim Gets Wrong

The folk version of this claim has three exaggerations built into it, and they are worth naming, because they are the reason the verdict here is "mostly true" rather than "true."

First, the folk version says rain. The evidence says humidity and pressure. Rainfall itself showed no association with pain in the Manchester study, and the 2026 review found precipitation had minimal or inconsistent associations. A dry, humid, windy day with falling pressure can be worse for joints than a day of steady rain under high pressure. Your grandmother's knee is not a rain gauge; it is, if anything, a barometer and a hygrometer.

Second, the folk version says cold. The evidence says it depends on the person. The Manchester study found no temperature effect at all when averaged across its population. The pooled meta-analysis found colder conditions associated with more pain, but the narrative review found that some people flare in heat instead. Cold plus damp plus wind is a bad combination; cold alone, on a dry, settled, high-pressure day, may be perfectly fine. If you moved to a warm climate expecting your arthritis to vanish, the evidence suggests you would be disappointed — and indeed, studies of people who move for their arthritis have found the benefit, if any, is small and fades as the body adjusts.

Third, the folk version implies certainty and scale: storm coming, pain guaranteed, pain severe. The evidence says probability and modesty: a damp, windy day raises the chance of a painful day from roughly 5 in 100 to roughly 6 in 100. For a person who is weather-sensitive, that shift is real and noticeable. For a person who is not, it is invisible. And even for the sensitive, the weather is one influence among many — the state of the joint itself, activity, sleep, stress, and medication matter far more than the forecast.

## What Actually Helps on a Bad-Weather Day

None of this means people with arthritis should ignore the forecast. If you are one of the people whose joints track the weather, the forecast is genuinely useful information — just useful in the way a weather forecast is useful, as a heads-up rather than a diagnosis.

The advice that follows from the research is unglamorous and practical. Keep moving on damp, windy days, even if that means moving indoors, because inactivity stiffens joints more reliably than any cold front. Keep warm, particularly the joints that bother you, since warmth improves blood flow and keeps joint fluid moving. Cold, on the other hand, is sometimes deliberately applied to sore tissue — see our fact-check of <a href="/blog/do-ice-baths-help-muscle-recovery-fact-check/">Do Ice Baths Help Muscle Recovery?</a> for what the trials actually show about cold and recovery. Plan demanding activity for the settled, high-pressure days when your joints are likely to cooperate, and keep the low-pressure days lighter. And treat the forecast as one input among many: if a storm is coming and your knee is aching, it is worth remembering that your knee may be responding to the pressure drop — and also worth remembering that a poor night's sleep or a long day sitting still could be doing the same thing.

One related point, raised by patient guides on the subject, is worth passing on for readers in northern climates. In the UK, sunlight is not strong enough from about October to early March for the skin to make enough vitamin D, and the NHS advises everyone to consider a daily vitamin D supplement of 10 micrograms during autumn and winter. That has nothing to do with weather sensitivity directly, but winter is when both the damp weather and the vitamin D gap arrive together, and bone health matters to everyone with arthritis. As with any supplement, it is worth discussing with a doctor or pharmacist rather than starting on the basis of an article.

## How the Studies Compare

The debate looks confusing until the studies are laid side by side. Then a pattern appears: the bigger and longer the study, and the more it measures people day by day, the more likely it is to find a small, real effect — while studies that average everyone together, or that test one condition in isolation, are more likely to find nothing.

| Study | People | Duration | What it found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloudy with a Chance of Pain, Manchester (2019) | 13,000+ recruited; 2,658 analysed | 15 months | Humid, windy, low-pressure days raised pain risk ~20%; humidity strongest; no temperature or rainfall link |
| Schultz et al., Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society | Same 13,000-person cohort | 15 months | Most painful days: 23% reported more pain vs 10% on least painful days; jet stream aimed at UK, low pressure, high humidity |
| Systematic review & meta-analysis, Annals of Medicine (2023) | 14 studies pooled | Studies to Sep 2022 | 13 of 14 studies linked weather to osteoarthritis pain; pressure r = 0.35, humidity r = 0.086, temperature r = −0.36 |
| Case-crossover meta-analysis, Seminars in Arthritis and Rheumatism (2024) | 15,315 people; 28,010 pain events | Studies to Sep 2023 | No link between humidity, pressure, temperature, or rain and rheumatoid arthritis, knee, or low back pain; gout flares doubled in hot, dry weather (OR 2.04) |
| Smedslund & Hagen review, European Journal of Pain (2011) | 9 studies (rheumatoid arthritis) | Studies to 2011 | No link at group level; a minority of individuals sensitive — 11–16% to temperature, 6–11% to humidity |
| Guedj & Weinberger (1990) | 62 rheumatic patients | 1 month | Weather changes raised symptoms in most patients; women more sensitive (62% vs 37%) |
| Brennan et al., International Orthopaedics (2012) | 53 end-stage hip osteoarthritis patients | 1 month | Pain rose with day-to-day changes in atmospheric pressure; rain and temperature had no effect |

Read down the table and the story is consistent: the effect is real, it is modest, it is driven by humidity and pressure more than by temperature or rain, and it is concentrated in a sensitive minority rather than spread evenly across everyone with arthritis. The studies that found nothing were not wrong — they were looking at averages, and averages wash out a minority effect.

It is also worth noting what the table leaves out. No study in this set found that weather damages joints, makes arthritis progress faster, or causes arthritis in the first place. The claim is about pain on a given day, not about the disease itself. That distinction matters, because it is the difference between "the weather makes my knee hurt today" and "the weather is ruining my knee," and only the first is supported.

## The Verdict

**Mostly True.** The claim that the weather affects arthritis pain is supported by the best evidence available — but only in the careful form, not the folk form.

The largest study ever conducted on the question, tracking more than 13,000 people with chronic pain through a smartphone app for 15 months, found that humid, windy days with low atmospheric pressure made pain about 20 per cent more likely, with humidity the strongest factor. A 2023 meta-analysis of 14 studies found that 13 of them linked weather conditions to osteoarthritis pain. And a follow-up analysis that mapped the weather on the most painful days found the jet stream aimed squarely at the country, with low pressure and above-normal humidity — a pattern no one had shown before.

But the claim overreaches in three ways. The folk version blames rain and cold; the evidence blames humidity, pressure, and wind, with temperature showing no effect at all in the biggest study. The folk version implies the effect is large and universal; the evidence says it is small — a painful day becoming roughly 6-in-100 likely instead of 5-in-100 — and concentrated in a sensitive minority, with some people flaring in heat rather than cold. And the most rigorous test of all, a 2024 meta-analysis of case-crossover studies covering more than 15,000 people, found no link between weather and rheumatoid arthritis, knee, or low back pain at all — a result that should keep everyone honest about how much the weather can really explain.

So your grandmother's knee is not a myth. It is a modestly accurate instrument, measuring the wrong variables, in a minority of people, on the damp and blustery days when the pressure is falling. That is less dramatic than the old saying — and, for once, more interesting. The next time someone rubs a joint and predicts rain, the fair response is not to scoff and not to reach for an umbrella. It is to check the humidity, check the pressure, and remember that the joint might be right — just not in the way either of you thought.

The weather will not fix your arthritis, and it will not ruin your joints. But on a damp, windy day with the pressure falling, it might just make them ache a little more. And after a century of doctors dismissing that observation, the largest study ever run on the subject says the people who felt it were, mostly, right all along.
