Scroll through any wellness feed and you will meet the cold shower. A creator stands under an icy stream at dawn, gasping, grinning, and telling you that thirty seconds of discomfort will "supercharge" your immune system, "flush toxins," and keep you from ever catching a cold again. The claim has travelled from 19th-century hydrotherapy clinics to modern biohacking podcasts, and it now arrives with a scientific-looking number attached: a study of more than 3,000 people supposedly proved it.

The number is real. The study is real. But what it proved is considerably narrower — and considerably stranger — than the version doing the rounds on social media. The people who finished their showers with cold water did take fewer days off work sick. They did not, however, get sick less often. And when researchers pooled every good trial of cold-water immersion in a 2025 meta-analysis, they found no measurable effect on immune function at all.

This is a claim with a genuine grain of evidence inside it, wrapped in a much larger exaggeration. Here is what the trials actually measured, what the famous Wim Hof study did and did not show, and why "boosting your immune system" is a phrase that should make you suspicious before you even turn the tap.

## The claim: a stronger immune system, on tap

The modern version of the claim goes roughly like this: regular cold showers stimulate your immune system, raise your white blood cell count, and make you resistant to colds and flu. Some versions add that cold water "activates" brown fat, "resets" the nervous system, and trains the body to handle stress. The supposed mechanism is plausible-sounding — a shock of cold triggers adrenaline and noradrenaline, mobilises immune cells, and over time teaches the body to respond more efficiently.

It is worth pausing on the phrase itself. "Boost your immune system" sounds precise, but immunologists will tell you it is almost meaningless as stated. The immune system is not a single dial you turn up. It is a network of cells, signals, and organs that must be balanced, not maximised: an overactive immune response is what drives allergies and autoimmune disease. A temporary rise in circulating white blood cells — which cold exposure does cause — is not the same thing as lasting protection against infection. It is the difference between calling more police officers onto the street for an hour and actually reducing crime over a year.

That distinction runs through every study in this story. Cold water does measurable things to the body. Whether those things add up to fewer infections is a different question, and it is the one the evidence answers most cautiously.

## The biggest trial: 3,018 people, 29% fewer sick days — and no fewer illnesses

The single most cited piece of evidence for cold showers is a randomised controlled trial published in PLOS ONE in 2016 by Geert Buijze and colleagues in the Netherlands. It is, by the standards of this field, a big and well-run study, and it is the reason the claim cannot simply be dismissed.

Between January and March 2015, the researchers recruited 3,018 adults aged 18 to 65 who had no severe comorbidity and no routine experience of cold showering. Participants were randomised into four groups: finish the daily shower with 30, 60, or 90 seconds of cold water at around 10°C, or continue showering warm as normal. The cold finish ran for 30 consecutive days, followed by 60 days in which the intervention groups could shower cold at their own discretion. The primary outcome was illness days and related sickness absence from work over 90 days.

The headline result is the one you have probably seen quoted: a 29% reduction in sickness absence for the cold-shower groups compared with the control group, an incidence rate ratio of 0.71 with a p-value of 0.003. Nearly four in five participants — 79% — completed the full 30-day protocol, and no serious adverse events were reported. The effect did not depend on dose: 30 seconds worked as well as 90. And participants who combined cold showers with regular physical activity took 54% fewer sick days than those who did neither.

So far, so impressive. Here is the part that rarely makes it into the social media version. The trial found no significant effect on illness days themselves. The cold-shower groups took fewer days off work, but they were not sick on fewer days. The authors' own abstract states the result plainly: "A routine (hot-to-) cold shower resulted in a statistical reduction of self-reported sickness absence but not illness days in adults without severe comorbidity."

Read that again, because the whole claim turns on it. People in the cold-shower groups felt well enough to go to work on days when the control group stayed home — or, to put it more carefully, they reported their illness differently. The authors interpreted this as an effect on perceived illness severity or quality of life rather than a direct immune effect. The trial did not measure a single immune biomarker: no white blood cell counts, no antibody levels, no cytokines. It measured days off work, self-reported, in an unblinded trial where everyone knew which group they were in.

That does not make the result worthless. Fewer sick days is a real outcome that matters to real people, and a randomised trial of 3,000 people is not something to wave away. But it is not evidence that cold showers "boost the immune system." It is evidence that they changed how people experienced — or at least how they reported — being unwell.

There is also a newer wrinkle. In September 2026, an independent re-analysis of the trial's published data argued that the famous 29% figure is fragile: it rests, the re-analysis says, on a handful of very long absences that happened to fall in the control group — six of the eight participants who reported thirty or more sick days were controls, in an arm holding less than a quarter of the sample. Cap those long absences or measure absence over the intervention period alone, and the effect shrinks toward nothing. The re-analysis is not peer-reviewed, and it does not erase the trial, but it is a reminder that a single striking percentage can be carried by a very small number of people. The evidence for cold showers is thinner than the headline suggests even before you ask what it proves about immunity.

## The Wim Hof study: a real result, a small group, and breathing in the driving seat

The second study everyone cites is more dramatic still. In 2014, researchers at Radboud University Medical Centre in the Netherlands published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences that seemed to overturn a textbook assumption: that the autonomic nervous system and the innate immune response cannot be voluntarily controlled.

The team, led by Matthijs Kox, recruited 24 healthy young men and randomised them into two groups of 12. One group spent ten days training in the Wim Hof Method — breathing exercises, meditation, and cold exposure including immersion in ice-cold water — under Wim Hof himself. The other group received no training. Then every participant was injected intravenously with a bacterial endotoxin, a fragment of E. coli cell wall that reliably triggers fever, flu-like symptoms, and a sharp inflammatory response.

The trained group responded differently. Their adrenaline levels surged far higher than the controls'. Their anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 rose about 200% higher. Levels of three pro-inflammatory cytokines — TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-8 — were roughly 50% lower. And their peak flu-like symptoms were 56% milder. It was the first controlled demonstration that people could voluntarily influence their innate immune response, and it made headlines around the world.

Everything about that paragraph is true. Everything about the way it is usually quoted is misleading, because the quotation almost always leaves out the limits — and the limits are doing most of the work.

First, the study was tiny: twelve people per group, all young healthy men, at a single centre, unblinded. Second, it bundled three interventions together — breathing, meditation, and cold — so it could not say which one mattered. A follow-up study by the same research group in 2022, designed specifically to pull the components apart, found that the breathing exercises alone were sufficient to raise adrenaline and blunt the inflammatory response. The cold, in other words, may have been a passenger in the study most often used to sell cold exposure.

Third, and most importantly, the study measured a response to an injected toxin in a laboratory. It did not measure whether anyone caught fewer colds, fought off a real infection faster, or stayed healthier over months. Blunting a lab-induced inflammatory spike for a few hours is not the same as being more resistant to disease — and in some contexts, dampening inflammation is not even desirable, since inflammation is part of how the body fights infection. No trial has shown that the Wim Hof Method prevents infections, treats autoimmune disease, or replaces medical care.

There is also a safety warning that belongs in any honest account of this method. The breathing technique involves repeated deep breaths followed by holding the breath on empty lungs, and it can cause fainting. It must never be done in or near water, while driving, or while standing. People have drowned doing breath-hold exercises in water. The cold is not the dangerous part of that pairing; the breathing is.

## What the 2025 meta-analysis found: no measurable immune effect

If the Buijze trial and the Kox study are the two pillars of the claim, the 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis by Cain and colleagues, published in PLOS ONE, is the building inspector. It gathered every randomised trial of cold-water immersion in healthy adults — eleven studies, 3,177 participants in total, with water at 15°C or colder for at least 30 seconds, delivered as baths or showers — and pooled their results.

On immune function specifically, the meta-analysis found no significant effect, either immediately after exposure or one hour later. The standardised mean differences were −0.16 immediately and −0.18 at one hour, both statistically indistinguishable from zero. In other words, when you combine all the controlled evidence, cold-water immersion does not measurably change immune function in the short term.

What it did find is a pattern that fits the rest of this story. Inflammation markers rose significantly right after immersion and one hour later — an acute stress response, not a calming one. Stress levels fell significantly twelve hours after exposure, but not at any other time point measured. Sleep quality and quality of life improved. Mood did not. The authors' conclusion was careful: cold-water immersion delivers time-dependent effects on inflammation, stress, immunity, sleep quality, and quality of life — but the evidence base is constrained by few randomised trials, small samples, and a lack of diversity in study populations.

A separate 2022 review in the International Journal of Circumpolar Health reached a similar place: the health effects of voluntary cold-water exposure remain a subject of debate, with promising signals and thin proof in roughly equal measure. Winter swimmers, the review noted, report around 40% fewer upper respiratory infections than comparable non-swimmers — but that is observational data from people who choose to swim in freezing water, exercise outdoors, and generally live active lives. It cannot separate the cold from everything else about them.

## The short-lived spike: what cold water does to your blood

None of this means cold water does nothing to the immune system. It does something — it just does not last, and nobody has shown that it protects you.

Within an hour of cold exposure, studies consistently show a transient rise in circulating white blood cells: neutrophils, lymphocytes, and natural killer cells all increase, a pattern called leukocytosis. It is the same pattern the body produces during short-term stress or exercise, and it is driven largely by stress hormones such as adrenaline and noradrenaline pushing cells out of storage and into the bloodstream. Natural killer cell activity peaks within about an hour, then returns toward baseline.

The key word is transient. A temporary redistribution of immune cells is not an upgrade to the immune system; the cells were already there. Nothing in the controlled evidence shows that the spike leaves you better defended a day later, a week later, or across a winter. And there is a counterpoint the wellness version never mentions: laboratory research suggests cold can suppress the antiviral defences in the nose, where colds actually begin, and experts advise against cold plunging while you are already ill, when the body needs its resources for recovery rather than for coping with an extra stressor.

This is the honest shape of the evidence. Cold exposure is a real physiological stressor with real, measurable, short-lived effects on immune cells and stress hormones. The leap from those effects to "you will get sick less" has not been made by any controlled study. The one large trial that tried found fewer sick days taken, not fewer illnesses — and even that result is now contested on its own data.

## Where the idea came from

The cold shower is not a new invention of the wellness industry. Roman baths ended with a plunge into the frigidarium, the cold room, and cold bathing has been prescribed in various forms for centuries. In the 19th century, the Bavarian priest Sebastian Kneipp built a whole hydrotherapy movement around cold water, walking barefoot in dew and plunging into cold streams, and his name still adorns spa towns across Germany.

The modern version owes more to one man: Wim Hof, the Dutch extreme athlete known as "the Iceman," who spent decades performing cold-endurance feats and promoting a method built on breathing, cold exposure, and commitment. The 2014 PNAS study gave his method a scientific calling card, and the method's popularity carried the cold shower into mainstream wellness culture. From there, the claim simplified at each retelling: a small lab study about blunting an injected toxin's effects became "cold boosts immunity," and a Dutch trial about sick days became "cold showers stop you getting sick."

The timeline matters because it shows how a claim can be old, popular, and partially studied all at once — and still be wrong in the form most people hear it. Longevity is not evidence. Neither is a good story.

## The risks: cold shock is real, and it is fast

A fair fact-check has to weigh the downside as well, and here the evidence is clearer than it is for the benefits. Sudden cold water triggers the cold shock response: a gasp reflex, rapid breathing, a spike in heart rate and blood pressure, and a surge of stress hormones. The response peaks in the first three minutes of immersion and is the main reason people drown in cold water — not hypothermia, which takes far longer, but the shock itself.

Blood pressure can jump by 20 to 40 mmHg systolic on immersion. For a healthy adult taking a brief cold shower, that is usually manageable; a shower, unlike a plunge, hits the body in stages and allows some adjustment. For someone with cardiovascular disease, a heart rhythm disorder, uncontrolled high blood pressure, or a history of heart attack or stroke, the same spike can be dangerous. Harvard Health advises against cold plunging for anyone with cardiovascular disease, and Cleveland Clinic lists high blood pressure, diabetes, pregnancy, peripheral neuropathy, and poor circulation as reasons to ask a clinician first. People with cold urticaria — an allergy to cold that can cause hives and, rarely, anaphylaxis — should avoid cold exposure altogether.

The practical guidance from clinicians is consistent and undramatic: start with cool rather than icy water, keep exposures short, never submerge your head, never do breath-hold exercises in or near water, and get out if you feel dizzy, faint, or unwell. A cold shower is a low-risk habit for most healthy adults. An ice bath is a bigger physiological event and deserves more caution. Neither is a substitute for sleep, vaccination, hand-washing, or any of the unglamorous things that actually keep people from getting sick.

## How this compares with other cold claims

Cold showers do not exist in isolation. The same logic — cold as a health tool — shows up in claims about ice baths for recovery, drinking ice water to burn calories, and winter swimming for longevity. The pattern across them is strikingly consistent: a real short-term physiological response, a plausible mechanism, and a large gap between what is measured and what is promised. ## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Cold showers are not a proven way to boost your immune system, and the claim as it is usually sold — that a daily blast of cold water will strengthen your immunity and keep colds away — goes well beyond the evidence.

What the evidence does support is narrower and more interesting. The largest randomised trial, Buijze and colleagues in 2016 with 3,018 participants, found that people who finished their showers with 30 to 90 seconds of cold water took 29% fewer sick days from work — but they were not sick on fewer days, and the trial measured no immune markers at all. A 2026 re-analysis of the trial's data argues even that headline figure is fragile. The famous 2014 Wim Hof study did show trained volunteers blunting their inflammatory response to an injected toxin, but it involved 24 young men, and a 2022 follow-up found the breathing — not the cold — drove the effect. And a 2025 meta-analysis of eleven trials and 3,177 participants found no significant effect of cold-water immersion on immune function.

None of that makes cold showers useless. People who take them often report feeling more alert, sleeping better, and coping with stress more easily — and the meta-analysis supports the sleep and quality-of-life parts of that. If you enjoy a cold finish to your shower and you are healthy, there is little reason to stop. But if you are doing it because someone promised you a stronger immune system, you have been sold a bigger claim than the science can carry. The honest version is shorter and less exciting: cold showers may help you feel better on sick days. They have not been shown to stop you getting sick.
