It is one of the oldest pieces of health advice still circulating on the internet: feel the first sting of a urinary tract infection, reach for the cranberry juice. The claim turns up everywhere — in family group chats, in wellness blogs, in the supermarket aisle where bottles of cranberry cocktail sit beside the orange juice, and in thousands of social media posts that present a glass of red juice as a natural antibiotic. Some versions of the claim are modest, promising only that cranberry might help keep infections away. Others are bolder, suggesting that drinking cranberry juice can flush out an infection that has already started, no doctor required.

The claim has survived for decades because it contains a real grain of science. Cranberries do contain compounds that interfere with the bacteria behind most urinary tract infections, and the best available review of the evidence — a 2023 Cochrane analysis of 50 randomised trials covering 8,857 people — found that cranberry products probably do reduce repeat infections in the women who get them most often. But the same body of evidence also draws a hard line that the viral version of the claim ignores: cranberry is a prevention aid for a narrow group of people, not a treatment for anyone, and the sweetened juice cocktail most people actually buy is not the product the trials tested. The gap between what the research shows and what the claim promises is exactly what makes this one misleading.

## The Claim

The viral claim comes in two versions, and they are often blurred together. The first version says cranberry juice can prevent urinary tract infections — drink it regularly and you will get fewer infections. The second, stronger version says cranberry juice can treat a urinary tract infection that has already begun — drink enough of it and the burning, the urgency and the infection itself will clear up without antibiotics.

Both versions travel well because urinary tract infections, or UTIs, are miserable and recurring. A UTI is usually defined as recurrent when a person has two infections within six months or three within a year, and women who fall into that pattern often spend years cycling between infections and antibiotic courses. Anything that promises to break that cycle without another prescription is going to be shared. The claim is also helped by the fact that cranberry has a long folk history: Cochrane notes that cranberries, as juice, tablets or capsules, have been used for many years to prevent urinary tract infections, long before the first rigorous trial was run.

The problem is that the two versions of the claim have very different relationships with the evidence. One of them is partly supported. The other has essentially no evidence behind it at all. And the product most people picture when they hear "cranberry juice" — the sweet red cocktail in the supermarket — is not the product that produced the positive results.

## What Cranberry Is Supposed to Do

The science behind the claim centres on a group of compounds called proanthocyanidins, usually shortened to PACs. Cranberries contain a particular type of PAC, known as A-type proanthocyanidins, which can stop certain E. coli bacteria from sticking to the cells that line the urinary tract. Most urinary tract infections are caused by E. coli that travel from the gut to the bladder, and if the bacteria cannot anchor themselves to the bladder wall, the theory goes, they are flushed out with the urine before an infection can take hold.

Laboratory studies support the mechanism. In lab tests, cranberry PACs stopped p-fimbriated E. coli — the strains equipped with tiny hair-like structures that grip the bladder lining — from attaching to cell surfaces like those lining the urinary tract. The mechanism is plausible, specific and testable, which is more than can be said for most folk remedies. It is also the reason the claim has been taken seriously by researchers for decades rather than dismissed outright.

But a plausible mechanism is not the same as a proven effect in people. Plenty of substances do something interesting in a petri dish and nothing at all in a human bladder. Whether cranberry products actually prevent infections could only be answered by trials in which real people took cranberry or a placebo and researchers counted who got sick. That is what the Cochrane review did — and its answer changed over time, which is the first clue that this story is more complicated than the social media version.

## The Biggest Review: 50 Trials, 8,857 People

The most rigorous look at this question is the Cochrane systematic review updated in 2023, led by Gabrielle Williams and colleagues. Cochrane reviews are generally considered the highest tier of evidence synthesis: they gather every randomised controlled trial they can find, assess each one for bias, and pool the results. This one pooled 50 randomised trials with 8,857 participants, comparing cranberry products — juice, tablets or capsules — against a placebo or no treatment.

The headline finding was positive, but carefully qualified. Across the trials that could be combined, cranberry products reduced the risk of a symptomatic, culture-confirmed urinary tract infection by about 30 per cent overall, a risk ratio of 0.70 with moderate certainty. In other words, the reviewers were fairly confident the effect is real, but not certain, and the size of the benefit was modest rather than dramatic. In absolute terms, the benefit worked out to roughly 6 fewer infections per 100 women treated — from about 24 infections per 100 women down to about 18.

Crucially, the benefit was not spread evenly. It showed up clearly in some groups and not at all in others, and that pattern is the heart of the story:

| Group studied | Trials and people | Risk ratio | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Women with recurrent UTIs | 8 trials, 1,555 women | 0.74 | About 26% fewer infections |
| Children with UTIs | 5 trials, 504 children | 0.46 | About 54% fewer infections |
| People at risk after a bladder procedure | 6 trials, 1,434 people | 0.47 | About 53% fewer infections |
| Older adults in care homes | Several trials | 0.93 | Little or no benefit |
| Pregnant women | Several trials | 1.06 | No benefit |
| Adults whose bladder does not empty fully | Several trials | 0.97 | Little or no benefit |
| All groups combined | 50 trials, 8,857 people | 0.70 | About 30% fewer infections |

The pattern matters because the viral claim is aimed at everyone, while the evidence is aimed at a specific group: women who keep getting infections, children prone to them, and people at risk after a medical procedure involving the bladder. For older adults living in care facilities, for pregnant women, and for people whose bladder does not empty properly because of a nerve or muscle disorder, the same review found little or no benefit. The data in those latter groups came from small studies with considerable uncertainty, so the door is not fully closed — but there is no positive case to make there either.

There is also an important caveat hiding inside the headline number. The 26 per cent reduction for women with recurrent infections is real but neither large nor tight: the upper end of the confidence interval sits at 0.99, a hair away from no effect at all, and six of the eight trials in women leaned toward cranberry while their individual intervals often crossed the line of no effect. Only about half of the included studies were judged relatively free of significant bias. This is not a slam-dunk result. It is a modest signal that survives careful scrutiny — which is genuinely more than most supplements can claim, and genuinely less than the advertisements suggest.

Side effects were mild. Few people reported any, with tummy pain the most common, and cranberry products were associated with a slight increase in gastrointestinal complaints. Nobody in the trials appears to have been seriously harmed by cranberry. But the review also found that there is currently no established regimen for what PAC dose to use, and no formal regulation by health authorities of cranberry products — a problem we will come back to, because it means the bottle on the shelf may not contain what the trials tested.

## The Flip: Why the Answer Changed

One of the most instructive parts of this story is that the same review reached the opposite conclusion eleven years earlier. Cochrane's 2012 review of 24 studies, covering 4,473 participants, found no statistically significant benefit from cranberry products. For years, that was the standard answer: cranberry does not work, the folk remedy is a myth, move on.

The 2023 update reversed that verdict — not because anyone changed their mind, but because the evidence base doubled. Fifty trials instead of twenty-four, nearly 9,000 participants instead of fewer than 4,500, and several newer, larger, better-designed trials that the earlier review could not include. A 2017 meta-analysis of 28 studies and 4,947 participants had already pointed in the same direction, finding that UTIs decreased significantly by 33 per cent. The 2023 Cochrane update, with moderate-certainty evidence, confirmed the direction.

This is how science is supposed to work, and it is also why confident claims in either direction were premature. In 2012, "cranberry doesn't work" was the best summary of the evidence available. In 2023, "cranberry probably helps some people a bit" became the better summary. Neither version was a lie; the evidence simply grew. Anyone still quoting the 2012 conclusion as the final word is a decade out of date — and anyone presenting the 2023 result as proof that cranberry juice cures UTIs is reading far more into it than the reviewers did.

The pattern will be familiar to readers of our other fact-checks about home remedies. We have seen the same arc — a plausible folk remedy, mixed early evidence, a slow accumulation of trials — in our checks of whether <a href="/blog/does-honey-help-a-cough-fact-check/">does honey help a cough</a> and whether <a href="/blog/does-chicken-soup-help-a-cold-fact-check/">does chicken soup help a cold</a>. In each case the honest answer turned out to be narrower than the viral claim: some benefit, for some people, in some circumstances, and never a substitute for medical care when it matters.

## The Trial That Said "Move On"

Not every trial points the same way, and the most famous negative result deserves a fair hearing. In 2016, a team led by Dr Manisha Juthani-Mehta at the Yale School of Medicine published a double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial in the Journal of the American Medical Association. The study enrolled 185 female nursing home residents aged 65 and over and gave them either two cranberry capsules a day — each containing 36 milligrams of PACs, 72 milligrams in total, equivalent to about 20 ounces of cranberry juice cocktail — or a placebo, for about a year.

The result was a flat negative. After adjusting for missing data and other variables, there was no statistically significant difference in the presence of bacteria plus white blood cells in the urine between the treatment group (29.1 per cent) and the control group (29.0 per cent). Nor was there any significant difference in the number of symptomatic UTIs (10 episodes in the cranberry group versus 12 in the placebo group), in deaths (17 versus 16), in hospitalisations (33 versus 50 episodes), or in antibiotics given for suspected UTIs. In an editorial published alongside the study, Dr Lindsay E. Nicolle concluded that cranberry products should not be recommended as a medical intervention for the prevention of UTI — adding, in a line that was widely quoted, "It is time to move on from cranberries."

The trial was well designed and its result was real. But it was also, in an important sense, the wrong trial for the question most people are asking. Only 5.4 per cent of the participants in the cranberry group had two or more urinary tract infections in the year before enrolment, and three-quarters had not had a UTI at all in the prior year. The trial was carried out on elderly women in nursing homes — precisely the group in which the later Cochrane review found little or no benefit (a risk ratio of 0.93) — rather than on the women with recurrent infections in whom the benefit shows up. The study's authors and critics both noted the mismatch: previous clinical trials had shown cranberry to be mainly effective in the prevention of recurrent UTIs, and this trial largely did not study people who suffered from recurrent UTIs.

The JAMA trial is therefore best read not as a refutation of cranberry, but as a confirmation of the Cochrane pattern: cranberry does not help everyone, and it does not help the group this trial studied. The editorial's call to move on was aimed at doctors recommending cranberry as a general preventive measure. It was not, and could not have been, a verdict on the women with recurrent infections who were barely represented in the trial at all.

## What the Regulators Actually Allow

If cranberry genuinely helps some people, why do regulators sound so cautious? The answer lies in what the US Food and Drug Administration actually did in July 2020 — and in the words it chose.

In a letter of enforcement discretion responding to a health claim petition submitted on behalf of Ocean Spray Cranberries, the FDA announced that it would not object to certain qualified health claims about cranberry products and a reduced risk of recurrent urinary tract infection in healthy women. But the agency was explicit that the evidence did not meet the standard of "significant scientific agreement" required for a fully authorised health claim. Instead, it concluded that there is "limited and inconsistent credible scientific evidence" to support a claim for cranberry juice beverages, and "limited credible scientific evidence" for cranberry dietary supplements.

The claims the FDA permits are correspondingly narrow, and the wording is worth reading in full, because it is far weaker than anything on a juice bottle's front label. For cranberry juice beverages containing at least 27 per cent cranberry juice, the permitted claim reads: "Limited and inconsistent scientific evidence shows that by consuming one serving (8 oz) each day of a cranberry juice beverage, healthy women who have had a urinary tract infection (UTI) may reduce their risk of recurrent UTI." For supplements, the wording is: "Limited scientific evidence shows that by consuming 500 mg each day of cranberry dietary supplement, healthy women who have had a urinary tract infection (UTI) may reduce their risk of recurrent UTI."

Note every qualifier in those sentences. Limited. Inconsistent. May. Healthy women who have had a UTI. Recurrent UTI. The claim does not cover men, children, pregnant women, or anyone who has never had an infection. It does not cover dried cranberries or cranberry sauce, which are excluded from the claims entirely. And the dose matters: the juice claim is built around a beverage containing at least 27 per cent cranberry juice, while most commercially available cranberry cocktails sit right around that line — and many fruit drinks marketed with cranberry on the label contain far less.

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, part of the National Institutes of Health, strikes a similar balance. In guidance updated in November 2024, it says that cranberry products may decrease the overall risk of symptomatic, recurrent UTIs in women by 25 per cent, and in some cases by more than 30 per cent — but adds that the effectiveness of cranberry is "still in question because of inconsistent findings." And it is unambiguous on the point that matters most to anyone currently in pain: cranberry is not recommended as a treatment for existing UTIs in any population.

## The 2025 Guideline Shift

The most recent development in this story came from the people who write the rules doctors actually follow. In a 2025 update, the American Urological Association, together with the Canadian Urological Association and the Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction, moved cranberry from something clinicians "may offer" women with recurrent urinary tract infections to something they "should offer" as an option. The recommendation is now graded as moderate, on Grade B evidence, up from a conditional recommendation on Grade C evidence in 2019. The guideline's wording is: "Clinicians should offer cranberry as an option for prophylaxis for women with rUTIs."

The word to notice is "option." The guideline does not say cranberry is the best prevention, or the only prevention, or that it works for most women. It says that, for women who keep getting infections, cranberry is a reasonable choice to discuss — alongside other options, including vaginal oestrogen for women around and after the menopause, and drinking more water for women who drink too little. The guideline also notes that products providing at least 36 milligrams of PACs a day appear to work better, a threshold that comes from laboratory studies of the dose needed to produce measurable anti-adhesion activity in urine.

New evidence published after the Cochrane review supports the cautious optimism. A 2025 trial in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition randomised 150 women in Australia, all with a history of recurrent UTIs, to 500 milligrams a day of whole cranberry fruit powder or a placebo for six months. Culture-confirmed UTIs occurred in 13 of 73 women taking cranberry (17.8 per cent) and 25 of 72 taking placebo (34.7 per cent), an adjusted relative risk of 0.48. But the trial comes with strings attached: it was sponsored by the maker of the cranberry powder, Swisse Wellness, and in the stricter per-protocol analysis — counting only the women who actually took the product as directed — the difference was no longer statistically significant, with a relative risk of 0.60 and a confidence interval stretching from 0.32 to 1.14.

The guideline shift is therefore real, but it is a shift toward offering cranberry as one option among several, not toward endorsing the viral claim. The same 2025 guideline, notably, concluded that D-mannose — another supplement heavily promoted for the same purpose — did not beat a placebo in a large 2024 trial of 598 women and should not be recommended for preventing recurrent UTIs. The contrast is instructive: two products with similar marketing and similar folk logic, examined by the same guideline writers, came out on opposite sides of the evidence.

## Juice, Capsules or Berries?

One of the most confusing things about the cranberry claim is that "cranberry" is not one product. The research covers at least three different things — juice, capsules and tablets, and whole fruit powder — and the results change with the form.

In the Cochrane analysis, 13 trials of juice or syrup, covering 2,831 participants, found a reduced risk overall, with a risk ratio of 0.78. But when the reviewers looked only at the six trials of women with recurrent UTIs, the juice result was little or no clear difference: a risk ratio of 0.84, with a confidence interval from 0.63 to 1.10 that crosses the line of no effect. Tablets and powders looked better on paper: three trials in 333 women with recurrent UTIs produced a risk ratio of 0.45. It is tempting to conclude that capsules beat juice, and the review's authors decline to draw that conclusion, because only one small trial compared the two forms head to head and found little or no difference between them.

What is clear is that dose and consistency matter. Laboratory work suggests that the PAC dose should be at least 36 milligrams a day, and pooled trial data converge on roughly the same threshold: below it, there is no statistically significant reduction in risk; above it, the benefit appears. Trials also tended to run for 12 to 24 weeks before showing an effect, which means cranberry is a daily-habit intervention, not something that does anything over a weekend. And the trials used unsweetened juice or standardised extracts — not the sweetened cocktail that dominates supermarket shelves. Most juice cocktails are high in added sugar and low in actual cranberry, and no cranberry product of any kind treats an active infection.

There is a practical problem on top of all this: many products do not list their PAC content on the label, and a 2026 government laboratory survey found that products declaring PAC content contained significantly less than stated. A shopper trying to follow the evidence has to find a product that states its PAC content, trust that the label is accurate, take it daily for months, and accept that the expected benefit is roughly a one-in-four reduction in repeat infections — not a cure, and not a guarantee.

## What Cranberry Cannot Do

The most important fact in this entire story is also the simplest: cranberry does not treat an active urinary tract infection. Cochrane's review of cranberry as a treatment, as opposed to prevention, searched the literature up to August 2023 and found no trial that met its inclusion criteria. There is no reliable evidence that cranberry clears an infection that has already started. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health says the same thing in plain language, and the UK's National Health Service advises that there is no evidence cranberry drinks ease symptoms once an infection has started.

This matters because the treatment version of the claim is the one that can cause real harm. A urinary tract infection left untreated can spread to the kidneys, and delaying antibiotics while drinking juice is a delay that can turn a straightforward infection into something serious. The symptoms that should send someone to a clinician rather than to the juice aisle include fever, back or flank pain, vomiting, blood in the urine, or symptoms that do not improve quickly. None of those is a situation in which cranberry has any role at all.

There are also interactions worth knowing about. The NHS advises avoiding cranberry products for people taking warfarin, a blood-thinning medication, and other sources add tacrolimus, an immunosuppressant drug, to the caution list. Cranberry products can also cause stomach pain, and they are not free of sugar: the juice cocktail that many people reach for is a sugary drink, and the sugar feeds the same gut bacteria that can migrate and cause UTIs in the first place — an irony the marketing never mentions.

The honest comparison, then, is not between cranberry and nothing. It is between cranberry and the other things that actually reduce repeat infections: drinking enough water, vaginal oestrogen for women after the menopause, and, for some women, low-dose preventive antibiotics under a doctor's supervision. The Cochrane reviewers noted that they did not find enough information to determine whether cranberry products are more or less effective than antibiotics or probiotics in preventing further UTIs, and the two trials that compared cranberry directly with low-dose preventive antibiotics found no clear difference — though the certainty of that comparison is very low. Cranberry's real advantage over antibiotics is not that it works better, but that it avoids contributing to antibiotic resistance and does not carry antibiotics' side effects. That is a genuine advantage, and it is also a much smaller claim than "nature's antibiotic."

Readers who enjoy tracing how a home remedy fares against the evidence will find the same pattern elsewhere on this site. Our check of whether <a href="/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/">does vitamin D prevent colds and flu</a> found a similarly modest, group-specific benefit wrapped in a much bigger popular claim. The lesson is consistent: the remedies that survive serious testing tend to survive in a smaller, quieter form than the one that went viral.

## How to Read the Claim

The claim-vs-evidence comparison below sums up where the viral version of this claim stands against what the research actually shows:

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Cranberry juice cures a UTI | False — no trial has tested cranberry as a treatment, and health authorities say it is not recommended for existing infections |
| Cranberry juice prevents UTIs for everyone | Misleading — benefit found only in women with recurrent UTIs, children, and people at risk after bladder procedures |
| Any cranberry juice works | Misleading — trials used unsweetened juice or standardised extracts; most commercial cocktails are sugary and low in cranberry |
| The effect is large and reliable | Misleading — about a 26% relative reduction in women with recurrent UTIs, with the confidence interval nearly touching no effect |
| Doctors dismiss cranberry entirely | False — the 2025 urology guideline says clinicians should offer it as an option for women with recurrent UTIs |
| It works fast | Misleading — trials ran 12 to 24 weeks, and the benefit depends on a daily dose of at least about 36 mg of PACs |
| It is completely safe for everyone | Mostly true, with exceptions — mild stomach pain is common, and people on warfarin should avoid cranberry products |

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Cranberry juice does help UTIs — but only in the narrow sense, for the narrow group, and in the narrow form that the viral claim never mentions. The 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials found that cranberry products probably reduce repeat infections by about a quarter in women who get them often, and by larger amounts in children and in people at risk after bladder procedures. The 2025 urology guideline now tells clinicians to offer cranberry as an option for women with recurrent infections. That is a real, evidence-based benefit, and it is more than most folk remedies can show.

But the claim as it actually circulates — drink cranberry juice and your UTI will clear up, or drink it and you will never get one — is wrong in the ways that matter. Cranberry does not treat an infection that has already started: no trial has shown it, and every major health authority says so. The benefit applies to women with recurrent infections, not to everyone, and not to older adults in care homes, pregnant women, or people whose bladder does not empty properly. The juice most people buy, a sweetened cocktail, is not the unsweetened juice or standardised extract the trials tested, and the benefit depends on a daily dose of at least around 36 milligrams of PACs taken for months — a dose many products do not even declare on the label.

The fair summary fits in one sentence: cranberry is a modest, daily prevention aid for women who keep getting urinary infections, worth discussing with a doctor, and useless as a cure. Anyone selling it as more than that is selling the juice, not the evidence. If you think you have a urinary tract infection right now, the cranberry juice can wait — call a clinician first.
