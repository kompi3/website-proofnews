Ask a room full of remote workers whether they get more done at home, and most hands will go up. Ask their managers the same question, and the room gets quieter. Ask the research, and the answer is stranger than either side expects: working from home has been measured, repeatedly, by randomised experiments — the gold standard of evidence — and the experiments disagree with each other. One famous trial found a 13% productivity gain. Another, published in Nature in 2024, found no gain at all. A third found productivity fell by as much as 19%. All three are rigorous. All three measured real output, not opinions. And all three can be true at the same time, because they were measuring different jobs, different arrangements, and different kinds of work.

That disagreement is the story. The claim that working from home makes you more productive circulates as a simple fact, usually attached to a single number — 13% — borrowed from a single study. It is quoted in articles, in job adverts, in arguments with bosses, and in social media posts that present it as settled science. But the 13% figure comes from one experiment, in one company, in one kind of job, more than a decade ago. When researchers repeated the question in other settings, the answer changed. The honest summary of the evidence is not a slogan. It is a set of conditions: what kind of work, how much of the week is spent at home, whether the worker chose the arrangement, and what, exactly, counts as productivity.

This article looks at the studies that measured output rather than asking people how they felt, because that distinction matters enormously. Surveys consistently find that remote workers believe they are more productive at home. Belief is not output. The experiments below measured calls answered, lines of code written, performance grades awarded, and quit rates recorded. They are the best evidence we have, and they point to a verdict that neither the remote-work evangelists nor the return-to-office hardliners will love: the claim, stated as a blanket rule, is misleading.

## The Claim Everyone Repeats

The claim takes several forms, and they are worth separating, because they are not equally supported. The softest version is personal: "I get more done at home." That is a report about one person's experience, and it may well be true for that person. The harder version is the one that travels: "Working from home makes people more productive." That is a general claim about human beings in general, and it is the version that appears in headlines and HR decks. The hardest version of all attaches a number to it: "Working from home increases productivity by 13%." That number has a source, and the source is a real study — but the number has travelled far beyond the setting that produced it.

The claim went mainstream during and after the pandemic, when remote work stopped being a perk for a small minority and became, for millions of people, the default. Before the pandemic, only about 6.5% of American workers mainly worked from home. By March 2025, according to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 22.9% of US employees — roughly 35.5 million workers — worked remotely at least some of the time. Gallup reported in the second quarter of 2025 that among employees whose jobs can be done remotely, 52% work in hybrid arrangements, 26% are fully remote, and only 21% are fully on-site. A question that once concerned a small group of telecommuters now concerns a third of the workforce. It is no surprise that people want a simple answer. The evidence, unfortunately, refuses to provide one.

Part of the problem is that "productivity" is a slippery word. It can mean output per hour, total output per day, the quality of the work, the speed of delivery, or the value created for the employer. A call-centre worker's productivity can be counted in calls per minute. A software engineer's productivity might be counted in lines of code, or in features shipped, or in bugs fixed — or, more honestly, in none of those things alone. A manager's productivity is harder still. When different studies measure different things, they can reach different conclusions without any of them being wrong. Keeping that in mind is the key to understanding why the literature looks so contradictory.

## The 13% Study: The Experiment Behind the Headline

The number that launched a thousand articles comes from a genuine randomised controlled trial, which is why it carries so much weight. In 2010, the Chinese travel company Ctrip — now Trip.com — allowed some of its call-centre employees in Shanghai, chosen at random, to work from home for nine months, while others stayed in the office. Stanford economist Nicholas Bloom and his colleagues studied the results, and the paper was published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2015 under the title "Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment."

The design was unusually clean for a workplace question. The employees were volunteers who had worked at the company for at least six months, had a spare room at home, and had broadband. They were randomly assigned to work from home or in the office. The home workers were given the same equipment, the same shifts, and the same targets. Because the assignment was random, the two groups were comparable — and because the work was answering calls, output could be measured precisely.

The result was a clear win for home working. Home workers' performance rose by 13% compared with their office-based colleagues. The increase broke down into two parts: about 9% came from working more minutes per shift — fewer breaks, fewer sick days, and no commute eating into the working day — and about 4% came from handling more calls per minute, which the researchers attributed to a quieter and more convenient working environment. The home workers also reported higher job satisfaction, and their attrition rate halved: they were about half as likely to quit. The company saved roughly $1,900 per employee in office costs.

Then came the second act, which is quoted less often but matters just as much. After the experiment ended, Ctrip rolled out the option to work from home to the whole firm, and employees could choose. More than half of them switched — and the gains from home working almost doubled, to about 22%. The lesson was not just that home working helped; it was that letting people choose helped even more. The people who thrived at home chose home; the people who preferred the office stayed there. Selection, it turned out, was part of the effect.

There was also a catch, and the paper reported it honestly: the promotion rate of home workers, conditional on their performance, fell. Working from home made people measurably more productive and measurably less likely to be promoted. The researchers called it a visibility problem — out of sight, out of mind — and it is a theme that recurs in every study since. The 13% figure is real, and it is rigorous. But it is the result for call-centre workers, doing individually measured, phone-based work, in one company, in 2010. Whether it generalises to a software engineer, a marketing manager, or a data analyst in 2026 is a separate question — and the researchers who asked it next got a different answer.

## The Twist: The Biggest Recent Trial Found No Gain at All

Nine years after the Ctrip experiment, the same lead researcher ran the question again, in the same company, with a different arrangement — and the headline result vanished. The study, by Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han and James Liang, was published in Nature on 12 June 2024 under the title "Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance."

This time the experiment tested hybrid work, not full-time home working. A total of 1,612 university-graduate employees at Trip.com, in the Airfare and IT divisions, were randomly assigned — by even or odd birthday — to either work from home on Wednesdays and Fridays and come to the office the other three days, or to work in the office all five days. The experiment ran from August 2021 to January 2022, and performance and promotions were followed for up to two years. The participants were engineers, marketers, and finance and accounting staff — knowledge workers doing creative, collaborative team work, not call handling.

The result on retention was large: quit rates fell by a third in the hybrid group, from 7.20% in the office-based control group to 4.80% among hybrid workers. The reduction was concentrated among non-managers, women, and employees with long commutes. The result on performance was a null. Using formal equivalence tests, the authors found that hybrid working did not affect performance grades over the next two years of reviews, and it had no effect on the lines of code written by the 653 computer-engineer employees in the trial. Promotion rates showed no difference either.

Read that carefully, because it is the single most important finding in this whole debate. In the strongest recent trial, the one with random assignment, a large sample, and two years of follow-up, working from home two days a week did not make people more productive. It did not make them less productive either. It made them stay. Managers' expectations shifted over the course of the experiment — before it began, managers expected hybrid work to reduce performance by 2.6%; afterwards, they expected it to raise performance by 1.0% — but every objectively measured output metric stayed statistically unchanged.

That does not contradict the 2015 result. It refines it. The two experiments measured different arrangements (full-time home versus two days a week), different jobs (call handling versus knowledge work), and different things (calls per minute versus performance grades and code). The 2015 trial found a gain for fully remote, individually measured work. The 2024 trial found no change for hybrid, collaborative work. Both can be true, and both are. The claim that working from home makes you more productive is, on this evidence, already looking shaky — and the next studies make it shakier still.

## The Studies That Found the Opposite

Most articles on this question cite the 2015 Ctrip trial and stop. That is a mistake, because two later studies that used administrative data — the records companies actually keep about hours, tickets, and calls — found the opposite direction. Leaving them out is what makes the popular answer look settled.

The first is by Michael Gibbs, Friederike Mengel and Christoph Siemroth, published in the Journal of Political Economy Microeconomics in 2023. They analysed personnel and IT ticketing records for more than 10,000 employees at an Indian technology company, tracking what happened when the whole firm went remote during the pandemic. Their abstract is blunt about the size and the cause: "Hours worked increased, output declined slightly, and productivity fell 8% to 19%… An important source is higher communication costs. Time spent on coordination activities and meetings increased, while uninterrupted work hours shrank considerably." In other words, people worked longer and produced less per hour, because remote work replaced quick desk-side questions with scheduled meetings and made it harder to get a stretch of uninterrupted focus.

The second is by Natalia Emanuel and Emma Harrington, published in the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics in October 2024, first circulated as a New York Federal Reserve staff report. They used administrative call-centre data and a difference-in-differences design, and they separated two effects that most commentary blends together: working remotely can lower output, and the kind of person who chooses remote work can differ from the kind of person who does not. Before the pandemic, remote workers in their data answered 12% fewer calls per hour than on-site workers. When formerly on-site workers went remote, their productivity declined by 4% relative to workers who were already remote — the treatment effect of remote work itself. Yet an 8% productivity gap persisted, which the authors attributed to selection: the people who chose remote work were, on average, less productive in that job to begin with. Two thirds of the raw gap was selection rather than the arrangement. That distinction matters, because it means the raw comparison between remote and office workers — the comparison most surveys and most managers make — exaggerates the effect of location itself.

A broader review points the same way. In "The Evolution of Working from Home," published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in autumn 2023, José María Barrero, Nicholas Bloom and Steven Davis reviewed the evidence and wrote that fully remote work "is associated with about 10% lower productivity than fully in-person work," while hybrid work "appears to have no impact on productivity." Note the word "associated": this is a summary of observational evidence, not a randomised result. But it is a summary written by the same economist who ran the famous 13% experiment, and it is notably more cautious than the headline his own study generated.

So the scoreboard, for measured output, looks like this: one randomised trial of fully remote call-centre work found +13%; one randomised trial of hybrid knowledge work found no change; one large study of IT workers found −8% to −19%; one call-centre study found a −4% treatment effect with an −8% selection gap; and the best-known review puts fully remote work at roughly −10% on average and hybrid at zero. No single number survives contact with the full literature. The arrangement, the job, and the measurement all change the answer.

## Why the Studies Disagree

The disagreement is not a sign that the research is broken. It is a sign that "working from home" is not one thing. At least four differences explain why the results point in different directions, and each one is worth understanding before anyone repeats the claim.

First, the job matters. Call-centre work is individually measured, repetitive, and largely independent: one person's calls do not depend on a colleague's calls. For that kind of work, a quiet home and fewer interruptions are a straightforward advantage, and the 13% gain makes sense. Knowledge work — engineering, marketing, finance — is collaborative and interdependent. It involves asking questions, reviewing each other's work, and coordinating across a team. For that kind of work, the cost of communication rises when everyone is remote, and the benefit of quiet is partly cancelled out. The Gibbs study's finding — more meetings, fewer uninterrupted hours — is exactly what you would expect when collaborative work moves home.

Second, the arrangement matters. Fully remote and hybrid are different interventions, and the evidence treats them differently. The 2015 trial tested full-time home working. The 2024 Nature trial tested two days a week at home. The review evidence suggests hybrid is roughly neutral for productivity while fully remote runs about 10% lower on average. That pattern makes intuitive sense: two days at home gives workers quiet time for focused tasks while keeping three days in the office for the collaboration that needs face-to-face contact. Full-time remote keeps the quiet but loses the coordination. The claim that working from home boosts productivity usually fails to say which version of working from home it means.

Third, choice matters. The Ctrip experiment's second phase is one of the most under-appreciated findings in the literature: when employees could choose, the gains nearly doubled, from 13% to about 22%. People are not identical. Some concentrate better at home; some need the structure and social pressure of an office; some have a spare room and fast broadband, and some are trying to work from a kitchen table with children in the next room. A policy that lets people sort themselves into the environment where they work best will outperform a policy that forces everyone into the same arrangement, in either direction. The Emanuel and Harrington finding reinforces the point from the other side: the people who choose remote work differ systematically from those who do not, and ignoring that difference distorts the comparison.

Fourth, what gets measured matters. The studies that found gains measured individual output — calls per minute, minutes worked. The studies that found losses measured output per hour across a whole workforce, including the time spent coordinating. And none of the studies can fully capture the quality of the work, the creativity of a solution, or the value of a conversation that prevents a mistake. A programmer who writes fewer lines of code at home might be writing better code, or reviewing a colleague's code, or thinking through a design that saves weeks later. The metrics we have are proxies, and proxies have limits. That does not make the studies useless — it makes them partial, and anyone who treats one metric as the whole truth is overclaiming.

There is also a time dimension. Industry-wide data leans more positive than the firm-level trials. A Bureau of Labor Statistics study published in October 2024, by Sabrina Wulff Pabilonia and Jill Janocha Redmond, looked across 61 industries and found that total factor productivity growth from 2019 to 2022 was positively associated with growth in remote work — with the authors' own warning that "remote work is only one factor that may affect productivity." A Federal Reserve note by Maria Tito, dated 25 August 2025, found that increases in remote work ability led to sizable productivity gains over time, with labour productivity gains appearing after about three years. And a 2025 survey by Stanford and the US Census Bureau, covering more than 150,000 firms, found that the most common view among businesses was that productivity is similar for home and on-site work, with only 7% saying on-site work is more productive. The economy-wide picture is gentler than the firm-level picture, possibly because firms adapted — better tools, better processes, better hiring — over several years. But association is not causation, and the industry-level data cannot tell us what would happen to any particular worker or team.

## The Statistics That Don't Survive Scrutiny

If the peer-reviewed evidence is mixed, the numbers that circulate on social media are worse. Two of the most repeated remote-work productivity statistics collapse on inspection, and they are worth naming, because they show how a claim can feel evidence-based while resting on nothing.

The first is the claim that remote workers are 47% more productive. It traces to a May 2020 press release from Prodoscore, a company that sells employee monitoring software. The company compared March and April 2020 against the same months in 2019 across its own user base — a period when the entire world was in lockdown and offices were closed by law. There was no control group, no published statistical methodology, and no peer review. It is a marketing claim from a vendor with a direct financial interest in the result, dressed up as research.

The second is the claim that 77% of remote workers are more productive. It traces to a December 2014 survey by ConnectSolutions and CoSo Cloud of 353 US internet users, with a stated margin of error of plus or minus 5.22 points. CoSo Cloud sold remote collaboration software at the time. The figure also circulates online attributed to a Statistics Canada report; Statistics Canada's 2024 Time Use Survey release contains no such figure, and the attribution appears to be an error passed from article to article. A decade-old survey of 353 people, conducted by a vendor, is not evidence about the modern workforce.

There is a subtler problem, too: almost every remote-work statistic you have read is self-reported. The 2024 Nature trial measured self-assessment and output side by side, which almost no other study does, and the two moved independently. Managers' surveyed expectations shifted from minus 2.6% before the experiment to plus 1.0% after it, while every objectively measured output metric stayed statistically null across two years of reviews. When a survey says a percentage of remote workers feel more productive, it is measuring belief. Belief matters — job satisfaction is a real outcome — but it is not output, and the two should not be confused. Microsoft's Work Trend Index makes the gap visible from the other side: in 2022, 87% of employees said they were productive at work, while only 12% of senior leaders said they had full confidence their team was productive. At least one side of that gap is wrong about the facts.

## The Commute Dividend

One benefit of working from home is not disputed by any study, and it explains part of why the claim feels true to so many people: the commute disappears, and the time comes back. Research by Barrero, Bloom and Davis estimated that working from home saves about 72 minutes a day in commuting and preparation time, with roughly 40% of the saved time reinvested into the job. That is a real gain — nearly half an hour of extra work a day, on average, without anyone working longer in any meaningful sense.

But the commute dividend is not the same as a productivity gain, and confusing the two is one of the most common errors in this debate. Working more minutes is not the same as working better per minute. The Ctrip study's own breakdown makes the distinction explicit: 9 of the 13 percentage points came from working more minutes per shift, and only 4 points came from handling more calls per minute. Two thirds of the headline gain was time, not efficiency. And time saved from commuting is not automatically time spent working — the same research found that much of it goes to sleep, leisure, and family, which is a genuine benefit to workers but not a gain in output.

There is also the question of what the commute was doing. For some people it was dead time. For others it was a boundary — the psychological line between work and home — and losing it has costs. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found that the average knowledge worker is interrupted by a meeting, email, or chat every two minutes during core work hours, about 275 times a day, and that 48% of employees and 52% of leaders say their work feels chaotic and fragmented. Some of that fragmentation is the office's fault, and some of it followed workers home. A home office does not automatically fix the interruption problem; it changes its shape.

## The Hidden Costs: Collaboration, Visibility, and Careers

Productivity is not the only thing at stake, and the studies that measured only output missed costs that workers feel directly. The most concrete is collaboration. A study by Longqi Yang and colleagues, published in Nature Human Behaviour in 2022, analysed 61,182 Microsoft employees in the United States during the first half of 2020 and found that the move to firm-wide remote work made the company's collaboration network "more static and siloed": people communicated more within their own groups and less across them, and the network became less interconnected. That is a cost that does not show up in calls per minute or lines of code, but it shows up, eventually, in slower innovation, weaker mentoring, and fewer chance encounters that spark new ideas.

The second cost is visibility. The Ctrip experiment found that home workers' promotion rates fell even though their performance rose. The 2024 Nature trial, by contrast, found no difference in promotion rates over two years — but that trial tested hybrid work, not full-time remote work, and the participants were in the office three days a week, which may have been enough to stay visible. The difference between the two results is itself informative: being entirely out of the office appears to carry a career cost that two days a week at home does not. For a worker deciding whether to go fully remote, that is a material fact, and it is not captured by any productivity statistic.

The third cost is the home itself. Not every home is a good office. The Ctrip volunteers were required to have a spare room and broadband; that filter alone excluded many workers. A kitchen table, a shared bedroom, a slow connection, or children who need attention during the working day can erase the advantages of quiet and turn the home into a worse workplace than the office. The research on average effects hides this variation, and it is one reason the aggregate numbers look so contradictory: the average conceals a wide spread, from workers who thrive at home to workers who are measurably worse off there.

## What Actually Works: Hybrid, Choice, and Design

If the blanket claim is misleading, the evidence still points to an arrangement that works well for most people, and it is the one the strongest trial tested: hybrid work, with choice, designed deliberately. The Nature 2024 trial found that two days a week at home cost nothing in measured output and cut quitting by a third. The Stanford and Census Bureau survey found most firms see no productivity difference between home and on-site work. The review evidence puts hybrid at roughly zero for productivity — neither a gain nor a loss — with a clear gain in retention. That is not a spectacular result, and it will not satisfy anyone looking for a slogan. But it is a solid, honest one: hybrid work does not make people more productive, and it does not make them less productive either. It keeps them.

Choice is the second ingredient. The Ctrip rollout showed that letting people select their arrangement nearly doubled the gains, and the Emanuel and Harrington study showed that selection explains much of the raw gap between remote and office workers. A policy that forces everyone home is as crude as a policy that forces everyone back, and both will waste the information that workers have about their own circumstances. The best-performing arrangement is the one that matches the person to the environment where they do their best work.

Design is the third ingredient, and it is the one employers control most directly. The Gibbs study's mechanism — more meetings, fewer uninterrupted hours — is a management problem, not a law of nature. Teams that protect focus time, measure output rather than hours, and use asynchronous communication can keep the benefits of remote work while limiting its costs. The home office is also a technology question, not just a location question: a worker on a home network faces the same security questions as anyone else, from whether <a href="/blog/do-you-need-antivirus-on-your-phone-fact-check/">do you need antivirus on your phone</a> to whether <a href="/blog/public-wifi-vpn-safety-fact-check/">is public Wi-Fi safe without a VPN</a> when working from a café — and, for that matter, whether <a href="/blog/is-your-phone-listening-to-you-fact-check/">is your phone listening to you</a> when it sits on the desk all day. None of those questions changes the productivity evidence, but they are part of what a well-designed remote setup has to answer.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The claim that working from home makes you more productive is not supported as a general rule. The most-cited evidence for it, the 2015 Ctrip experiment, is real and rigorous — but it measured fully remote call-centre work and found a 13% gain, of which two thirds came from working more minutes rather than working better per minute. The strongest recent trial, a randomised experiment published in Nature in 2024, tested hybrid work among 1,612 knowledge workers and found no effect on measured performance at all, while cutting quit rates by a third. Other rigorous studies found the opposite: productivity fell 8% to 19% among IT workers in one large study, and a call-centre study found a 4% productivity penalty from remote work itself, with an additional 8% gap explained by who chooses to work remotely. The best-known review of the evidence puts fully remote work at about 10% lower productivity than fully in-person work, and hybrid work at no impact either way.

What the evidence actually supports is narrower and more useful than the slogan. Hybrid work — two days a week at home — does not hurt output and keeps people in their jobs. Fully remote work can help for individually measured, independent tasks, and can hurt for collaborative work, depending on the job and the setup. Letting people choose their arrangement improves the outcome. And the statistics most often quoted in favour of the claim — 47% more productive, 77% more productive — trace to a vendor press release and a decade-old survey of 353 people, and should not be repeated.

The question "does working from home make you more productive" has no single answer, because it is not a single question. It is a bundle of questions about different jobs, different schedules, and different people — and the research answers each of them differently. Anyone who tells you otherwise is quoting one row of the table and calling it the whole story.
