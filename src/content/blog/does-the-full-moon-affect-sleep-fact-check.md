Every month, as the moon swells toward full, the same complaint surfaces in bedrooms, hospital wards, and police stations: nobody can sleep. Nurses swap stories about chaotic night shifts. Parents blame a restless toddler on the bright disc outside the window. Millions of people are convinced that the full moon wrecks their night, and the belief is so old that the word for madness itself — lunatic — comes from the Latin *luna*, the moon.

It would be easy to dismiss all of this as folklore. The moon's pull on the human body sounds like astrology dressed up as biology, and most of the grander claims attached to it — that full moons drive crime, madness, and emergency-room chaos — have collapsed under serious testing. But sleep turns out to be the one place where the folklore has something real behind it. Not much. Not for everyone. And not in the way most people imagine. Yet when scientists put volunteers in windowless laboratories and tracked their brains through the night, something measurable happened around the full moon. When other scientists tried to repeat the result with a thousand more volunteers, it vanished. And when a third team followed people out of the lab and into the real world, the effect came back — on different nights than anyone expected.

This is the story of three studies, one famous disagreement, and a surprisingly simple explanation that has nothing to do with gravity.

## The Claim: A Belief as Old as the Word "Lunatic"

The idea that the moon disturbs sleep is not a modern internet invention. It is woven into language and folklore across cultures. The Romans believed the moon could drive people mad — hence *lunaticus*, moon-struck. Werewolf legends tied transformation to the full moon. In the modern era, the belief survives in a more practical form: emergency-room staff, police officers, and psychiatric nurses are famously convinced that full-moon nights bring more chaos, more admissions, and more restless patients.

Sleep is where the claim feels most personal, because it is where people test it on themselves. A bad night is memorable, and a bright moon outside the window offers a ready explanation. The question is whether that explanation survives contact with measurement — with electrodes on the scalp, blood samples through the night, and wrist monitors worn for weeks. Over the past decade, three landmark studies have tried to answer it, and their results read like a detective story with a twist in every chapter.

Before going further, it is worth separating the claim into two parts, because science treats them very differently. The narrow claim is that the lunar cycle changes how people sleep: how long it takes to fall asleep, how much deep sleep they get, and how long they sleep overall. The broad claim is that the moon changes human behaviour in general — mood, aggression, mental illness, crime. The evidence for the narrow claim is genuinely mixed and interesting. The evidence for the broad claim is, to put it plainly, a rout: decades of research have found essentially nothing.

That split is the key to this whole fact-check. The full moon does something to sleep, sometimes, in small doses. It does not turn people into lunatics. The rest of this article is about how scientists know that, and how big — or small — the real effect is.

## The 2013 Study That Put the Moon on the Scientific Map

The modern scientific debate began, fittingly enough, in a bar under a full moon. Christian Cajochen, a chronobiologist at the University of Basel in Switzerland, and his colleagues were sitting outside one evening when someone raised the old question: does the moon really affect sleep? The team realised they already had the data to find out. Years earlier, they had run a tightly controlled sleep study to investigate how ageing affects sleep, and that study had been conducted under conditions so strict that they accidentally created the perfect experiment for testing the moon.

Thirty-three volunteers, aged 20 to 74, had spent nights in a sleep laboratory with no windows, no clocks, and no cues about the time of day or the phase of the moon. Their brain waves were recorded with electrodes, their eye movements tracked, and their hormone levels measured through the night. Nobody involved — neither the volunteers nor the researchers running the nights — had any reason to think about the moon at all. Years later, Cajochen's team went back to those 64 nights of data and sorted them by lunar phase, asking a question the original study had never been designed to answer.

The result, published in the journal *Current Biology* on 25 July 2013, was startling. Around the full moon, the volunteers' deep sleep — the slow-wave, non-REM sleep measured by delta activity in the brain's electrical signals — dropped by 30 percent. They took about five minutes longer to fall asleep. Their total sleep, measured by the brain recordings, was about 20 minutes shorter. Their evening melatonin levels were lower, and the nightly peak of melatonin was delayed by about 50 minutes. And when asked the next morning, the volunteers rated their sleep as roughly 15 percent worse.

"The lunar cycle seems to influence human sleep, even when one does not 'see' the moon and is not aware of the actual moon phase," Cajochen said at the time. The paper's conclusion was careful but bold: "This is the first reliable evidence that a lunar rhythm can modulate sleep structure in humans when measured under the highly controlled conditions of a circadian laboratory study protocol without time cues."

The numbers deserve a closer look, because they show both why the study made headlines and why it should not be overread. A 30 percent drop in deep-sleep brain activity sounds dramatic, but it is a relative change in a signal, not a 30 percent loss of all deep sleep. Five extra minutes to fall asleep is real but small — smaller than the effect of a cup of coffee in the evening. Twenty minutes less sleep is noticeable, but it is the kind of shortfall most people absorb without realising it. The melatonin finding was the most intriguing: levels in the full-moon group averaged around 4 picograms per millilitre of blood, against about 8.2 in the new-moon group, a halving of the hormone that signals bedtime to the brain.

The study had an obvious weakness, and Cajochen himself acknowledged it: 33 people is a small sample. The analysis was also retrospective — the researchers had not set out to study the moon, but had gone looking for a pattern in old data, which raises the risk of finding a fluke. Small studies with striking results are exactly the kind of finding that demands a repeat performance. Within a year, one arrived.

## The Replication That Found Nothing

In June 2014, less than a year after the Basel study made headlines around the world, a team led by Martin Dresler at the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry in Munich published its answer in the same journal, *Current Biology*. The researchers had analysed the sleep data of 1,265 volunteers across 2,097 nights — roughly forty times as many people as the original study. They looked for the same pattern: shorter sleep, less deep sleep, lower melatonin around the full moon. They found nothing.

"Investigating this large cohort of test persons and sleep nights, we were unable to replicate previous findings," Dresler said. "We could not observe a statistical relevant correlation between human sleep and the lunar phases."

The Munich team went further than simply reporting a null result. They dug up several unpublished analyses — studies that had looked for a lunar effect on sleep and found nothing, and had never been published. Added together, those unpublished datasets covered more than 20,000 nights of sleep. The pattern, the authors argued, looked like a classic case of the "file drawer problem": positive, surprising results get published and publicised, while boring null results sit in a drawer, so the published literature makes an effect look far more solid than it is.

Cajochen's team pushed back. In a published reply, they argued that bigger is not automatically better in sleep research: increasing the number of volunteers does not automatically increase the quality of the data, and their small study had been conducted under far stricter laboratory control than the larger datasets, which had been collected for other purposes under looser conditions. Both sides had a point, and the field was left with a genuine scientific standoff: one rigorous small study saying yes, one much larger analysis saying no, and a drawer full of unpublished null results whispering that the first result might have been a statistical fluke.

A later reanalysis from the University of Surrey added to the confusion. Researchers there examined 205 people studied in windowless laboratories and found no overall effect of lunar phase on either objective or subjective sleep — but they did find some sex-specific patterns, with women sleeping slightly less around the full moon and men showing slightly more REM sleep. The picture was not settling into a clean yes or no. It was fragmenting into something more complicated: perhaps the effect is real but small, perhaps it affects some people and not others, perhaps it depends on conditions the lab studies were not capturing.

That is where the story stood for several years — until a team of researchers decided to stop arguing about laboratories and go outside.

## The 2021 Field Study: The Answer Was in the Evenings Before

In January 2021, a team from the University of Washington, the National University of Quilmes in Argentina, and Yale University published a study that reframed the entire question. Instead of bringing people into a laboratory, they followed them into their lives.

The researchers fitted wrist activity monitors — devices that track movement and infer sleep timing — to 98 people in three Indigenous Toba/Qom communities in the Argentine province of Formosa. The three communities differed in a crucial respect: one had no access to electricity at all, one had limited access, and one had full access. The team also tracked 464 university students in Seattle, a city of more than 750,000 people bathed in artificial light. The idea was to test the lunar effect across the full spectrum of modern life, from a world lit only by fire and moonlight to a world that never really gets dark.

The result, published in *Science Advances* under the title "Moonstruck sleep: Synchronization of human sleep with the moon cycle under field conditions", was clear and unexpected. In every group — rural and urban, with electricity and without — sleep started later and lasted for a shorter time in the nights leading up to the full moon. The peak of the effect came three to five days *before* the full moon, not on the night of the full moon itself.

"Our results show that sleep starts later and is shorter on the nights before the full moon when moonlight is available during the hours following dusk," the authors wrote. That timing is the clue that changes everything. In the days before a full moon, the waxing moon rises in the late afternoon or early evening, so it hangs high and bright in the sky in the hours just after sunset — exactly when the body is preparing for sleep. On the night of the full moon itself, the moon does not rise until around sunset and climbs later, so the early evening is darker. If the moon were acting through some mysterious internal rhythm, the effect should peak on the full-moon night. If it is acting through light, it should peak in the evenings before — and that is precisely what the data showed.

The effect was strongest in the community without electricity, where moonlight is the only light in the evening, and weakest — but still detectable — among the students in Seattle, who live under streetlights and screens. That gradient is exactly what a light-driven explanation predicts. The researchers suggested that moonlight once served our ancestors as a useful signal: a bright evening meant it was worth staying up a little longer to hunt, gather, or socialise, and the body adapted to take advantage of it. Artificial light, they proposed, may now mimic that ancient effect every single night, which would help explain why the lunar signal is so faint in the modern world: it is drowned out by a much brighter, constant version of the same stimulus.

There is an important caveat. The 2021 study was observational: it can show that sleep timing moves with the lunar cycle, but it cannot prove the moonlight caused the shift. Other factors that cycle with the moon — social activity, for instance — could play a role. Still, the study did something neither of the laboratory studies could do: it found the same pattern in four very different populations, and it found it at exactly the time of the month when the light-based explanation says it should appear.

## Why Moonlight, Not Moon Magic

With three landmark studies in hand, the scientific picture is finally coming into focus — and it points to light, not to gravity, magnetism, or any hidden lunar clock.

The gravitational explanation can be dismissed quickly. The moon's gravity does move the oceans, but the gravitational difference it creates across a human body is vanishingly small — far too weak to tug at blood, brain fluid, or hormones in any way that could change sleep. Studies of geomagnetic and atmospheric changes across the lunar cycle have produced inconsistent and negligible results. As Joanna Fong-Isariyawongse, a neurologist specialising in sleep medicine at the University of Pittsburgh, put it in an explainer for *The Conversation*: the gravitational forces that move oceans are far too weak to affect human physiology.

Light, by contrast, is the master switch of human sleep. The brain's internal clock takes its cues overwhelmingly from light falling on the eyes, and evening light delays the clock, suppresses melatonin — the hormone of darkness, produced by the pineal gland — and keeps the brain alert. This is why sleep specialists warn against bright screens late at night, and why the same researchers who study the moon also study streetlights. A bright moon in the early evening is, in effect, a dim lamp left on in the sky. It is far weaker than a room light, but for people whose evenings are otherwise dark, it is enough to nudge the clock later.

That mechanism also explains the pattern of disagreement between the studies. In a windowless laboratory, where the only light is whatever the experimenters control, a lunar effect should not appear at all if light is the whole story — yet Cajochen's team found one. That is the result the light explanation struggles with, and it is why the 2013 study still matters: either there is an internal lunar rhythm that nobody has yet identified, or the small study's striking numbers were a statistical fluke of the kind the file-drawer problem predicts. The 2014 replication, with forty times as many volunteers, points toward fluke. The 2021 field study points toward light. The honest summary is that both explanations may hold a piece of the truth: a small, light-driven effect in the real world, and a laboratory result that has never been cleanly reproduced.

One more detail from the research is worth noting, because it shows how uneven the effect is. The Surrey reanalysis found that men and women responded differently, and Fong-Isariyawongse's summary of the literature reports the same pattern: men seem to lose more sleep during the waxing phase of the moon, while women experience slightly less deep and restful sleep around the full moon itself. If the effect were a simple physical force acting on all bodies alike, it would not split by sex. A light-driven effect filtered through different sleep patterns, different evening routines, and different sensitivity to light would behave exactly this way — messily, and differently from person to person.

The practical consequence is that the moon is a minor player in most people's sleep. Fong-Isariyawongse's bottom line is blunt: the moon's effect on sleep is probably smaller than the effect of your screen time at night. A single evening of scrolling, a late coffee, or an irregular bedtime will cost you far more sleep than a full moon ever will.

## What About Mood, Crime, and "Lunar Lunacy"?

If sleep is the one place where the moon leaves a fingerprint, the rest of the folklore has fared far worse. The claim that full moons drive people mad, violent, or into hospital is one of the most studied ideas in all of psychology — and one of the most thoroughly rejected.

In 1985, psychologists James Rotton and Ivan Kelly published a meta-analysis that pooled 37 studies of the so-called lunar-lunacy effect, covering crime rates, suicides, psychiatric hospital admissions, and crisis calls. Their conclusion was unambiguous: there was no reliable relationship between the phase of the moon and any of these outcomes. Later reviews reached the same verdict. The belief survives among nurses, police officers, and emergency staff, but when researchers check the records, the busy full-moon shifts turn out to be no busier than any other night.

A 2019 study of 17,966 psychiatric inpatient cases in the Swiss canton of Grisons looked specifically for a lunar pattern in admissions, discharges, and lengths of stay, and found none. Smaller studies have occasionally reported quirks — slightly more use of restraints in Indian psychiatric hospitals during full moons in 2016–2017 data, a small rise in schizophrenia admissions in one Chinese dataset — but these are isolated findings in large datasets where chance alone guarantees that something, somewhere, will look significant. They have not replicated, and they sit alongside a much larger body of work that finds nothing.

Why does the belief persist if the data is so clear? Psychologists point to a combination of confirmation bias and the power of a good story. A chaotic night shift on a full moon is memorable and retold; a chaotic night shift on a crescent moon is forgotten by morning. The moon is visible, dramatic, and always available as an explanation, so it collects credit for coincidences. It is the same cognitive shortcut that keeps dozens of other myths alive — and the reason fact-checking them one at a time is a slow business.

The split verdict matters for how to talk about this topic. Saying "the moon affects you" is too vague to be useful. The moon affects your sleep a little, sometimes, mostly through its light. It does not affect your mood, your sanity, or your likelihood of committing a crime. Anyone selling a broader lunar theory — full-moon detoxes, lunar-charged crystals for better sleep, emergency-room folklore dressed up as science — is extrapolating far beyond what the evidence supports.

## The Three Studies, Side by Side

The value of this story is in the comparison. Three serious teams, three different methods, three different answers — and the disagreement itself is the finding. Here are the numbers that matter, in one place.

| Study | Year | People studied | Setting | What it found |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cajochen et al., *Current Biology* | 2013 | 33 volunteers, 64 nights | Windowless sleep lab, no time cues | Deep-sleep brain activity down 30%, 5 min longer to fall asleep, 20 min less total sleep, melatonin lower around full moon |
| Cordi et al., *Current Biology* | 2014 | 1,265 volunteers, 2,097 nights | Reanalysis of three sleep datasets | No correlation between lunar phase and sleep; unpublished null results covering 20,000+ nights suggest publication bias |
| Casiraghi et al., *Science Advances* | 2021 | 98 people in three Argentine communities + 464 Seattle students | Field study with wrist monitors | Sleep started later and was shorter 3–5 nights before full moon, when moonlight is available after dusk; strongest without electricity |

Read together, the table tells a coherent story rather than a contradictory one. The effect, if it exists, is small — measured in minutes, not hours. It is inconsistent — it appears in some studies and vanishes in others. It tracks moonlight, not the full moon itself — it peaks in the evenings before, when the waxing moon is high after dusk. And it shrinks as artificial light grows — strongest where there is no electricity, faintest in a bright city. That is not the profile of a powerful lunar force. It is the profile of a faint environmental signal that modern life has mostly buried.

It is also a useful reminder of how science actually works. The 2013 study was not "wrong" in any simple sense — it was a careful analysis that produced a real, publishable result. The 2014 study was not a "debunking" in any simple sense either — it could not prove there is no effect, only that a large sample could not find one. And the 2021 study did not "settle" the debate — it was observational, and its authors said so themselves. What the three studies did together is far more valuable than any single verdict: they narrowed a folk belief down to a specific, testable mechanism, measured it, and found its limits. That is what progress looks like.

## What This Means for Your Next Full Moon

None of this means you should ignore a bad night that happens to coincide with a full moon. If you slept badly and the moon was bright, the moon may genuinely have contributed — especially if you sleep in a room with thin curtains, or if you were out in the evening while the waxing moon was high. But the contribution is likely to be small: a few minutes longer to fall asleep, a little less deep sleep, perhaps twenty minutes less sleep in total. If you lost two hours, something else did the damage.

The fixes are the same ones sleep specialists recommend for every other night, and they matter far more than the lunar calendar. Keep the bedroom dark — blackout curtains or a sleep mask will block moonlight far more effectively than any amount of worrying about the phase. Keep the wake time consistent, even at weekends, because a regular schedule is the strongest anchor for the body clock. Get bright light in the morning, which sets the clock more firmly than evening moonlight can shift it. And keep screens and bright light out of the last hour before bed, because the light from a phone held inches from your face is vastly stronger than any moonlight coming through a window.

That last point deserves emphasis, because the modern sleep crisis has a much more obvious culprit than the moon. The same light mechanism that makes moonlight a mild sleep disruptor makes screens, late-night television, and bright room lighting far stronger ones — they are brighter, closer, and present every night rather than a few nights a month. Anyone blaming a single bad night on the moon while scrolling in bed until midnight is looking at the wrong light source.

For people who do struggle with sleep more generally, the evidence points toward habits rather than lunar cycles. Supplements are a popular shortcut, but the record is underwhelming: as we found in <a href="/blog/does-magnesium-help-you-sleep-fact-check/">Does Magnesium Help You Sleep? What 12 Trials Actually Found</a>, the best trials show people falling asleep about seventeen minutes sooner, with no significant gain in total sleep time, on low-quality evidence. Viral bedtime rituals fare no better — <a href="/blog/does-the-sleepy-girl-mocktail-help-you-sleep-fact-check/">Does the Sleepy Girl Mocktail Help You Sleep? What the Trials Actually Show</a> found the same pattern of a plausible idea, a modest effect, and marketing running far ahead of the data. And if your sleep debt comes from the working week, the weekend lie-in is not the rescue it feels like. The unglamorous truth is that regular sleep habits outperform every shortcut, lunar or otherwise.

There is one more reason to keep the moon in perspective. The moon's other supposed effects on the body have not survived testing either. We examined the claim that the lunar cycle synchronises menstruation in <a href="/blog/does-the-moon-affect-your-period-fact-check/">Does the Moon Affect Your Period? The 7.5-Million-Cycle Answer</a>, and the largest dataset ever assembled — 7.5 million menstrual cycles — found no synchronisation at all. The moon is a beautiful object and a useful calendar. It is not a puppeteer.

None of that should diminish the genuine charm of the finding. There is something quietly wonderful about the idea that a light our ancestors slept under for hundreds of thousands of years still nudges our brains today — that the same moonlight which once let early humans hunt and gather after dark still delays our bedtime by a few minutes, three nights before the full moon, in villages without electricity and in cities that never sleep. The effect is small. The history behind it is enormous. And the fact that scientists argued about it in public, replicated each other, dug unpublished failures out of drawers, and eventually found the answer in the timing of the evenings before the full moon is a better advertisement for science than any single study could be.

## The Verdict

Does the full moon affect sleep? The evidence says yes — but only a little, only sometimes, and mostly because of moonlight rather than the moon itself. The best laboratory study found about 20 minutes less sleep, 30 percent less deep-sleep brain activity, and lower melatonin around the full moon. A much larger replication found no effect at all and uncovered unpublished null results covering more than 20,000 nights. And the best field study found that sleep starts later and runs shorter in the three to five nights *before* the full moon, when the waxing moon shines bright in the early evening — exactly what a light-driven explanation predicts, and strongest where there is no electricity to drown it out.

That is a real effect, but it is a modest one, measured in minutes and visible only when researchers look carefully. It is not the dramatic, night-ruining force of folklore, and it says nothing about mood, madness, or crime, where the evidence is firmly negative. So the claim earns a Mostly True: the moon does affect sleep, a bit, for some people, through its light. If you sleep badly on a full moon, the moon may have helped — but your curtains, your screen, and your bedtime probably helped more.
