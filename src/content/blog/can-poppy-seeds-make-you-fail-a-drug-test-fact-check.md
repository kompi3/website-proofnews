It is one of the most repeated excuses in the history of workplace drug testing, and one of the most mocked. An employee tests positive for opiates and blames breakfast. A sitcom character loses a job opportunity over a muffin. A police officer with an exemplary record is suspended, and the only defence on offer is a bagel. For decades, the poppy seed defence sounded like the kind of story someone invents when they have run out of better ones. The surprise is that it is real — and that the science behind it is both more convincing and more complicated than the joke suggests.

Poppy seeds really do carry traces of morphine and codeine, the same compounds that opiate drug tests are designed to detect. Controlled studies have fed volunteers poppy seed cakes, rolls and bagels and then watched their urine test positive for opiates, sometimes at concentrations far above the threshold a laboratory uses to call a result positive. The effect is documented, repeatable, and strong enough that the United States federal government rewrote its workplace testing rules because of it. At the same time, the risk for an ordinary person eating an ordinary breakfast is far smaller than the legend implies, because the modern federal cutoff was deliberately set high enough to let most poppy seed eaters pass. Whether a poppy seed breakfast fails your drug test turns out to depend on three things: how many seeds you ate, where those seeds came from, and — above all — which test you took.

## The Claim

The claim, in its simplest form, is that eating poppy seeds can make you fail a drug test. It circulates in a few different versions. The mild version says a single poppy seed bagel can trigger a positive result. A stronger version says that poppy seeds can make you test positive for heroin specifically. The strongest version, usually told as a warning before a pre-employment screening, says you should avoid all poppy seed foods for days before any test, because no amount is safe.

All three versions contain a grain of truth, but they are not equally true, and the differences between them matter. The mild version was once true under the old testing rules and is now mostly false under the federal rules that govern most American workplace testing. The heroin version is false in an important technical sense, because the laboratory marker that proves heroin use cannot come from food. The warning version is the closest to good advice, not because a bagel will ruin a federal workplace test, but because not every test follows the federal rules — and the ones that do not can be far less forgiving.

To sort this out, it helps to start with the seed itself. The poppy seed is the edible seed of the opium poppy, *Papaver somniferum*, the same plant from which opium is harvested. The seeds themselves do not manufacture opiates. The opium is a milky latex that sits inside the seed pod, and during harvest the seeds can become coated with traces of that latex. That contamination is where the morphine and codeine come from. How much ends up on the seeds depends on how they were grown, harvested, washed and processed — and the variation is enormous.

A study published in 2004 by Moeller and colleagues compared the morphine content of poppy seeds by country of origin and found a range that would surprise anyone who thinks of a poppy seed as a standardised ingredient. Seeds bought from a retail store in Oregon carried the highest morphine content, at 294 milligrams per kilogram. Seeds from Spain carried 251 milligrams per kilogram, and seeds from Australia 200 milligrams per kilogram. Seeds from Turkey had the lowest content, with some samples containing less than 0.5 milligrams per kilogram. Other research has put the overall range at roughly 2 to 251 micrograms of morphine per gram of seeds, with codeine ranging from 0.4 to 57.1 micrograms per gram. In other words, two muffins that look identical can carry wildly different amounts of opiates, and there is no way to tell by looking at them, tasting them, or reading the label. Washing and processing reduce the alkaloids, and raw, unwashed seeds can carry far more than baked ones — but the variation is baked into the product before it ever reaches a bakery shelf.

That variability is the first reason the poppy seed question has no single yes-or-no answer. It is also why researchers who study the problem are careful to say that there is no safe, universal number of seeds. A person could eat a bagel made with Turkish seeds and produce almost nothing measurable, while another person eating a cake made with unwashed seeds from a high-alkaloid batch could produce a urine concentration thousands of times higher. The United States Food and Drug Administration has taken the variability seriously enough that in January 2025 it asked the food industry for information about the practices that raise or lower opiate alkaloid levels in poppy seeds, after its own preliminary surveillance sampling found that the levels vary widely from batch to batch.

## What the Studies Actually Found

The poppy seed effect is not a rumour built on a single anecdote. It has been tested repeatedly, in several countries, with volunteers eating measured amounts of poppy seed foods and then providing urine samples at set intervals. The results are consistent: poppy seeds can push a person's urine morphine and codeine concentrations above the level that many drug tests use to declare a positive result.

One of the most cited experiments was conducted by researchers affiliated with Johns Hopkins University. Volunteers ate poppy seed products every day for ten days, and their urine was tested against the older, lower cutoff of 300 nanograms per millilitre. Under that standard, 45 percent of the samples tested positive for morphine and all of them tested positive for codeine. Peak urinary morphine in the research reviewed reached into the thousands of nanograms per millilitre, and in one individual as high as 13,854 nanograms per milligram of creatinine. Those are not borderline numbers. They are the kind of concentrations a laboratory would ordinarily associate with drug use, produced by nothing more exotic than breakfast food.

A German study from 2003, conducted by researchers at the Institute of Biochemistry, produced even more striking figures. Volunteers ate different quantities of an ordinary commercial poppy seed cake, and their urine was tested 24 hours later and again at 48 hours. At both time points, every participant tested positive for opiates, with concentrations reaching as high as 10 micrograms per millilitre — that is 10,000 nanograms per millilitre, five times the modern federal cutoff. For comparison, the International Olympic Committee's cutoff at the time stood at 1 microgram per millilitre, so the cake eaters exceeded the sporting threshold tenfold.

An earlier British study, by Meadway, George and Braithwaite in 1998, fed volunteers large amounts of poppy seeds from one or two poppy seed rolls and found high concentrations of normorphine, morphine, thebaine, norcodeine and codeine in their urine. The study also recorded something the other papers did not: the volunteer who ate the largest quantity of poppy seed cake relative to body weight reported a light drug effect, including drowsiness and a slower pupil reaction. The doses in food are far too small to produce the effects of morphine as a drug, and that single report is an outlier rather than a rule — but it underlines that the compounds in the seeds are pharmacologically real, not a laboratory artefact.

The numbers for ordinary servings are smaller but still meaningful. Research summarised by Medical News Today found that eating a poppy seed roll can produce urinary morphine concentrations of 155 to 1,408 nanograms per millilitre. A separate account of the older research reported that a single poppy seed bagel produces an opiate level of approximately 250 nanograms per millilitre three hours after eating, while ingesting three teaspoons of poppy seeds can produce a level of 1,200 nanograms per millilitre six hours later. Those figures sit below the modern federal cutoff of 2,000 nanograms per millilitre, which is exactly why the federal rules were changed — but they sit well above the 300 nanograms per millilitre cutoff that many clinical and private tests still use. The same bagel, in other words, can be a pass and a fail depending on who is running the test.

Television put the effect to its own test. On the Discovery Channel programme *MythBusters*, one participant ate a poppy seed cake and tested positive for opiates half an hour later, while another ate three poppy seed bagels and tested positive two hours later. Both continued to test positive for 16 hours. The demonstration was entertainment rather than a peer-reviewed trial, but its results lined up with the published literature: the effect is fast, it lasts well into the next day, and it does not take an absurd quantity of seeds to produce it.

## The Cutoff Is the Whole Story

If there is one idea that explains the entire poppy seed controversy, it is the cutoff. A drug test does not report the exact amount of a substance in a sample and leave the interpretation to the reader. It compares the measured concentration against a threshold, and anything at or above that threshold is reported as positive. The threshold is a policy choice, not a law of nature, and different testing programmes have chosen very different numbers. That single choice determines whether a bagel is a problem.

The history of the federal cutoff is the history of the poppy seed problem being recognised and then engineered around. Until December 1998, the federal workplace drug testing programme set its opiate cutoff at 300 nanograms per millilitre. At that level, the programme could not reliably tell a poppy seed eater from an opiate user, because a bagel could produce 250 nanograms per millilitre and a few spoonfuls of seeds could produce 1,200. In 1995, the Department of Health and Human Services proposed raising the morphine and codeine cutoffs from 300 to 2,000 nanograms per millilitre, precisely because people who had eaten poppy seeds — as well as people taking prescribed codeine or morphine — were testing positive. The change took effect in 1998, and the 2,000 nanograms per millilitre threshold has been the federal standard ever since, confirmed in the 2023 revision of the Mandatory Guidelines for Federal Workplace Drug Testing Programs, which took effect on 1 February 2024. That revision also raised the confirmatory cutoff for morphine to 4,000 nanograms per millilitre, while codeine stayed at 2,000.

The evidence that the change was needed came from the government's own data. A Department of Health and Human Services study examined 317,500 urine specimens tested for opiates and reviewed by three Medical Review Officer groups, along with 1.1 million specimens from five certified laboratories. Medical Review Officers reversed 87 percent of the positive urinalysis results, attributing them to false positives from poppy seed ingestion, prescription medication or other reasons. About 81 percent of the specimens that tested positive for codeine and morphine had concentration levels under 2,000 nanograms per millilitre — the level that later became the cutoff. In other words, under the old rules, the overwhelming majority of opiate positives were not drug users at all.

The federal numbers are only part of the picture, because most drug testing in America is not federal workplace testing. Clinical and private-sector programmes set their own thresholds, and the usual screening threshold in clinical monitoring is far lower: 300 nanograms per millilitre for morphine, hydrocodone and codeine. The goal is different — a pain clinic monitoring a patient wants to catch any opiate exposure at all, while a federal workplace programme wants to avoid accusing an innocent employee — so the same urine sample can be negative under one programme and positive under another. The United States military sets its cutoff higher still, at 3,000 nanograms per millilitre, specifically to avoid false positives. Oral fluid, or saliva, testing uses much lower numbers again — a federal screening cutoff of 30 nanograms per millilitre and a confirmation cutoff of 15 — because concentrations in saliva are far lower than in urine. The table makes the verdict almost self-evident. The claim that poppy seeds can make you fail a drug test is true under some testing regimes and false under others, and the regime you happen to be tested under is usually not something you get to choose. Federal workplace testing, which covers truck drivers, pilots, train engineers and federal employees, was redesigned around this exact problem. Clinical testing, pain management monitoring, probation screening and many private employers were not. A 2023 study found that poppy seed consumption can even produce codeine-only urine results, where morphine is absent or below the cutoff, and a 2024 case report described codeine-positive results in pain management monitoring that were traced to a seasoning containing poppy seeds. For a patient in a pain management programme, a positive codeine result can have serious consequences, even when the explanation is a spice jar.

## How Long Do the Traces Last?

Timing matters almost as much as the cutoff. Morphine and codeine from poppy seeds appear in urine quickly — within 30 minutes to two hours of eating, according to the *MythBusters* demonstration and the published studies — and published sources commonly say they remain detectable for up to about 48 hours after eating. If a person consumes large doses of seeds, traces may remain for up to 60 hours. The window is short compared with some other drugs, which is good news for anyone who ate a bagel on Monday and is tested on Thursday, but it is long enough to matter for a test taken the morning after a poppy seed dinner.

That timing is why the standard advice from medical sources is to avoid foods containing poppy seeds for at least three days before a drug test. Three days is a conservative buffer: it covers the 48-hour detection window with a day to spare, and it accounts for the fact that nobody knows how much opiate was in the particular seeds they ate. The advice is sometimes dismissed as overcautious, and for a federal workplace test it probably is — a bagel three days before a test was never going to cross 2,000 nanograms per millilitre. But for a clinical test with a 300 nanograms per millilitre cutoff, or for a pain management programme where any positive can trigger a difficult conversation, the buffer is cheap insurance. Skipping the poppy seed bagel for a few days costs nothing; explaining a positive result costs a great deal more.

There is also a second line of defence built into proper testing, and it is worth understanding because it is what separates a screening result from a verdict. A legitimate drug test has two stages. The first is an immunoassay screen, which is fast and cheap but cannot distinguish one opiate from another — it reacts to the class of compounds, not to the specific drug. The second is a confirmatory test, usually gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, or GC/MS, which identifies the exact molecules present. A screening positive that is not confirmed is not a positive result; it is a preliminary finding awaiting confirmation. The federal programme requires confirmation, and a Medical Review Officer — a licensed physician — reviews any confirmed positive before it is reported to an employer, precisely to catch cases like poppy seeds and prescription medication.

Confirmation also resolves the heroin question. Heroin breaks down in the body into a unique metabolite called 6-acetylmorphine, or 6-MAM, which appears only with heroin use and never from eating poppy seeds. The federal cutoff for 6-MAM is 10 nanograms per millilitre, and a confirmed 6-MAM finding is essentially diagnostic of heroin use. Poppy seeds cannot produce it, so a properly confirmed test can distinguish a bagel from heroin even when both produce morphine in the urine. The problem arises with cheap, single-stage tests — the kind sold over the counter or used in settings without confirmation — where a screening positive is treated as a final answer. In those settings, a poppy seed breakfast genuinely can be reported as a failed drug test, and the person tested may have no Medical Review Officer to appeal to.

## The Seinfeld Effect: How a Sitcom Made the Claim Famous

The poppy seed defence entered popular culture long before the science was settled, and it did so through a joke. The claim's most famous outing came in 1996, in the *Seinfeld* episode "The Shower Head", in which Elaine Benes fails a drug test after eating a poppy seed muffin for breakfast. The episode treated the excuse as a punchline — the kind of desperate story a character invents when the truth is unflattering — and for years afterwards, anyone who blamed a muffin was assumed to be quoting a sitcom rather than describing a real phenomenon.

What the episode's writers may not have known is that the story had already happened in real life, six years earlier. In 1990, a police officer in St. Louis with an exemplary work record underwent a random drug test and was suspended after it came back positive for opiate use. The officer's explanation was poppy seeds, and at the time it sounded exactly like the excuse it later became on television. Subsequent research vindicated him: the science that emerged over the following decade showed that ordinary poppy seed foods could indeed produce the concentrations found in his sample, under the testing standards then in force.

The sitcom and the science then fed each other for the next three decades. After the *Seinfeld* episode aired, researchers, journalists and television producers kept returning to the question, each time confirming the core effect while refining the details. The *MythBusters* demonstration in the 2000s gave the claim a visual proof that millions of viewers could follow: eat the cake, wait half an hour, watch the test change. The 2004 study of seed origins explained why the effect was so inconsistent from person to person. The 2023 and 2024 reports — the codeine-only findings and the pain management case report — showed that the problem had not gone away with the higher federal cutoff, because not every test uses that cutoff.

There is a lesson in that history that goes beyond poppy seeds. A claim can be a joke and a fact at the same time. The *Seinfeld* episode was funny precisely because the excuse sounded absurd, and it sounded absurd because most people had no idea that a muffin could contain morphine at all. The laughter was aimed at the gap between a breakfast food and a drug test, and the science eventually closed that gap: the breakfast food genuinely contains the drug test's target compounds, just in amounts that depend on details nobody at the breakfast table is tracking. The same pattern runs through many of the food and health claims we have examined on this site — from <a href="/blog/does-activated-charcoal-detox-your-body-fact-check/">whether activated charcoal can really detox your body</a> to <a href="/blog/does-lemon-water-detox-your-body-fact-check/">does lemon water detox your body</a> — where a simple yes-or-no question turns out to hide a measurement problem. The interesting question is rarely whether an effect exists; it is how large the effect is, and under what conditions it matters. Food safety questions behave the same way, which is why the details matter in our earlier check of <a href="/blog/is-it-safe-to-eat-raw-cookie-dough-fact-check/">is it safe to eat raw cookie dough</a>, where the risk also depended on dose, handling and the specific test applied.

## What to Do If You Test Positive

The practical advice that follows from the evidence is straightforward, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the part of this story that can save someone a job or a medical dispute. If you have a drug test coming up and the result matters — a pre-employment screening, a Department of Transportation test, a probation check, a pain management programme — avoid poppy seeds entirely for at least three days beforehand. That means bagels and muffins, but also rolls, cakes, breads, salad dressings and any seasoning blend that lists poppy seeds. The three-day window covers the 48-hour detection period with a margin for the unknown alkaloid content of the seeds you ate.

If you have already eaten poppy seeds and you are facing a test, tell the testing programme about your diet before the test where you can. Disclosure is not an admission of anything, and it is exactly the information a Medical Review Officer needs in order to interpret a result correctly. If a screening test comes back positive, ask for the cutoff values on any result you dispute, and ask whether the result was confirmed by GC/MS. A screening positive without confirmation is a preliminary finding, not a verdict, and in a properly run programme it should never be reported to an employer as a failure on its own. If confirmation testing is available, request it: it will show the absence of 6-acetylmorphine, the heroin metabolite, which poppy seeds cannot produce, and it will give the laboratory a precise concentration to compare against the relevant cutoff.

It is also worth knowing which test you are taking. A federal workplace test with a 2,000 nanograms per millilitre cutoff and a confirmation stage is a very different proposition from a cheap single-stage home test or a clinical screen with a 300 nanograms per millilitre cutoff. Reputable laboratories follow the federal mandatory cutoff levels for workplace testing, and their programmes include a Medical Review Officer review. If a test has no confirmation stage and no review process, its results should be treated with corresponding caution — by the person tested and by anyone relying on the result.

None of this is a licence to use the poppy seed defence dishonestly. The defence works, when it works, because the science is real and the numbers are documented. It fails when it is used to explain a concentration that food cannot produce — a morphine level far above anything a bagel could deliver, or the presence of 6-acetylmorphine, which points to heroin and nothing else. The same research that vindicates the innocent bagel eater also sets the limits of the excuse. A laboratory that follows the rules can tell the difference, and that is ultimately why the federal system was redesigned: not to let drug users hide behind breakfast, but to stop breakfast from being mistaken for drug use.

## The Verdict

Can poppy seeds make you fail a drug test? Yes — but with qualifications large enough to change the answer for most people. The seeds carry real morphine and codeine, left on them by the opium poppy's latex during harvest, in amounts that vary enormously by origin and processing — from less than 0.5 milligrams of morphine per kilogram in some Turkish seeds to 294 milligrams per kilogram in some American retail seeds. Controlled studies have shown those traces reaching urine concentrations of 155 to 1,408 nanograms per millilitre from a poppy seed roll, around 250 from a single bagel, 1,200 from three teaspoons of seeds, and as high as 10,000 in a German study of poppy seed cake. Under the old federal cutoff of 300 nanograms per millilitre, those numbers were enough to fail. Under the modern federal cutoff of 2,000 — and the morphine confirmation cutoff of 4,000 — an ordinary bagel is nowhere near failing, and only very heavy consumption of high-alkaloid or raw seeds approaches the line. But many clinical and private tests still use the 300 cutoff, and under those rules a poppy seed roll can genuinely produce a positive result.

That split is why the verdict is **Mostly True** rather than simply True or False. The claim is true in the sense that matters most: poppy seeds can cause a positive drug test, the effect is documented in peer-reviewed research, and real people have lost jobs and faced suspension over it. It is misleading in the form most people hear it — the idea that any poppy seed bagel will fail any drug test — because the federal workplace programme that governs the most consequential testing in the United States was specifically redesigned to prevent exactly that. The truth sits between the joke and the warning: the bagel is not an urban legend, and it is not a guaranteed failure either. It is a measurable, documented risk whose size depends on the seeds, the serving, the timing, and above all the number printed at the top of the laboratory's report.

So the next time someone blames a muffin, the right response is not laughter and not alarm. It is a question: which test? Because in the world of drug testing, the food on the plate is only half the story. The other half is the threshold, and the threshold is a human decision — one that was changed, in 1998, because a breakfast food kept failing it.
