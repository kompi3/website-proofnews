Scroll through any hair-care feed long enough and you will meet the same promise, delivered with total confidence: a few drops of rosemary oil, massaged into the scalp, will grow your hair back — naturally, cheaply, and just as well as minoxidil, the drugstore treatment that has dominated hair loss for decades. The videos are seductive. Before-and-after photos show fuller hairlines. Creators boil rosemary sprigs into "rosemary water" and spray it on like a miracle. Comment sections overflow with people swearing their shedding stopped within weeks.

The claim has travelled far beyond beauty TikTok. It now appears in magazine roundups, wellness newsletters, and product pages for serums and shampoos that borrow the language of clinical science. And unlike most viral health claims, this one is not built on nothing. There really is a published, peer-reviewed human trial that compared rosemary oil directly against minoxidil — and rosemary held its own. That single fact is the engine of the whole phenomenon.

But a claim can be built on a real study and still be misleading. The trial behind the rosemary craze was small, short, and designed in a way that cannot answer the question people actually care about: does rosemary oil grow hair better than doing nothing? It compared rosemary against the weaker strength of minoxidil, not the strength most people use today. It measured no change at all for the first three months. And the rest of the evidence — the mouse studies, the test-tube work, the 1990s aromatherapy trial — is either about a different condition, a different preparation, or a different species entirely.

This fact-check follows the evidence where it actually leads: through the one human trial, the older blend study, the animal research, and the newer commercial trials that the videos rarely mention. The short version is that rosemary oil is the most interesting natural hair product on the internet — and also the most oversold.

## The Claim Flooding Your Feed

The viral version of the claim usually comes in one of three forms. The strongest says rosemary oil "works just as well as minoxidil" — a drug — for growing hair. A softer version says rosemary oil "promotes hair growth" or "stimulates the follicles." The most casual version, the one in the videos, simply shows someone applying the oil and implies the results speak for themselves: thicker hair, less shedding, a fuller hairline, all from a plant.

Rosemary oil has become, in the words of one trichology practice, "probably the most shared hair tip online." The trend accelerated as creators began filming rosemary water — sprigs steeped in hot water, cooled, and sprayed onto the scalp — alongside the oil itself. The two preparations are routinely treated as interchangeable in the videos. They are not, and the distinction matters more than almost anything else in this story: the compounds that researchers think might matter live in the oil, not in the water.

The claim also borrows authority from real science in a specific way. Nearly every article, video caption, and product page that promotes rosemary for hair points to the same source: a 2015 clinical trial that put rosemary oil head-to-head against minoxidil. The study is real, it was published in a peer-reviewed journal, and its headline result is genuinely interesting. The problem is what happens between the study and the slogan. Each retelling drops a caveat. The "compared with 2% minoxidil" becomes "compared with minoxidil." The "in 100 people with pattern hair loss" becomes "in people." The "after six months" becomes "fast results." By the time the claim reaches a thirty-second video, it has shed every limitation that made the original result honest.

That pattern — a real study, aggressively simplified — is exactly what this fact-check is built to catch. So let us look at the study itself, in full, before deciding what it can and cannot support.

## What Rosemary Oil Actually Is

Rosemary, *Rosmarinus officinalis*, is a woody Mediterranean herb long used in cooking, perfumery, and folk medicine. The oil sold for hair care is an essential oil: a concentrated, volatile extract, usually produced by steam-distilling the leaves. It is potent stuff. Essential oils are not meant to be applied undiluted to skin, and rosemary oil is no exception — every responsible guide insists it must be mixed into a carrier oil, such as jojoba or grapeseed, before it touches the scalp.

That matters because the products in the claim are not one thing. There are at least three different preparations circulating under the single name "rosemary":

First, rosemary essential oil, diluted in a carrier oil and massaged into the scalp. This is what the 2015 trial tested, and it is the only preparation with any human trial behind it.

Second, rosemary leaf extract — a different preparation, containing non-volatile compounds from the leaf, including a molecule called 12-O-methylcarnosic acid. This is what the much-cited mouse study tested. Leaf extract and essential oil are not the same substance, and results from one cannot simply be transferred to the other.

Third, rosemary water — sprigs steeped in hot water, the version all over social media. The active compounds researchers associate with hair effects are fat-soluble and live in the oil, not the water, which makes rosemary water the weakest of the three by a wide margin. No human trial has tested rosemary water for hair growth at all.

Confusing these three is the first way the claim goes wrong. When a video cites the 2015 trial to sell rosemary water, it is borrowing evidence for a product the trial never tested. When a product page cites the mouse study to sell an essential oil, it is borrowing evidence for a different preparation in a different species.

Rosemary oil is also, in regulatory terms, a cosmetic or aromatherapy product — not a medicine. It is not approved to treat hair loss anywhere. That does not mean it does nothing. It means no regulator has ever reviewed evidence strong enough to authorise it as a treatment, and anyone selling it as one is ahead of the evidence.

## The One Human Trial Everyone Cites

The study behind almost every rosemary claim is Panahi and colleagues, published in the journal *SKINmed* in 2015: "Rosemary Oil vs Minoxidil 2% for the Treatment of Androgenetic Alopecia: A Randomized Comparative Trial." It is, to this day, the best human evidence anyone has for rosemary and hair. It is also the only randomised human trial that tested rosemary oil on its own against a recognised treatment. Understanding exactly what it did — and did not do — is the whole ballgame.

The researchers, based at Shiraz University in Iran, recruited 100 patients with androgenetic alopecia — the medical name for pattern hair loss, the common, largely genetic thinning that affects both men and women. They randomly assigned 50 people to rosemary oil and 50 to minoxidil 2%, applied twice daily for six months. Hair counts were measured from standardised close-up photographs of the scalp at the start of the trial, at three months, and at six months. The design was a randomised comparative trial: two active treatments, head to head.

Here is what happened. At three months, neither group showed a significant change in average hair count. Nothing measurable had happened — for the drug or for the oil. At six months, both groups showed a significant increase in hair count compared with where they started, and compared with the three-month measurement. And at neither time point was there a significant difference between the two groups: the rosemary group and the minoxidil group ended up in roughly the same place. On tolerability, scalp itching rose from baseline in both groups, but it was significantly more frequent in the minoxidil group at both check-ins — a known side effect of minoxidil's alcohol and propylene glycol base.

Read plainly, that is a genuinely encouraging result for a plant oil: in 100 people with pattern hair loss, six months of twice-daily rosemary oil produced hair-count gains comparable to six months of 2% minoxidil, with less itching. It is easy to see why the finding went viral.

But three features of the trial decide how much weight it can carry, and the viral claim drops all three.

The first is the missing placebo group. The trial had two arms — rosemary and minoxidil — and no untreated or vehicle-only group. That means it cannot show how much of either group's gain was caused by the treatment rather than by chance, by the passage of time, or by the simple fact that people in a trial tend to look after their hair more carefully. Both groups improved, and both groups improved by similar amounts. That is consistent with rosemary working as well as minoxidil — but it is equally consistent with both groups improving for reasons that had nothing to do with either product. A trial without a placebo arm cannot distinguish those two stories. It is a comparison, not a proof of efficacy.

The second is the comparator. The trial tested rosemary against minoxidil 2%, the lower strength, generally reserved for women or for people who cannot tolerate the stronger dose. Men with pattern hair loss are usually advised to use minoxidil 5%. In a separate 48-week trial of 393 men, published by Olsen and colleagues in the *Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology* in 2002, the 5% solution produced 45% more hair regrowth than the 2% solution. No published trial has ever compared rosemary oil against 5% minoxidil. So even if we accept the 2015 result at face value, the claim it supports is narrow: rosemary matched the weaker strength of minoxidil, in one small trial, over six months. The viral version — "as good as minoxidil," full stop — quietly upgrades the opponent to the stronger drug that was never tested.

The third is size and singularity. One hundred people, one centre, six months, one trial. Minoxidil's evidence base runs to decades of large-scale, randomised, placebo-controlled trials across diverse populations, and regulatory approval in multiple countries. Rosemary's human evidence base is this single study. A single small study is a reason to run better trials, not a body of evidence. It is a promising signal, not a verdict.

None of this makes the 2015 trial worthless. It makes it preliminary — the kind of result that justifies a bigger, better trial with a placebo group, not the kind of result that justifies telling millions of people that a kitchen-cupboard oil replaces a medicine.

## The Other Studies in the Story

The 2015 trial is not the only study the rosemary claim leans on, but the supporting cast is weaker than it looks — and in one case, it is not about rosemary alone at all.

The oldest human study comes from Scotland. In 1998, Hay and colleagues at Aberdeen Royal Infirmary published a randomised, double-blind, controlled trial in the *Archives of Dermatology* that tested aromatherapy for alopecia areata — a different condition from pattern hair loss, an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks hair follicles, causing patchy bald spots. Eighty-six patients massaged their scalps daily for seven months with either a blend of four essential oils — thyme, rosemary, lavender, and cedarwood — in jojoba and grapeseed carrier oils, or with the carrier oils alone. The result was striking: 19 of 43 patients in the active group, 44%, showed improvement, compared with 6 of 41, 15%, in the control group, a difference the authors reported as statistically significant. They concluded, in their own words, that "the results show aromatherapy to be a safe and effective treatment for alopecia areata."

It is a real, well-conducted trial, and it remains the strongest evidence for essential oils in patchy autoimmune hair loss. But it cannot support the viral claim for two reasons. It tested a blend of four oils, not rosemary alone, so there is no way to credit rosemary specifically for the result. And it tested alopecia areata, not the pattern hair loss that most people buying rosemary oil actually have. A treatment that helps one kind of hair loss does not automatically help another; the two conditions have different causes, different mechanisms, and different treatments.

Then there is the mouse study, the one that supplies the "mechanism" in most rosemary marketing. In 2013, Murata and colleagues published a study in *Phytotherapy Research* in which a rosemary leaf extract improved hair regrowth in mice whose hair growth had been suppressed with testosterone. The researchers linked the effect to a compound in the extract, 12-O-methylcarnosic acid, which appeared to interfere with the hormonal pathway that drives pattern hair loss. It is elegant laboratory work. It is also work in mice, using a leaf extract rather than the essential oil people buy, with hair loss artificially induced by hormones. Mouse and test-tube results routinely fail to carry over to people — that is precisely why human trials exist — and this one has never been replicated in humans.

A 2026 mouse pilot mentioned in recent reviews underlines the point: it used just three mice per group, and its own authors said the results could not be applied directly to people. Small animal studies are where hypotheses are born, not where treatments are proven.

## The Newer Trials — and Their Limits

The evidence has not stood still since 2015, and honesty requires saying so. Two newer studies are now cited by rosemary sellers, and both deserve a careful look rather than a dismissal.

The first is a 2025 double-blind, randomised, three-arm, placebo-controlled trial of a commercial rosemary-based formulation, sold under the brand name Rosmagain, run over 90 days. As reported in trade coverage, the formulation increased hair growth rate by up to 57.73% and reduced hair fall by more than 40%. On its face, that sounds like the placebo-controlled evidence the field has been missing. The catch is in the details: it tested a branded commercial formulation, not plain rosemary oil; the headline percentages describe growth rate and shedding in a short, 90-day window; and the product itself is the thing being sold. A company-funded trial of a company's own formulation is not worthless, but it is not the same as independent evidence for rosemary oil in general, and its results have not yet been replicated by anyone without a stake in them.

The second comes from India, also in 2025: a double-blind trial in which 90 adults with thinning hair and noticeable shedding applied rosemary-lavender oil, rosemary-castor oil, or plain coconut oil to the scalp for 90 days. The rosemary-lavender group reportedly saw hair density rise by about 32% and shedding fall by more than 40%, significantly better than coconut oil. Again, a real result — and again, with a caveat that matters: the study was funded by the company that makes the oils being tested. Funding does not falsify a result, but it is exactly the kind of detail that should travel with the number, and in the viral retellings it never does.

A 2025 systematic review of 16 randomised controlled trials of herbal hair-loss remedies named rosemary among the plants with promising results for hair growth, hair density, and reduced shedding. That is a fair summary of where things stand: promising. Not proven. The review's own framing is that the evidence is preliminary and the trials are small.

Put together, the newer research strengthens the case that rosemary is worth studying. It does not change the verdict on the viral claim, because the viral claim is not "rosemary is worth studying." The viral claim is "rosemary grows hair as well as minoxidil," and nothing published since 2015 has tested that claim against the stronger minoxidil, against a placebo, in a large independent trial.

## Why "As Good As Minoxidil" Is a Stretch

It is worth pausing on the logic, because the error here is a common one and it shows up in health claims far beyond hair care. The 2015 trial found "no significant difference" between rosemary and minoxidil 2%. The viral claim reads that as "rosemary equals minoxidil." Those are not the same statement.

"No significant difference" is a statement about what a study could detect, not about what is true. In a trial of 100 people, a real difference between two treatments can easily hide inside the statistical noise. Failing to find a difference is not the same as proving there is none — and proving two treatments are equivalent requires a specially designed equivalence trial with a pre-set margin, a larger sample, and a placebo arm to prove the trial could detect a difference at all. The 2015 study was none of those things. It was a comparative trial that found both groups improved and could not separate them. That is interesting. It is not equivalence.

There is a second logical slip in the viral claim: it treats "matched 2% minoxidil" as "matched minoxidil." But minoxidil is not one thing. The 2% solution is the weaker formulation, and the gap between strengths is large — 45% more regrowth for 5% over 2% in the Olsen trial. The formulation most men are actually prescribed was never in the ring with rosemary at all. Claiming victory over "minoxidil" without naming the strength is like claiming to have beaten a football team without saying whether it was the first team or the reserves.

And there is a third slip, quieter but just as important: the timeline. The 2015 trial found nothing measurable at three months in either group. Gains appeared only at six months. Hair grows slowly — roughly a centimetre a month — and no treatment, drug or botanical, can make new growth visible faster than the hair cycle allows. The videos promising visible regrowth in weeks are not just ahead of the evidence; they are ahead of human biology. Anyone who used rosemary for a month, saw nothing, and quit was behaving rationally. Anyone who used it for a month, thought they saw results, and posted about it was almost certainly seeing something else: better lighting, a fresh haircut, less shedding from the simple act of massaging the scalp, or the ordinary variation of a condition that waxes and wanes on its own.

## What the Claim Gets Wrong About Hair

Part of why the rosemary claim spreads so easily is that most people have a shaky picture of how hair actually works, and the claim fills the gaps with a satisfying story: blocked follicles, poor circulation, a natural oil that "stimulates" growth. Some of that story has a grain of truth. Rosemary oil is thought to improve microcirculation in the scalp, and the mouse research suggests a compound in rosemary leaf may interfere with the conversion of testosterone to DHT, the hormone that shrinks follicles in pattern hair loss. But a plausible mechanism is not a proven treatment. Plenty of substances do interesting things in a petri dish and nothing at all on a human head.

The basics matter more than the mechanism. Losing 50 to 100 hairs a day is normal, according to the UK's National Health Service — hair sheds, and shedding alone is not hair loss. Pattern hair loss, the condition the 2015 trial studied, is driven mainly by genetics and hormones; it progresses over years, not weeks. Telogen effluvium — the diffuse shedding that follows stress, illness, childbirth, or a crash diet — is a different condition again, and rosemary has no trial evidence for it at all. Stress does affect hair in real ways, as we found when we checked <a href="/blog/can-stress-turn-your-hair-gray-fact-check/">can stress turn your hair gray</a>, but the mechanism there is the gradual depletion of pigment stem cells, not something an oil can reverse in weeks. Postpartum shedding, the kind many new mothers experience, is telogen effluvium, not pattern loss, and the rosemary videos aimed at new mothers are selling a treatment tested on a different disease.

This is why a clinician's first step with hair loss is diagnosis, not a product. The cause decides the treatment. Pattern loss, autoimmune patchy loss, stress shedding, thyroid problems, iron deficiency, and scarring conditions all look like "thinning hair" from the outside and are treated differently. An oil cannot diagnose, and a viral video cannot tell you which condition you have.

## Safety: The Part the Videos Skip

The rosemary claim is usually presented as risk-free — it is natural, so it must be safe. That is the naturalistic fallacy, and with essential oils it is a genuinely bad idea. Rosemary oil is concentrated, and concentrated plant oils are among the more common causes of allergic contact dermatitis: an itchy, red, sometimes blistering rash where the oil touched the skin. Dermatology references note that allergic reactions to such products usually appear 24 to 72 hours after contact, which means a quick one-hour patch test is not enough — a proper test needs a full day or two of waiting before the oil goes anywhere near the whole scalp.

The other safety rule the videos routinely skip is dilution. Essential oil must be mixed into a carrier oil before it touches skin, and even then it should be used in small amounts. Undiluted essential oil on the scalp can irritate, sensitise, or burn, and once skin is sensitised to a substance, the allergy tends to be permanent. The irony is sharp: a product bought to save hair can, misused, inflame the very scalp it was meant to help.

None of this means rosemary oil is dangerous when used properly. Diluted, patch-tested, and used as a cosmetic, it is a pleasant and low-risk product for most people. But "low-risk when used properly" is a different claim from "completely safe," and the videos that never mention dilution or patch testing are leaving out the two instructions that matter most.

It is also worth saying plainly who should not take the claim at face value: anyone with sudden, patchy, or rapidly worsening hair loss, anyone with a sore, scarring, or inflamed scalp, and anyone whose hair loss came with other symptoms should see a clinician first. Persistent thinning belongs with a doctor before it belongs with a bottle. That is not caution for its own sake — it is because some causes of hair loss are signs of something else, and months spent massaging oil into the scalp are months in which a treatable cause goes untreated.

## The Verdict

So, does rosemary oil grow hair? The honest answer is narrower, and less exciting, than the viral claim — and that is precisely why the claim is misleading rather than simply false.

There is a real, peer-reviewed human trial in which rosemary oil produced hair-count gains comparable to 2% minoxidil over six months, with less scalp itching. That is more than most viral remedies can offer, and it is why rosemary deserves to be called the best-studied natural option rather than dismissed as a myth. But the trial was small, had no placebo group, tested only the weaker strength of minoxidil, and found nothing at three months. The older human study tested a four-oil blend on a different condition. The mechanism work was done in mice, with a different preparation. And the newer trials tested commercial formulations, in short windows, with industry funding. None of it adds up to proof that rosemary oil grows hair — and none of it justifies the slogan "as good as minoxidil."

**Verdict: Misleading.** The claim takes one small, preliminary trial and inflates it into a proven treatment, drops the missing placebo group, the weaker comparator, and the six-month timeline, and then applies the result to preparations — rosemary water, home blends, shampoos — that no trial has ever tested. Rosemary oil is a promising cosmetic with a genuinely interesting signal behind it. It is not a proven hair-growth treatment, and it is not a replacement for minoxidil.

## What To Do Instead

If you are losing hair and want something that works, the evidence points in a clear direction — and it starts with a diagnosis, not a shopping list. See a GP or dermatologist first, find out which kind of hair loss you have, and then choose a treatment matched to it. For pattern hair loss, minoxidil 5% remains the topical treatment with the strongest evidence base: decades of large, placebo-controlled trials and regulatory approval. Oral finasteride is an option for many men, under medical supervision. Both require commitment — minoxidil only works while you keep using it, and stopping means losing the gains — and both deserve a conversation with a clinician about side effects, not a decision made from a video.

If you still want to try rosemary oil, treat it as what it is: a cosmetic experiment, not a treatment. Dilute it properly in a carrier oil. Patch-test on a small area of skin and wait 24 to 72 hours before using it more widely. Use it consistently for at least six months before judging it — the trial found nothing at three — and do not stop any prescribed treatment to make room for it. Keep expectations modest: the best available evidence says it might help somewhat, in pattern hair loss, over half a year, and that "might" is doing a lot of work. Hair supplements attract similar overclaiming — we have also checked <a href="/blog/does-creatine-cause-hair-loss-fact-check/">does creatine cause hair loss</a>, another hair claim where the evidence turned out thinner than the headlines.

And if someone tells you rosemary water regrew their hair in three weeks, remember what the evidence actually says: nothing changed at three months, in either group, in the only trial that ever tested the oil properly. Hair biology does not move at the speed of a viral video. The claim did — and that, more than anything, is why it needs checking. Hair is full of stories that sound true until the evidence is weighed: we found the same pattern when we checked <a href="/blog/can-hair-turn-white-overnight-fact-check/">can hair turn white overnight</a>, a dramatic claim that collapsed once the biology of the hair shaft was understood.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** One small 2015 trial found rosemary oil matched 2% minoxidil for pattern hair loss over six months, with less scalp itching — a genuinely interesting result. But the trial had no placebo group, so it cannot prove rosemary beats doing nothing; it tested only the weaker strength of minoxidil, never the 5% formulation most people use; and it found nothing at all for the first three months. The rest of the evidence is a four-oil blend tested on a different condition, a mouse study using a different preparation, and short commercial trials with industry funding. Rosemary oil is the most promising natural hair product on the internet — and the claim that it grows hair as well as minoxidil is still an overstatement.
