It arrives in a glass Mason jar with a thick cream line floating on top, photographed in golden morning light. The caption promises that this — milk straight from the cow, never heated, never "processed" — is what dairy was meant to be, and that the pasteurized carton in your refrigerator is a depleted, even dangerous, imitation. Across social media, raw milk has become a badge of wellness rebellion: fitness coaches pour it into protein shakes, parenting influencers say their children stopped getting sick once they switched, and comment sections fill with converts describing better digestion, clearer skin, and fewer colds.

The promise is seductive because it is simple. Modern food made milk worse; going backward — to the farm, to the udder, to "nature" — makes it better. Health authorities, from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), have been warning for years that the opposite is true: raw milk is one of the riskiest foods you can drink, and pasteurization is what made milk safe in the first place. Yet sales keep climbing. Data from NielsenIQ, reported in early 2026, showed U.S. raw milk sales rising between 20 and 65 percent year over year — growth that continued even as a bird flu outbreak swept through American dairy cattle. So which side is right? We spent weeks digging into the studies, the outbreak records, and the specific claims. The answer is more interesting — and more cautionary — than either camp's slogan.

## The Viral Claim

Strip away the aesthetics and the claim is usually some version of this: raw milk is healthier than pasteurized milk because heating destroys the good stuff — enzymes, vitamins, "natural probiotics," immune factors — while leaving the nutrition intact. The strongest versions go further. Raw milk, advocates say, can cure or prevent lactose intolerance, heal asthma and allergies, fix gut problems, build immunity against infections (in 2024 and 2025, some even claimed it could confer immunity to bird flu), and taste so much better that your body is clearly telling you something.

Each clause is worth examining on its own, because a claim can be partly true in the details and false in the conclusion. That is exactly what we find here. There are kernels of real science buried in the raw milk story — most notably, a genuine, peer-reviewed observation about farm milk and childhood asthma that researchers are still trying to understand. But the leap from those kernels to "raw milk is healthier" does not survive contact with the evidence, and the same studies that provide the movement's best talking points also come with warnings the movement prefers not to quote.

## What Pasteurization Actually Does

To judge the claim, it helps to know what pasteurization is and what it is not. Pasteurization is simply heating milk to a specific temperature for a set period of time to kill disease-causing microorganisms. The most common method — high-temperature, short-time pasteurization — heats milk to at least 161 degrees Fahrenheit for 15 seconds, then cools it rapidly. Homogenization, the separate process that keeps cream from separating, is a different treatment entirely; milk can be pasteurized without being homogenized, and vice versa.

The process targets a grim roster of pathogens: the bacteria responsible for listeriosis, salmonellosis, campylobacteriosis, typhoid fever, tuberculosis, diphtheria, and brucellosis. For more than a century, pasteurization has been recognized around the world as an essential tool for protecting public health. Before it became standard, milk was one of the leading causes of foodborne illness and infant death — raw milk could carry tuberculosis bacteria from infected cows' udders straight into children's glasses. In the United States, the FDA has required since 1987 that all milk packaged for human consumption be pasteurized before being shipped across state lines; many states additionally restrict or ban raw milk sales within their own borders.

Here is the part that the "heating destroys nutrition" narrative gets wrong: research has shown that these claims are myths. There is no meaningful nutritional difference between pasteurized and raw milk. Pasteurization preserves milk's protein, calcium, and other major nutrients while greatly reducing the risk of foodborne illness. The heat does cause tiny losses in a few heat-sensitive vitamins, but milk is not a meaningful source of those vitamins to begin with, and the losses are nutritionally trivial compared with the pathogens destroyed. Put bluntly by the FDA's own fact sheet on the subject: "There is no meaningful nutritional difference between pasteurized and raw milk, and raw milk does not contain compounds that will kill harmful bacteria." That second clause matters too, because one of the movement's favorite defenses — that raw milk is "inherently antimicrobial" and therefore needs no pasteurization — has also been shown to be a myth.

## The Kernel of Truth: Farm Milk and Asthma

This is where the story gets genuinely interesting, and where the raw milk movement found its most respectable piece of ammunition. In 2011, a large European research project called the GABRIELA study published findings in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology that surprised even the researchers. Surveying more than 8,300 school-aged children across rural Germany, Austria, and Switzerland — and analyzing 800 actual milk samples collected from participants' homes — the team found that children who drank raw farm milk had lower rates of asthma and allergies than children who drank shop-bought milk. The numbers were striking: raw milk consumption was inversely associated with asthma (an adjusted odds ratio of 0.59), with atopy (0.74), and with hay fever (0.51), independent of other farm exposures.

Even more specific: the protection did not seem to come from the milk's bacteria or its fat content. It tracked instead with whey proteins — BSA, alpha-lactalbumin, and beta-lactoglobulin — heat-sensitive proteins whose protective association withered when milk was boiled. Boiled farm milk, the study noted, showed no protective effect at all. Later European studies, including the PARSIFAL project and the PASTURE birth cohort, pointed in the same general direction.

Raw milk advocates present this as proof. But read what the researchers and medical bodies actually concluded, and the proof dissolves into something much narrower. The GABRIELA data were observational — retrospective questionnaire data about what children drank, correlated with health outcomes. As the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology summarized the situation, epidemiologic findings of this kind "stimulate hypothesis generation but are not sufficient for policy decisions," and the investigators themselves acknowledged that the mechanism of any protective effect is uncertain. Critically, the academy emphasized that "the benefits of unboiled cow's milk ingestion would need to be balanced against the potential risks" — risks the study was never designed to measure. Correlation in a rural European farming population, where children are exposed to livestock, hay, endotoxins, and a hundred other microbial influences that city dwellers never encounter, is not the same as a causal finding about the milk in a bottle shipped from a dairy in another state. And no controlled trial has ever shown that drinking raw milk treats or prevents asthma in the general population. The most honest reading of the asthma research is that it identified a fascinating lead — perhaps a whey-derived compound worth isolating and studying — not a dietary recommendation. When wellness accounts cite these studies as a reason to drink raw milk, they are using science's homework to write conclusions the scientists did not reach.

## The Risks Are Not Theoretical

Whatever the whey-protein story eventually yields, it sits alongside a far larger and far grimmer body of evidence: the outbreak record. From 1998 through 2018, the CDC documented 202 outbreaks linked to raw milk consumption in the United States, resulting in 2,645 people becoming ill and 228 hospitalizations. A 2017 study comparing unpasteurized and pasteurized dairy found that unpasteurized products cause 840 times more illnesses and 45 times more hospitalizations than pasteurized products. Raw milk can carry Salmonella, E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes, and Campylobacter, organisms that enter milk through an infected animal's udder, through manure, through milking equipment, or simply from the farm environment. Careful farming and sanitation can reduce contamination, but they cannot guarantee that raw milk is free from pathogens. As health agencies repeatedly stress: raw milk, no matter how carefully produced, may be unsafe.

Children bear the heaviest share of the harm. Illness outbreaks tied to raw milk have become more common over the past two decades, and children account for nearly half of those who get sick. E. coli strains in raw milk can cause hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), a kidney-destroying complication that strikes young children hardest. In February 2024, raw cheddar from California's Raw Farm was linked to an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak that sickened 11 people across five states, hospitalized five, and left two with HUS. In October 2025, aged raw milk cheese from Washington's Twin Sisters Creamery was tied to an E. coli outbreak that sickened 10 people in Washington and Oregon. These are not hypothetical risks discussed in journals; they are emergency rooms, dialysis machines, and frightened parents.

Certain people face disproportionate danger: children under five, adults over 65, pregnant people, and anyone with a weakened immune system. During pregnancy, raw dairy infections including listeriosis can cross to the baby, and Listeria can cause miscarriage or fatal newborn illness even when the mother feels fine. "Pasteurization goes a long way in preventing the illnesses associated with raw milk," says Cleveland Clinic dietitian Anna Rossi, summarizing the consensus of the FDA, the CDC, and every major pediatric and medical organization that has examined the question.

## Bird Flu Added a New Chapter

In 2024, raw milk acquired a new and especially alarming risk. H5N1 avian influenza — the "bird flu" virus — began spreading through dairy cattle in the United States, infecting cows through their udders and appearing at high concentrations in raw milk. The FDA responded with an urgent letter to state officials, recommending that states stop the sale of raw milk from herds that had tested positive for the virus and expand surveillance testing of dairy herds producing unpasteurized milk. Pasteurization was shown to kill the virus, but there was a genuine chance that the live virus could survive in raw milk and transmit infection. The concern was not abstract: cats on infected farms died after drinking raw milk, and several dairy workers became infected through on-the-job exposure.

As of early 2026, the human cases of H5N1 tied to the dairy outbreak had been confined to workers with direct animal contact, and commercial pasteurized milk remained safe. But the episode exposed something telling about the raw milk marketplace: sales did not dip. NielsenIQ data showed raw milk sales climbing 20 to 65 percent even in the middle of the dairy cattle outbreak, while some influencers went so far as to claim that drinking raw milk could build immunity against H5N1 — a claim with no scientific basis whatsoever. When a new, credible biological threat appears in raw milk and the market's response is to drink more of it, that is not a health movement. It is a loyalty movement.

## Why the Individual Claims Fall Apart

Take the specific health claims one by one, and a pattern emerges: each contains either a misunderstanding of biology or an extrapolation the evidence cannot support.

**"Raw milk has more nutrients."** False as stated. Pasteurized milk provides the same protein, fat, and vitamins as raw milk. The differences measured in laboratories are nutritionally meaningless — the kind of small vitamin variations that vanish next to the difference between drinking one glass or one and a quarter.

**"Raw milk is full of beneficial probiotic bacteria."** The AAP addressed this directly in its 2025 fact-check: "Advocates of raw milk often falsely claim it contains beneficial bacteria that promote gastrointestinal health. However, the bacteria present in raw milk are typically not probiotic and can include harmful pathogens." The bacteria in raw milk are whatever happened to be in the barn — including, often, the organisms that cause bloody diarrhea and kidney failure. No one would market that as a probiotic.

**"Raw milk cures lactose intolerance."** This one misunderstands the condition entirely. Lactose intolerance is caused by the body producing too little lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar. Drinking milk with bacteria in it does not supply the drinker with a working enzyme system, and the FDA's debunking materials list this claim among the movement's standard misconceptions. Anybody whose digestion genuinely improves on switching milks is far more likely responding to the placebo effect, a change in overall diet, or simply drinking less milk.

**"Raw milk cures or prevents asthma and allergies."** This is the claim with the real studies behind it — and, as discussed above, the studies show an observational association in farm children, linked to heat-sensitive whey proteins, that has never been confirmed in trials and that the researchers themselves did not translate into a recommendation. It also ignores a crucial detail: whatever protective fraction exists in raw milk, it would have to survive being shipped, stored, and poured into a glass by someone who is not a Bavarian farm child — and even then, the effect would have to outweigh a pathogen risk that is 840 times higher. No health authority on Earth has concluded that the trade-off favors the milk.

**"Raw milk is natural, and natural is safer."** This is the claim's emotional engine, and it is not a scientific argument at all. Arsenic is natural. Tuberculosis bacteria in a cow's udder are natural. Pasteurization is a technology, yes — but it is a technology that made one of history's most dangerous everyday foods into one of its safest. "Natural" describes where something comes from, not whether it will harm you.

## What the Experts Actually Say

The expert consensus on raw milk is about as close to unanimous as public health gets. The CDC, FDA, USDA, American Medical Association, American Academy of Pediatrics, American Public Health Association, National Environmental Health Association, and the National Public Health Information Coalition all advise against drinking raw milk — with the warnings strongest for infants, children, pregnant and elderly people, and the immunocompromised. As AAP infectious disease chair Robert Frenck put it: "Pasteurized milk provides all the nutritional benefits of milk while eliminating harmful bacteria that can cause severe illness."

Note what is absent from that consensus: not a single major health body recommends raw milk for any health benefit. The movement's case rests entirely on social media testimonials, cherry-picked readings of observational studies, and the aesthetic authority of the Mason jar. That asymmetry should tell you something. When one side has the CDC, the FDA, the pediatricians, and the outbreak data, and the other side has influencers and vibes, the burden of proof is not close to being met.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: Misleading.**

The claim that "raw milk is healthier than pasteurized milk" is misleading because it wraps a handful of real but narrow observations in a conclusion the evidence does not support. It is true that pasteurization slightly alters heat-sensitive whey proteins, and that large European observational studies found an intriguing association between raw farm milk consumption and lower childhood asthma rates — an association researchers have not confirmed in trials and have explicitly said is insufficient for policy decisions. It is true that raw milk can taste different, with a cream line and a richness that pasteurization's sibling process, homogenization, removes. None of that makes it healthier.

What the evidence actually shows is this: there is no meaningful nutritional difference between raw and pasteurized milk; the "beneficial bacteria" in raw milk are typically not probiotic; the lactose-intolerance and immunity claims are unfounded; and raw milk carries a dramatically higher risk of serious foodborne illness — 840 times more illnesses and 45 times more hospitalizations than pasteurized dairy, plus a new and still-evolving bird flu risk. The honest version of the claim would read: "Raw milk tastes the way milk used to taste, may contain heat-sensitive proteins of unproven benefit, and is substantially more likely to make you — and especially your children — seriously ill." That is not a health claim. It is a warning label.

If you enjoy dairy, drink pasteurized milk. You will get every nutrient the cow put there, and you will not be gambling with E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria, Campylobacter, or H5N1 to get it. The cream line in the Mason jar is real. Everything else in the caption is marketing — and the fine print, written in outbreak reports and hospital records, tells a very different story.
