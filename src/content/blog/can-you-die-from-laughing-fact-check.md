"Dying of laughter" is one of the oldest jokes in the English language. We say it after a good punchline, after a friend's story lands perfectly, after a comedy sketch catches us off guard. It is hyperbole, a figure of speech, and nobody in the room takes it literally. Except that, on a handful of occasions across history, people really have died while laughing — and in at least one modern case, doctors believe the laughter itself was the trigger that stopped a heart.

The most famous of those cases happened on an ordinary evening in England in 1975. A 50-year-old bricklayer sat down after dinner to watch his favourite television comedy, laughed for about twenty-five minutes, and never got up from the sofa again. Fourteen years later, a Danish doctor laughed through a screening of a hit comedy film and suffered a fatal heart attack in his cinema seat. These are not urban legends invented for the internet age. They were reported in newspapers at the time, investigated by doctors, and in the first case, revisited nearly four decades later when a family diagnosis finally explained what had happened.

So is the old saying true? Can a person really die from laughing? The honest answer is more interesting than a simple yes or no. Laughter is, for the overwhelming majority of people, harmless — and research suggests it is even good for the heart. But laughter is also a physical event. It shakes the chest, changes blood pressure, alters breathing, and floods the body with adrenaline. In a person carrying a hidden weakness — a faulty heart rhythm, a bulging artery in the brain, severe asthma, a seizure disorder — that shaking can be the final push. The laughter does not kill a healthy person. It can, in extraordinarily rare circumstances, kill a person whose body was already on the edge.

This is the story of the people who laughed themselves to death, what doctors found when they looked closer, and why the rest of us can keep laughing without worry.

## The Night a Comedy Show Killed a Man

On the evening of 24 March 1975, Alex Mitchell, a 50-year-old bricklayer from King's Lynn in Norfolk, England, settled into his usual routine. After dinner he sat down with his wife, Nessie, to watch his favourite television programme: *The Goodies*, a BBC comedy series built on surreal sketches and slapstick. That night's episode was called "Kung Fu Kapers", and it featured the show's stars performing a spoof martial art called "Ecky Thump", in which combatants pelted each other with black puddings and defended themselves with bagpipes.

Mitchell found it hysterical. According to his wife, who was watching beside him, he laughed and laughed and could not stop. The sketch went on, the laughter went on, and after about twenty-five minutes of continuous, convulsing laughter, Mitchell slumped on the settee and died of heart failure. His wife later wrote a letter to the three stars of *The Goodies* — Tim Brooke-Taylor, Graeme Garden and Bill Oddie — thanking them for making her husband's final half-hour so enjoyable.

The story made headlines around the world. A man had, quite literally, died laughing at a television show. For decades it stood as the single best-documented case of death by laughter in the modern era, repeated in newspapers, trivia books and television documentaries. But the story had a second act, and it would take thirty-seven years to arrive.

In May 2012, Mitchell's granddaughter, 23-year-old Lisa Corke, suffered a near-fatal cardiac arrest at home on the Isle of Sheppey in Kent. Doctors diagnosed her with Long QT Syndrome, an inherited condition in which the heart's electrical system misfires, causing the heartbeat to become dangerously irregular when the person is under physical or emotional stress — such as laughing without stopping for twenty-five minutes. Once the family connection was made, cardiologists concluded that Mitchell had almost certainly suffered from the same condition. Consultant cardiologist Dr Pier Lambiase explained at the time that the circumstances of the death were good circumstantial evidence: families with Long QT Syndrome often have unexplained deaths that were put down to ordinary heart attacks at the time, when the inherited condition was the real cause.

Mitchell's death, in other words, was real — but the laughter was the trigger, not the underlying cause. He was, in the words of one later account, a man with a rare heart condition in which excitement can adversely affect the electrical activity of the heart, precipitating a cardiac arrest. The joke did not kill him. The joke found the one weakness his body could not survive.

## A Cinema Seat, a Comedy Film, and a Stopped Heart

Fourteen years after Mitchell's death, laughter claimed another victim in a darkened cinema. In 1989, Ole Bentzen, a Danish audiologist, went to see the hit comedy *A Fish Called Wanda*, a heist farce starring John Cleese, Jamie Lee Curtis, Kevin Kline and Michael Palin. During a scene in which Kevin Kline's character shoves French fries up Michael Palin's nose, Bentzen began to laugh — and did not stop. He suffered a heart attack in his seat and died.

The official cause of death was recorded as heart fibrillation, which may have been caused by an increased heart rate due to extended laughter. Newspapers at the time reported that he had died from laughter, and the story has been retold ever since as the second great modern case of death by comedy. John Cleese himself considered using the event for publicity for the film, then decided that doing so would be in bad taste.

Bentzen's case is often presented as the cleaner of the two famous deaths, because unlike Mitchell, he was thought to have been in perfect health. But "perfect health" is a phrase that hides a great deal. A heart can carry a silent weakness for decades without ever announcing itself — an undiagnosed rhythm disorder, a narrowed artery, a structural defect — until the day something stresses it beyond its limit. A fit of prolonged, violent laughter is one of the most intense physical stresses the body can experience without moving a muscle: the chest heaves, the diaphragm spasms, blood pressure swings, and the heart races. For most of us, that is a workout with a punchline. For someone with a hidden cardiac flaw, it can be the last one.

## The Ancient Deaths That Started the Legend

The idea that laughter can kill is far older than television. It stretches back to the ancient world, where a string of famous men were said to have died laughing — and where the stories, told and retold for centuries, blur the line between history and legend.

The best known is Chrysippus, the influential Stoic philosopher of the third century BC, known to later writers as "the man who died from laughing at his joke". According to the story, Chrysippus watched a donkey eating his fermented figs, told a slave to give the animal undiluted wine to wash them down, and then laughed so hard at his own joke that he died. A century and a half earlier, the Greek painter Zeuxis was said to have died laughing at the humorous way in which he had painted an old woman. In 1410, King Martin of Aragon was said to have died from a combination of indigestion and uncontrollable laughter. In 1556, the Italian writer Pietro Aretino was said to have died of suffocation from laughing too much — though the more mundane truth, historians now suspect, is that he died from a stroke or a heart attack.

The pattern continues into the modern era, with cases that are better documented but still carry a whiff of the tall tale. On 22 June 1910, Thomas Henry Brown, a saloon employee from Marysville, Montana, spotted two nickels and a dime on the floor of his saloon and began laughing continuously at his good fortune for several minutes, until he fell to his death after his last laugh. In 1920, Arthur Cobcroft, a 56-year-old dog trainer from Leichhardt, Australia, was reading a five-year-old newspaper, was amused at the prices of commodities in 1915 compared with 1920, burst into laughter, and collapsed and died; the doctor called to the scene stated that the death was due to heart failure, brought on by excessive laughter. In 1928, William F. Sangster, a 46-year-old man from Glasgow who was visiting Toronto on business, died in similar circumstances.

None of these older cases can be verified to modern medical standards. There were no autopsies in the modern sense, no heart monitors, no genetic tests for Long QT Syndrome. What they show, more reliably, is how old and how durable the idea is — and how often, when a person dies suddenly during a moment of intense emotion, the people around them reach for the emotion itself as the explanation. The ancient stories are probably not medical records. They are the folklore that the modern cases inherited.

## How Laughter Can Actually Kill a Human Body

To understand whether laughter can kill, it helps to understand what laughter does to the body. Laughter is normally harmless, and it is controlled by two systems in the brain: an involuntary system that involves the amygdala, and a voluntary system that involves the premotor opercular areas. But a hard, prolonged laughing fit is a violent physical event. The chest heaves, the diaphragm contracts in rapid spasms, air is forced out of the lungs in bursts, blood pressure rises and falls, and the heart rate climbs. In a healthy body, all of this is trivial — a brief workout. In a body with a hidden fault, each of those changes is a stress test, and a stress test can find the fault.

Doctors and medical writers describe several distinct ways in which a laughing fit could turn fatal. The first and most important is the heart. In rare cases, an especially hearty chuckle can cause something called laughter-induced syncope, a condition in which a person's blood pressure drops rapidly during an exaggerated laugh, leaving them lightheaded or causing them to faint. The first documented case of laughter-induced syncope was recorded in 1997, when a 62-year-old patient with hypertension and other heart-related problems fainted multiple times while roaring with laughter at a television show. The patient did not die, and this type of fainting usually lasts less than a few minutes before the person comes to. But as cardiologists have pointed out, it is possible that somebody with this condition could fall and hit their head, or fall down a flight of stairs, and die from the injury rather than from the laughter itself. Fainting during any sudden medical episode carries its own dangers, which is why the question of <a href="/blog/can-you-swallow-your-tongue-during-a-seizure-fact-check/">can you swallow your tongue during a seizure</a> matters in the same way: the emergency is rarely the single thing people imagine, and the secondary injury is often the real risk.

The second pathway is the heart's rhythm. A prolonged laughing fit can trigger a fatal arrhythmia — a chaotic, uncoordinated heartbeat — in a person whose heart is already vulnerable. This is what doctors believe happened to Alex Mitchell, whose inherited Long QT Syndrome made his heart's electrical system prone to exactly this kind of failure under excitement or exertion. It is also what happened in the single clearest case in the medical literature, described by Dr Jorge Antonio Gutierrez, an assistant professor of cardiology at Duke University School of Medicine: a 50-year-old woman who was taking medication for schizophrenia, a class of drugs that can cause an irregular heartbeat, was told to stop the medication because she was at risk of cardiac death, did not follow the advice, and later laughed for two or three minutes at a joke at home before passing out and dying. The laughter triggered the arrhythmia. She actually died laughing.

The third pathway is the airway. In theory, laughter could trigger a sudden spasm of the vocal cords — a condition known as laryngospasm — cutting off the flow of air into the lungs. If the spasm persisted and nobody started CPR or used a defibrillator, the heart, deprived of oxygen, could fall into a lethal rhythm. Laughing while eating carries a related risk: food can be aspirated into the trachea, blocking the airway entirely. Death from laughter can also occur if laughing too hard leads to asphyxiation, with the body shutting down from lack of oxygen — a mechanism that is also associated with nitrous oxide overdoses, where uncontrollable laughter is a symptom rather than a cause.

The fourth pathway is the blood vessels. A brain aneurysm is a bulge that forms in an artery in the brain, and if it ruptures it can quickly lead to brain damage, coma or death. Laughing increases pressure inside the chest, and that pressure can be transmitted into the vascular system. If a person has an undiagnosed brain aneurysm, a hard laugh could potentially lead to a rupture or a leak. The same logic applies to an aortic aneurysm in the chest: the pressure spike from a violent laugh could, in principle, rupture it. People with inherited defects in collagen production, such as Ehlers-Danlos syndromes, may be particularly vulnerable to sudden spikes in blood pressure, though that vulnerability is not limited to laughing.

The fifth pathway is the lungs. A 2009 study surveyed 105 patients with asthma and found that more than 40 percent experienced laughter-induced asthma. In severe cases, an asthma attack can be fatal if the person does not have access to their inhaler. Laughter, which forces rapid, shallow breathing, is a known trigger for bronchospasm in susceptible people.

The sixth and strangest pathway is the brain itself. Gelastic seizures are a rare form of epilepsy that commonly starts in the hypothalamus, the brain region that helps regulate emotion. These seizures are unique because they are associated with uncontrollable laughing or giggling while the person is awake or asleep, and they are sometimes caused by brain tumours in the hypothalamus. Depending on the size of the lesion, the emotional lability may be a sign of an acute condition rather than the cause of the fatality — but a seizure triggered during a laughing fit is a medical emergency in its own right.

## What the Doctors Actually Say

When journalists have put the question to cardiologists — can you laugh to death? — the answers have been strikingly consistent, and strikingly careful. The doctors do not say it is impossible. They say it is almost impossible for a healthy person, and that every real case has a second story underneath it.

Dr Todd Cohen, chief of cardiology at the New York Institute of Technology, told Live Science that an exaggerated laugh can make people pass out in very rare cases, and that death from laughter, while technically possible and documented, remains an overall unlikely cause of death for healthy individuals. His summary of the evidence is reassuring and precise: "I believe laughter is the best medicine, and it's very unlikely to kill someone — but it's theoretically possible."

Dr Megan Kamath, a cardiologist at UCLA Health, made the same distinction from the other side: while there have been reported cases of death from laughter due to asphyxiation or cardiac arrest, the risk to a healthy person is vanishingly small. Laughter, in her assessment, is harmless — or even beneficial — in the vast majority of situations.

Dr Jorge Antonio Gutierrez of Duke University was blunter. Asked whether the act of laughing itself could kill a person, through an aneurysm or a heart attack, his answer was essentially no. Laughing can increase the pressure inside the chest, and if a person happens to have an aortic aneurysm, that pressure could rupture it — but in that case, the person just happened to laugh. The laughing did not get them. Somebody can have a heart attack while they are laughing, he said, but they were going to have a heart attack no matter what. The one exception he knew in the medical literature was the 50-year-old woman whose medication had already put her heart at risk — and even there, the laughter was the trigger, not the disease.

Dr Dana Abendschein, an associate professor of medicine at Washington University School of Medicine in St Louis, walked through the laryngospasm scenario in detail: if laughing induced a persistent spasm of the vocal cords, the heart could be deprived of oxygen, resulting in a lethal arrhythmia, and unless someone started CPR right away and shocked the heart with a defibrillator, the person could die from laughing. Conceivable, he said — not common, not likely, but conceivable.

And Dr Ronald L. Dalman, a professor of surgery at Stanford University, added the vascular caveat: not likely, especially if you are otherwise healthy, but certain individuals with inherited defects in collagen production or cross-linking face a real risk from sudden pressure spikes — whether the spike comes from laughter, lifting a heavy box, or straining on the toilet.

The consensus, in short, is a careful yes wrapped around a much larger no. Yes, laughter can be the final trigger. No, laughter does not kill healthy people. And the distinction matters, because it decides whether the rest of us should worry — we should not — and whether people with known heart conditions should be cautious about anything that stresses the heart — they already are, and laughter is only one item on a long list that includes exercise, strong emotion, and sudden fright.

That last point connects to a wider truth about the heart and emotion. Sudden stress of any kind can damage the heart, which is why we previously investigated <a href="/blog/can-you-die-from-a-broken-heart-fact-check/">can you die from a broken heart</a> and found that intense grief can trigger a real, measurable cardiac condition. Laughter is the joyful end of the same spectrum: an intense emotional and physical event that the heart must absorb. A healthy heart absorbs it easily. A damaged heart may not.

## The Twist in the Mitchell Case

The most important development in the entire story of death by laughter did not happen in 1975. It happened in 2012, when Lisa Corke's diagnosis rewrote the meaning of her grandfather's death — and, by extension, the meaning of every famous laughing death.

Long QT Syndrome is an inherited disorder of the heart's electrical system. In a normal heart, each heartbeat follows a precise electrical sequence: a signal tells the muscle to contract, and then the system resets. In Long QT Syndrome, the reset takes too long. The delay is usually silent and harmless. But under physical exertion, emotional stress, a sudden loud noise, or prolonged laughter, the delayed reset can degenerate into a chaotic rhythm called torsades de pointes, which can stop the heart within seconds. The condition is hereditary, it is manageable once diagnosed — with medication, lifestyle changes, and sometimes an implanted defibrillator — and it is frequently missed, because the first symptom is often a faint, a seizure, or a sudden death that looks like an ordinary heart attack.

When Corke collapsed in 2012 and was diagnosed, her doctors put two and two together. Her grandfather had died after laughing for half an hour at a comedy show; her own heart had nearly stopped at home for no obvious reason. The family history, the circumstances, and the genetics pointed in the same direction. Dr Pier Lambiase, the consultant cardiologist involved, said the evidence was good circumstantial support for the idea that Mitchell had carried the same condition, while noting that absolute certainty would require finding the same genetic abnormality in the granddaughter and in the man who had died — something that could never be tested, because Mitchell had been dead for thirty-seven years.

The Mitchell family's story has a happier ending than its beginning. Once the condition was identified, other family members could be tested. Mitchell's daughter was found to carry the syndrome too, and it is manageable once it is known. A death that had looked like a freak accident — a man killed by a joke — turned out to be a warning that saved his descendants. The comedy show did not murder Alex Mitchell. It exposed a condition that his family did not know it carried, and the exposure, decades later, is what allowed his granddaughter to survive the same flaw.

That is the pattern that repeats across nearly every case in this story. The laughter is visible, dramatic, and memorable. The condition underneath it is invisible, silent, and ordinary — a rhythm disorder, a bulging artery, a narrowed airway, a drug side effect. We remember the laugh because it is the story. The doctors remember the condition because it is the cause.

## Laughter Is Still, Overwhelmingly, Good for You

It would be a strange irony if the conclusion of this investigation were that laughter is dangerous, because the evidence points in exactly the opposite direction. A study published in the Journal of Epidemiology that tracked how often around 17,000 people laughed found that the people who laughed less were more likely to die, and that their risk of cardiovascular events was higher. Laughter, in other words, is associated with living longer, not with dying sooner.

This is consistent with everything doctors know about laughter's effects on the body. A good laugh relaxes blood vessels, improves blood flow, reduces stress hormones, eases muscle tension, and lifts mood. Hospitals run laughter therapy programmes. Cardiac rehabilitation guidelines encourage activities that produce genuine, relaxed laughter. The same cardiologists who warn that a violent laughing fit could trigger an arrhythmia in a vulnerable heart will also tell you that a life without laughter is worse for the heart than a life full of it.

Laughter, like yawning, is partly involuntary — a reflex wired deep into the brain, which is one reason the question of <a href="/blog/is-yawning-contagious-fact-check/">is yawning contagious</a> turns out to have a real neurological answer rather than a folk one. The involuntary system, centred on the amygdala, is what makes a genuine belly laugh impossible to fake and difficult to stop. That loss of control is precisely what makes prolonged laughter physically intense: you cannot choose to breathe normally while your diaphragm is convulsing. But that intensity is also what makes laughter such an effective release. The body shakes itself out, and then it relaxes.

The people who should think about the risks are a narrow group: those with a diagnosed heart rhythm disorder, an untreated aneurysm, severe uncontrolled asthma, or a seizure disorder involving the hypothalamus. And even for them, the advice is not to stop laughing. It is to treat the underlying condition, carry an inhaler or medication if prescribed, and seek help immediately if a laughing fit is followed by chest pain, breathlessness, fainting, or a seizure. For everyone else, the risk of dying from laughter is so small that it barely registers against the ordinary risks of a single day — driving, climbing stairs, eating a meal.

## Claim vs. Evidence: What the Saying Gets Right and Wrong

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Laughter itself can kill you | No documented case shows laughter killing a healthy person; every verified case involved a hidden condition |
| A man died laughing at a TV show in 1975 | True — Alex Mitchell laughed for about 25 minutes at *The Goodies* and died of heart failure; in 2012 his granddaughter's Long QT Syndrome diagnosis explained why |
| A doctor died laughing at a film in 1989 | True — Ole Bentzen died of heart fibrillation during *A Fish Called Wanda*; laughter raised his heart rate, but a silent cardiac weakness was the underlying cause |
| Ancient philosophers died of laughter | Unverifiable legend — Chrysippus, Zeuxis and others are "said to have" died laughing; no medical evidence survives |
| Laughter can stop your heart | Possible in rare cases — a laughing fit can trigger a fatal arrhythmia, laughter-induced syncope, or laryngospasm, but only in a vulnerable person |
| Laughter can make you faint | True — the first documented case of laughter-induced syncope was in 1997, in a 62-year-old with hypertension; fainting itself is usually brief and harmless |
| Laughter can trigger a fatal asthma attack | True — a 2009 study of 105 asthma patients found more than 40% had laughter-induced asthma; severe attacks can be fatal without an inhaler |
| Laughter is dangerous and should be avoided | False — a study of around 17,000 people found those who laughed less had a higher risk of cardiovascular events and death |

## The Verdict

Can you die from laughing? Mostly true — but with a qualification so large that it changes the meaning of the answer.

People really have died while laughing. Alex Mitchell died in 1975 after twenty-five minutes of laughter at a comedy show. Ole Bentzen died in 1989 during a screening of *A Fish Called Wanda*. A woman on heart-rhythm medication died after laughing at a joke at home. These are documented facts, not myths, and they are the reason this claim earns a verdict of Mostly True rather than False.

But the qualification is the whole story. In every case that doctors have been able to examine, the laughter was the trigger and something else was the cause: an inherited Long QT Syndrome, a silent cardiac weakness, a drug that destabilised the heart's rhythm. The cardiologists who have studied these deaths are unanimous that laughter does not kill healthy people, and the large-scale evidence points the other way — people who laugh less die sooner. Laughter is, for almost everyone, one of the safest and healthiest things a body can do.

So keep the phrase. Say you died laughing when a joke lands perfectly. Just know that the phrase is a compliment to the joke, not a medical warning — and that the only people who have ever paid for it with their lives were carrying a hidden weakness that the laughter merely found. The joke did not kill them. It found them first.
