It is one of the cruellest jokes in all of survival lore. A person adrift at sea, throat burning, lips cracked, is surrounded by more water than they could drink in a thousand lifetimes — and every drop of it will make them die faster. The claim shows up in films, in novels, in playground debates and, worryingly, in real emergencies: that when you are dying of thirst, seawater is better than nothing. It is not. The ocean is not a backup water supply. It is, chemically speaking, the opposite of one.

This is not a close call, and it is not a matter of opinion. It is arithmetic, done by your kidneys, and the arithmetic does not care how thirsty you are.

## The claim that refuses to die

The idea that seawater can keep you alive has several versions, and it is worth separating them, because one of them contains a grain of truth that makes the false versions more dangerous.

The strong version — the one that gets people killed — says that a stranded person can drink seawater to stay hydrated, or at least to buy time. The weaker version says that a little seawater, mixed with fresh water or with the fluids squeezed from fish, can stretch a small fresh-water supply. The weakest version says that swallowing a mouthful while swimming is harmless. Only the last of these is true, and it is true only because the amount involved is trivial.

The strong version fails for a reason that has nothing to do with germs, pollution, or the taste. Even perfectly clean, laboratory-pure seawater would be dangerous. The problem is the salt, and the salt is the whole point of seawater.

## What seawater actually is

Seawater is, by weight, about 3.5 percent dissolved salts. That works out to roughly 35 grams of salt in every litre — about seven teaspoons, dissolved invisibly. Most of it is ordinary sodium chloride, table salt, but seawater also carries magnesium, calcium, potassium and sulphates, a chemical cocktail your body did not evolve to process in bulk.

Your blood, by contrast, sits at roughly 9 grams of salt per litre. As Dr. Christanne Coffey, an emergency medicine specialist at the University of California San Diego who directs its wilderness medicine fellowship, has explained, that makes seawater about four times saltier than blood. That single ratio is the whole story. Everything that follows — the thirst, the vomiting, the confusion, the organ failure — is your body trying, and failing, to close that gap.

Your cells are mostly water, and they are exquisitely sensitive to the saltiness of the fluid around them. Water moves across cell membranes toward the saltier side, a process called osmosis. It is the same process that shrivels a slug in salt, and it is happening, at a smaller and more dangerous scale, inside you when seawater hits your bloodstream. Salt floods in; water rushes out of your cells to dilute it; your cells shrink and struggle to do their jobs. Your brain notices the rising salt level in your blood almost immediately, and it does the one thing it knows how to do: it makes you thirsty. Thirstier than you were before you drank.

That is the first cruelty of seawater. It does not merely fail to quench thirst. It manufactures more of it.

## Your kidneys, and the math that dooms you

Your kidneys are the body's water-treatment plants. They filter your blood, keep what you need, and throw the rest away in urine. They are remarkable organs, but they have a hard limit, and seawater exceeds it.

Human kidneys can concentrate urine only so far — to a salt concentration of roughly 2 to 2.5 percent, well below seawater's 3.5 percent. This is the decisive fact. To get rid of the 35 grams of salt in one litre of seawater, your kidneys cannot simply pass that litre straight through. They have to dilute the salt down to a concentration they can actually excrete, and the diluting water has to come from somewhere. It comes from your own body.

The commonly cited result of this calculation is stark: excreting the salt from a litre of seawater costs your body more than a litre of water — estimates put it at around 1.4 litres or more of internal fluid. You drink a litre and lose more than a litre. Every mouthful is a loan taken out at a ruinous interest rate, repaid from water your cells, your blood and your brain cannot spare.

This is why the comparison that matters is not "seawater versus fresh water" but "seawater versus nothing." Drinking nothing, you lose water slowly, through breathing, sweating and the small amount of urine your body still produces. Drinking seawater, you lose water faster, because you have handed your kidneys a salt load they can only clear by spending water you do not have. The U.S. Naval Aero-Medical Safety Journal put it bluntly in 1955: all physiological knowledge at the time indicated that seawater was "inimical to the human organism" and that its use would shorten, rather than prolong, survival time.

That is not a metaphor. Historical estimates compiled during and after the Second World War, when navies studied shipwreck survival intensively, suggested that a person starting out in water balance in temperate conditions might survive around ten days with no water at all — but only around six days if they drank seawater. Even the advocates of seawater drinking, the researchers noted, claimed no more than that. The numbers are old, the experiments behind them were necessarily limited, and nobody should treat "ten days" or "six days" as a personal guarantee. But the direction of the effect is not in doubt, and it has never been reversed by later research.

## What happens when you drink it

The damage does not arrive all at once. It arrives as a cascade, and each step makes the next one worse.

First, the gut rebels. Seawater in the stomach and intestines draws water out of the surrounding tissues as the body tries to dilute it, and the result is nausea, cramping and diarrhoea — each of which throws away still more water. Vomiting, a common response, does the same. A person who drinks seawater to save themselves is, within hours, losing water through three routes at once.

Then the blood itself becomes too salty, a condition doctors call hypernatremia. The brain is especially vulnerable, because brain cells shrink as water leaves them. The symptoms read like a horror story precisely because they are the brain malfunctioning: intense and unquenchable thirst, confusion, irritability, muscle twitching and spasms, delirium, hallucinations, seizures, and eventually coma. Emergency physicians describe survivors of severe salt poisoning as delirious and hallucinating within a day or so of heavy seawater drinking, and that timeline matches accounts from wartime life rafts, where men who drank seawater after days adrift deteriorated with shocking speed — delirium within twelve to twenty-four hours in some documented cases, followed by kidney shutdown.

The kidneys, meanwhile, are being worked to destruction. Forced to filter an impossible salt load with dwindling water, they eventually cannot keep up at all. Kidney failure is not a side effect of seawater poisoning; it is the endpoint of the same process, the moment the water-treatment plant breaks down under a load it was never built to carry.

None of this requires drinking a heroic quantity. Small sips repeated over days accumulate, because the salt has nowhere to go except out through urine that costs more water than it saves. That is why survival experts are so absolute about it: the danger is not only in the single reckless gulp, but in the slow, reasonable-sounding habit of "just a little, just to wet my mouth." A little, repeated, is how the salt load builds.

## The small print: what is actually safe

Fairness demands the exceptions, because they are real, and because understanding them shows why the main claim is false.

Swallowing a mouthful of seawater while swimming is, for a healthy adult, not dangerous. The salt load is tiny, your body has ample fresh water on board to dilute and excrete it, and drinking fresh water afterwards settles the matter entirely. Surfers swallow seawater all the time. The dose makes the poison, and a mouthful is a trivial dose.

Gargling with salt water for a sore throat is a different act entirely — you spit it out — and it has nothing to do with hydration. Likewise, oral rehydration salts, the sachets used to treat diarrhoea, are sometimes confused with seawater by people who reason that "salt water helps dehydration." They do not resemble seawater at all. An oral rehydration solution is mostly water with a small, carefully measured pinch of salt and sugar, designed to help the gut absorb water. Its salt concentration is a small fraction of seawater's. Drinking a rehydration solution is drinking fresh water with a garnish; drinking seawater is drinking a salt solution with a garnish of water.

Boiling seawater does not help either. Boiling kills bacteria and viruses, which is why it is excellent for making dubious fresh water safer. But salt does not boil away. It stays behind, and as the water evaporates, the remaining liquid actually gets saltier. To make seawater drinkable you must remove the salt, not the microbes — by distillation, catching the steam and condensing it back to fresh water, or by reverse osmosis, forcing it through a membrane fine enough to block salt. Both are real technologies, and both are the reason desalination plants exist. Neither is something a person can improvise by heating a pot over a fire and drinking what is left in the pot.

Nor can you "get used to" seawater. Kidneys do not strengthen with practice, and there is no acclimatisation protocol that raises the maximum concentration of human urine above seawater's. The persistent myth that you can train yourself onto salt water, or that a seawater enema somehow bypasses the problem, has been examined and dismissed: the intestinal lining absorbs the salt either way, and the same hypernatremia follows.

## The man who drank the ocean — and why he does not prove the claim

Every defence of seawater drinking eventually arrives at the same name: Alain Bombard. The French physician's story is so extraordinary that it is worth telling properly, because the popular version of it is exactly backwards.

In 1951, Bombard was a young doctor in Boulogne when a trawler sank and he was unable to save a single member of its crew. The experience redirected his life. He became convinced that castaways died not because the sea offered nothing, but because nobody had taught them to use what it offered. He took a post at the Oceanographic Institute of Monaco, studied the nutritional value of marine life, and then decided to test his ideas on himself.

On 19 October 1952, Bombard set out from the Canary Islands in a 15-foot inflatable dinghy, L'Hérétique — "the heretic" — carrying no food and no fresh water, only a sextant, fishing gear, a fine silk plankton net, and a sealed set of emergency rations he had promised not to open. For 65 days he drifted toward Barbados, eating raw fish and plankton, collecting rainwater when it fell, and drinking small amounts of seawater — about 700 millilitres a day, roughly a pint and a half — mixed with the liquid squeezed from fish flesh. He arrived on 23 December, having lost more than 25 kilograms, covered in sores, his body so weakened that his spine would later need bone grafts. He could walk ashore. He had survived.

Bombard's voyage is often retold as "a man drank seawater for 65 days and lived, therefore seawater is drinkable." That retelling drops every fact that matters. Bombard did not drink seawater as his water supply. He drank a small, limited quantity, always diluted with fish fluids and supplemented by rainwater, as part of a diet in which fish flesh — itself about three-quarters water, and fresh water at that — supplied most of his hydration. He never claimed that seawater alone could keep a person alive. His point was the opposite of the viral claim: that a castaway who harvested the sea properly, fish and rain included, might not need to drink seawater in quantity at all.

Even so, his claims were fiercely disputed. The German physician Hannes Lindemann, who made his own Atlantic crossings to test Bombard's methods, later accused Bombard of secretly carrying extra provisions — a charge Bombard denied, and one that was never proven. The seal on Bombard's emergency rations was reportedly intact when he landed, which supports his account, but the dispute itself is instructive: even the most famous case in history for seawater drinking is contested, partial, and wrapped in suffering that no reasonable person would volunteer for. Bombard described the voyage as a starving, thirsty hell. He lost a quarter of his body weight. His is not a success story for seawater. It is a warning about what surviving on the ocean's terms actually costs — and a demonstration that the only version of "drinking seawater" ever shown to be survivable involved drinking very little of it, heavily diluted, alongside real sources of fresh water.

Modern survival manuals have absorbed that lesson and simplified it into an instruction that leaves no room for improvisation. The U.S. Navy SEAL survival handbook's guidance for a lifeboat crew that has run out of fresh water is unambiguous: "Do not drink seawater or urine!" The U.S. Air Force survival manual carried in kits and on aircraft says the same about seawater. These are not cautious suggestions. They are written for people who will be making this decision while terrified, dehydrated and possibly injured, and they are written in that tone for a reason.

## What to do instead when there is no fresh water

The honest answer to "what should a stranded person do instead?" is less dramatic than drinking the ocean, which is precisely why it works.

Catch rain. Keep a tarpaulin, a sail, or any clean sheet ready to spread at the first sign of a shower, and rig it at night with the edges turned up to collect dew. The Navy handbook notes, with characteristic practicality, that a small amount of seawater mixed into caught rainwater is not a cause for concern — the mixture is still overwhelmingly fresh, and it will not cause a physical reaction. Dilution is the whole trick, and rainwater provides it.

Use a solar still or a desalting kit if you have one, and save the desalting kit for overcast days when the still cannot work. In cold regions, old sea ice — bluish, with rounded corners, that splinters easily — has lost most of its salt and can be melted for water, while new, grey, milky ice cannot. Iceberg water is fresh, though icebergs themselves are dangerous to approach.

Eat fish, and use the fish. The fluids along the spine and in the eyes of large fish are low in salt, and the flesh itself is mostly fresh water — the same resources Bombard relied on. And if the fresh water runs out entirely, the manuals give advice that sounds counterintuitive until you understand the chemistry: do not eat. Digesting food costs water, and a body with no water to spare cannot afford the transaction.

Conserve what you have. Stay in the shade, move as little as possible, keep your clothes damp with seawater on the outside — wetting clothes cools by evaporation without putting a drop of salt inside you, though the manuals warn to do this only when necessary, because prolonged contact causes saltwater boils and rashes. Ration fresh water by measuring it, counting the people it must serve, and protecting it from saltwater contamination as if it were medicine. It is.

And there is one more piece of advice that belongs in any honest account of this topic, because it is the advice that saves the most lives: do not wait until you are desperate to learn any of this. The people who die from drinking seawater are rarely people who weighed the evidence and chose wrong. They are people who were thirsty, frightened, surrounded by water, and acting on an instinct that feels like common sense. The instinct says: water is water. The chemistry says: salt is not negotiable.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that you can drink seawater to stay hydrated, or to survive longer than you would by drinking nothing, is false. Seawater is about 3.5 percent salt — roughly four times saltier than your blood — and your kidneys can only produce urine far less salty than seawater itself. To flush out the salt from a litre of seawater, your body must spend more than a litre of its own water, so drinking seawater leaves you more dehydrated than you were before. Heavy or repeated drinking leads to vomiting and diarrhoea, then to hypernatremia — confusion, hallucinations, seizures — and ultimately to kidney failure and death. The evidence runs from wartime naval research to modern emergency medicine, and every major survival manual agrees: do not drink seawater.

The nuances do not rescue the claim. An accidental mouthful while swimming is harmless because the dose is trivial. A small amount of seawater diluted into rainwater, or mixed with fish fluids as Bombard did, is a different act from drinking seawater as a water supply — and even Bombard's celebrated voyage left him emaciated and disputing accusations for years afterward. Boiling does not remove salt, you cannot acclimatise your kidneys to it, and oral rehydration salts are not seawater by any meaningful measure.

The ocean will give a stranded person many things: fish, rain collected on a sail, the cooling of wet cloth, and, eventually, rescue. Fresh water is not one of them. The cruellest thing about being lost at sea is not that there is no water. It is that there is water everywhere, and the one thing standing between you and survival is understanding why you must not drink it.

## Why this myth matters on dry land

It would be easy to file this under "advice for shipwrecks" and move on. But the seawater myth is a close cousin of claims this site has examined before, and the family resemblance is worth noticing. Like the <a href="/blog/eight-glasses-water-rule-fact-check/">eight glasses of water a day rule</a>, it takes a real fact about hydration and turns it into a rigid prescription that the evidence does not support. Like the question of whether <a href="/blog/is-bottled-water-safer-than-tap-water-fact-check/">bottled water is safer than tap water</a>, it invites people to pay for, or reach for, the more dramatic option when the ordinary one is better. And like the <a href="/blog/does-hot-water-freeze-faster-mpemba-fact-check/">Mpemba effect</a>, it shows how a counterintuitive-sounding story about water can travel much further than the careful science behind it.

Water advice spreads because thirst is universal and water is mysterious — it is the only substance we must consume daily, in large quantities, to stay alive, and most of us understand it poorly. That combination makes water a perfect vehicle for myths: they feel testable, they feel like common sense, and they are wrong in ways that only show up at the extremes, where the consequences are worst.

The seawater claim is the most dangerous member of that family, because its extreme is not a laboratory curiosity. It is a life raft, a capsized boat, a beach holiday that goes wrong, a hiker who runs out of water near the coast and reasons that the sea is right there. If you take one sentence from this article and keep it, make it the sentence the survival manuals print in bold: do not drink seawater. Not a little to get by. Not to buy time. Not because it is better than nothing. It is worse than nothing, and the ocean, for all its abundance, is not offering you a drink.
