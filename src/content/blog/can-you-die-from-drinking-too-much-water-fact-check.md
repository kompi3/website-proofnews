Water is the one substance nobody warns you about. Every health campaign tells you to drink more of it. Bottles, apps, and smart watches remind you to keep sipping. Dehydration is the villain of a thousand articles, and water is the hero. So the question sounds like a trick, or a joke: can you die from drinking too much water?

You can. It is rare, it is hard to do by accident, and it takes a lot of water in a short time — but it is real, it is documented, and it has killed healthy young adults who had no idea they were in danger. A 28-year-old mother died after a radio contest. A 28-year-old runner died during the Boston Marathon. A 21-year-old student died during a fraternity hazing ritual. A man died after winning a beer-drinking contest. And in 2023, actress Brooke Shields collapsed with a full-blown seizure after flooding her system with water while preparing for a stage show.

The mechanism has a name — water intoxication, or hyponatremia — and the physiology behind it is well understood. This is not a story about contaminated water, or about water being secretly toxic. It is a story about dose, speed, and a balancing act your body normally performs without you ever noticing. When that balance breaks, the most harmless liquid on Earth can swell your brain inside a skull that has no room to give.

## The Short Answer

Yes. Drinking far more water than your kidneys can excrete in a short period dilutes the sodium in your blood, a condition called hyponatremia. When blood sodium falls far enough, water moves by osmosis into your cells — including your brain cells. The brain swells inside a rigid skull, pressure rises, and the result can be confusion, seizures, coma, and death. The kidneys of a healthy adult at rest can clear roughly 800 to 1,000 millilitres of water per hour, so danger generally requires drinking more than about a litre an hour for several hours, or a very large volume in a single sitting. Ordinary drinking — a glass with meals, sipping through the day — is nowhere near that line. But contests, hazing rituals, endurance races, and determined over-hydration have all crossed it, with fatal results.

## How Plain Water Turns Dangerous

Your body is about 60 percent water, and it guards the concentration of that water with obsessive precision. The key number is the concentration of sodium in your blood. In a healthy person it sits between 135 and 145 millimoles per litre. Sodium is the main solute outside your cells, and it is what keeps water distributed correctly between the inside and outside of cells. When you drink water, it dilutes the sodium in your blood. Normally that is no problem at all: your kidneys simply excrete the excess, and a hormone system led by vasopressin — also called antidiuretic hormone — fine-tunes how much water you keep and how much you pass.

The trouble starts when water arrives faster than the kidneys can remove it. A healthy kidney at rest can excrete roughly 800 to 1,000 millilitres of water per hour, according to Dr. Joseph Verbalis, an endocrinologist at Georgetown University, speaking to Scientific American. That is the body's speed limit. Drink faster than that for hours, and the excess has nowhere to go. Blood sodium falls. Below 135 millimoles per litre, you have hyponatremia by definition.

Once the blood is more dilute than the fluid inside your cells, osmosis takes over. Water moves across cell membranes toward the saltier interior, and the cells swell like balloons. Most tissues can stretch to accommodate this. Fat and muscle have room to give. Brain cells do not. They are packed tightly inside a rigid bony cage, sharing that space with blood and cerebrospinal fluid, and as the clinical neuroscientist Wolfgang Liedtke of Duke University Medical Center has explained, inside the skull there is almost no room to expand. Rising pressure compresses brain tissue, and if the swelling continues it can force the brainstem downward through the opening at the base of the skull — a catastrophic event called herniation.

That is why the symptoms of water intoxication are neurological, not digestive. The early signs are headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, fatigue, and muscle cramps. As sodium falls further, the symptoms escalate: drowsiness, irritability, loss of coordination, seizures, loss of consciousness, coma, and death. A case report published in the medical literature lays out the thresholds starkly: nausea and malaise typically appear as plasma sodium falls below 125 millimoles per litre, headache and lethargy set in around 115 to 120, and seizures, coma, and death can follow below 115.

There is a cruel twist to this story. The early symptoms of drinking too much water look almost exactly like the symptoms of drinking too little. Headache, nausea, confusion, fatigue — a person suffering from overhydration can easily conclude they are dehydrated and drink even more. That confusion between the two conditions is one reason water intoxication is so dangerous, and one reason it is so often recognised too late.

It is worth putting the scale of the risk in perspective. Water is, by any ordinary measure, one of the least toxic substances known. Its lethal dose in rats exceeds 90 grams per kilogram of body weight — a figure that would translate to an absurd volume for a human. Nobody dies from an extra glass at dinner. The deaths on record share a pattern: very large volumes, very fast, often combined with circumstances that cripple the kidneys' ability to cope. Exercise, stress, pain, nausea, and heat all trigger the release of vasopressin, which instructs the kidneys to conserve water even when the body does not need it. Under the stress of a marathon, Verbalis has noted, the kidneys' excretion capacity can fall as low as 100 millilitres per hour — a tenth of the resting rate. A runner drinking a litre an hour in that state is gaining water with every sip, even while sweating.

## The Radio Contest That Killed a Mother of Three

The most famous case of fatal water intoxication began as a joke on morning radio. In January 2007, a Sacramento radio station, KDND, ran an on-air contest called "Hold Your Wee for a Wii." Contestants would drink increasing amounts of water without urinating, and the winner would take home a Nintendo Wii game console. The station's DJs joked on air about the risks. Listeners called in to warn them.

Jennifer Strange, a 28-year-old mother of three, drank about six litres of water in three hours — some accounts put the total closer to two gallons. She complained of a splitting headache, vomited, and went home. She was found dead at home later that day. The coroner ruled the cause of death water intoxication.

The case became a national scandal and a textbook example. The station's parent company eventually paid a multi-million-dollar settlement to Strange's family, and the contest's name became shorthand for a particular kind of preventable tragedy: an ordinary person, with no medical condition and no warning, killed by a substance everyone assumes is harmless. What made Strange's death so shocking was not only the cause but the ordinariness of the act. She was not an athlete pushing her limits or a patient with a kidney disorder. She was a healthy young woman who drank water, too much of it, too fast, because a radio station offered her a prize.

Strange's death also exposed a gap in public knowledge. Most people have a vague sense that you can drown in water from the outside. Almost nobody knows you can drown in it from the inside — that the same molecule, swallowed in the wrong quantity at the wrong speed, can flood the brain. The DJs who ran the contest reportedly knew so little about the risk that they treated the warnings as a joke. The coroner's ruling was the correction, and it arrived too late.

## The Marathon Problem

If the radio contest is the most famous case, the marathon is the most common setting. Endurance athletes have died from drinking too much water, and many more have been hospitalised with hyponatremia that was only recognised because someone thought to test their blood sodium.

The defining study was published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2005. Christopher Almond and colleagues at Children's Hospital Boston studied runners in the 2002 Boston Marathon, weighing 766 of them before the race and taking blood samples from 488 at the finish line. Thirteen percent of those tested had hyponatremia — blood sodium at or below 135 millimoles per litre — and 0.6 percent had critical hyponatremia, below 120. The strongest predictor was not how little the runners drank. It was how much: runners who gained weight during the race, meaning they drank more fluid than they lost in sweat, were about four times more likely to be hyponatremic. Drinking more than three litres of fluid during the race, drinking at every mile, and taking more than four hours to finish were all associated with low sodium. And it made no difference whether the runners drank water or sports drinks — what mattered was the volume.

That same race claimed a life. Cynthia Lucero, a 28-year-old runner, died of hyponatremia during the 2002 Boston Marathon. She was not a novice pushing recklessly; she was a participant in one of the world's most prestigious races, following the hydration advice of the era. For years, runners had been told to drink as much as possible, to drink before they felt thirsty, to stay ahead of dehydration at all costs. The advice was well-intentioned and, for slow runners who spend five or six hours on the course stopping at every water station, dangerous. The slowest runners have the most time to drink and the least sweat loss, which is exactly the combination that produces weight gain during a race — the clearest warning sign of overhydration.

The sports-medicine world has since reversed course. The current guidance from exercise physiologists is to drink to thirst and to replace roughly what you sweat — if you are sweating 500 millilitres an hour, that is about what you should be drinking. Weighing yourself before and after a long training run is the simplest check: if you finish heavier than you started, you drank too much. A weight gain of three percent of body weight is considered a serious red flag. The tragedy of the Boston study is that the fix is so simple, and the old advice so persistent. A generation of runners was told that dehydration was the enemy and that water was the answer, without anyone mentioning that the answer has a limit.

## A Beer-Drinking Championship and a Fraternity Basement

Not every case involves athletes or contests with water as the drink. In Spain, a man died after winning a 20-minute beer-drinking contest in which he consumed six litres of beer. He collapsed and died within 80 minutes. Beer is mostly water, and the same physiology applies: a huge volume of electrolyte-free fluid, swallowed faster than the kidneys can clear it, can crash blood sodium even when the kidneys are working normally. Doctors who reviewed the case proposed a term for it — hyperacute water loading and hyponatremia — to describe lethal hyponatremia that develops simply because the volume and speed of drinking overwhelm a healthy excretion system.

In 2005, a fraternity hazing ritual at California State University, Chico, left a 21-year-old man dead after he was forced to drink excessive amounts of water between rounds of push-ups in a cold basement. The combination was lethal in more than one way: forced drinking removed the body's natural defence — the simple unwillingness to keep swallowing — while exercise and cold stress pushed vasopressin up and the kidneys' excretion rate down.

And the risk is not limited to deliberate excess. In 1995, 18-year-old Leah Betts died at her birthday party in England after taking an ecstasy tablet and then drinking large quantities of water, reportedly because she had been warned that the drug could cause dangerous overheating and dehydration. The media widely blamed the drug. The inquest determined that her death was caused by water intoxication and hyponatremia. The drug contributed indirectly, by prompting the drinking and perhaps by stimulating vasopressin, but the water itself was the lethal agent.

The United States military has documented the same pattern in its own ranks. One Army study found 17 trainees admitted to hospital over a single year for water intoxication, and another recorded three soldiers who died. Trainees in hot conditions, ordered to drink on a schedule and pushed through exertion that floods the body with vasopressin, are a textbook high-risk group — and the military's hydration guidance has been rewritten more than once as a result.

What ties these cases together is not recklessness in the ordinary sense. None of these people set out to harm themselves. They were following instructions — from a radio station, a fraternity, a coach, a poster on a wall, or a well-meaning warning about dehydration. The danger of water intoxication is precisely that it hides behind good intentions and good advice taken too far.

## The Celebrity Seizure

In September 2023, the risk made headlines again, this time with a famous face. Actress Brooke Shields was preparing for her one-woman show at New York's Café Carlyle and had been drinking large amounts of water throughout the day, worried about staying hydrated through long singing rehearsals. People around her began to notice something was wrong. Things stopped making sense. She walked to a corner of a Manhattan restaurant for no apparent reason and wondered why she was there. Then everything went black. She fell face first, turned blue, began frothing at the mouth, and had a generalised tonic-clonic seizure — the kind formerly called a grand mal seizure — in front of fellow diners. Actor Bradley Cooper, who happened to be nearby, held her hand as she was loaded into an ambulance.

In hospital, doctors told her she had drunk too much water and had too little sodium in her system. She was admitted to intensive care, put on an IV, and told something she never expected to hear from a doctor: drink less water. "I flooded my system, and I drowned myself," she later told Glamour magazine. "And if you don't have enough sodium in your blood or urine or your body, you can have a seizure."

Shields had no epilepsy and no kidney disease. She was 58, an age at which the body's water-handling capacity is already lower than it was in youth, and she was drinking far more than she needed, quickly, because she believed hydration could only help. Her case is the clearest modern reminder that water intoxication is not a freak accident confined to contests and marathons. It can happen to anyone who drinks a great deal, fast, on a day when their kidneys are not keeping up — and the first sign that something is wrong can be a seizure, with no warning at all.

Doctors who commented on her case stressed how unusual it is. "The possibility of developing a seizure or a major side effect of drinking too much is extremely rare," said Stavros Kavouras, a professor of nutrition and director of the Hydration Science Lab at Arizona State University. But rare is not impossible, and Shields' experience shows the other side of a public-health message that has spent decades telling people to drink more. "Adequate is always better," Kavouras said — a sentence that sounds like a platitude until you realise it is the actual medical advice.

## How Much Water Is Too Much?

There is no single lethal dose, because the danger depends on speed, body size, kidney function, exercise, medication, and how much salt you have taken in. But the research does give us clear numbers to work with, and they are the numbers in the table below.

| Measure | What the research shows |
|---|---|
| Normal blood sodium | 135–145 mmol/L |
| Hyponatremia begins | Below 135 mmol/L |
| Nausea, malaise | Below ~125 mmol/L |
| Headache, lethargy, confusion | Around 115–120 mmol/L |
| Seizures, coma, death | Below ~115 mmol/L |
| Kidney excretion at rest | 800–1,000 mL per hour |
| Kidney excretion during marathon stress | As low as 100 mL per hour |
| Symptoms often start | After 3–4 litres within a few hours |
| Fatal case, radio contest (2007) | ~6 litres in 3 hours |
| Fatal case, beer contest | 6 litres in 20 minutes |
| Boston Marathon 2002, hyponatremia | 13% of 488 tested runners |
| Boston Marathon 2002, critical hyponatremia | 0.6% (below 120 mmol/L) |
| Recommended total daily water intake | 3.7 L/day for men, 2.7 L/day for women |

The last row needs explaining, because it surprises people. The National Academy of Medicine's adequate intake figures — 3.7 litres a day for men and 2.7 for women — count total water, including the water in food, which supplies roughly a fifth of daily intake. The popular rule about eight glasses a day has no scientific basis, as we examined in our earlier fact-check on the eight-glasses rule. A person drinking eight glasses of water on top of a normal diet is already well hydrated. A person forcing down twice that amount, quickly, is heading for trouble.

Doctors cited by the University of Mississippi Medical Center put the practical boundary in plain terms: problems generally require upwards of eight litres of water a day, and most people with healthy kidneys regulate far smaller amounts without any issue. The same doctors advise that if you are drinking more than about four litres a day, you should also take in electrolytes — salt, a commercial hydration mix, or an oral rehydration solution. The salt is the point: hyponatremia is not really a disease of too much water, it is a disease of too little sodium relative to water, and replacing salt matters as much as limiting water.

It is also worth noting what does not protect you. Sports drinks contain some sodium, but the Boston Marathon study found that hyponatremia was just as likely in runners who drank sports drinks as in those who drank water. The sodium in a typical sports drink is far too dilute to offset a large volume of fluid. Only deliberate electrolyte replacement — salt tablets, oral rehydration salts, or salty food — changes the equation, and even then it cannot compensate for drinking several litres an hour.

## Who Is Most at Risk?

Water intoxication can happen to anyone who drinks fast enough, but some people cross the line far more easily than others.

**Endurance athletes** top the list. Marathoners, ultramarathoners, triathletes, and long-distance hikers combine the three ingredients that make overhydration dangerous: hours of drinking, vasopressin flooding the system under physical stress, and the well-meaning advice to drink constantly. The slower the athlete, the greater the risk, because slow finishers spend the most time on the course and have the most opportunities to drink. The Boston study found that a race time over four hours was one of the strongest risk factors of all, with an odds ratio of 7.4 compared with finishing in under three and a half hours.

**Military trainees** face a similar combination of forced drinking schedules, heat, and exertion. The Army's cases in the late 1990s were the reason military hydration guidance was rewritten to emphasise drinking to thirst and monitoring urine colour rather than drinking on a fixed timetable.

**Infants** are at risk for a different reason: their kidneys are smaller and less mature, so their maximum rate of water excretion is proportionally lower. Babies have been hospitalised with water intoxication after well-meaning caregivers gave them excessive amounts of water or overly diluted formula. Paediatric guidance is clear — babies under six months should not be given water at all, and formula must be mixed exactly as directed, never watered down to make it last longer.

**Older adults** are more vulnerable because the kidneys' maximum water-clearing capacity declines with age, independent of any kidney disease. Add in medications that affect fluid balance — diuretics, some antidepressants, and certain anti-seizure drugs such as oxcarbazepine — and the margin shrinks further. Brooke Shields was 58 when she seized; her doctors' first piece of advice was about sodium, not about willpower.

**People with certain medical conditions** carry a higher risk too. Kidney disease, heart failure, liver disease, and conditions that cause inappropriate vasopressin release all reduce the body's ability to excrete free water. So does a psychiatric condition called psychogenic polydipsia, in which a person compulsively drinks enormous volumes of water — sometimes tens of litres a day. In these cases the danger builds over weeks rather than hours, but the endpoint is the same.

And then there is the group nobody counts: ordinary people following hydration advice they found online. The wellness industry has spent years selling the idea that more water is always better — gallon-a-day challenges, "hydration" influencers, apps that nag you to drink whether you are thirsty or not. Most people who take up a gallon challenge will simply feel uncomfortable and stop. But someone with a small body, a hot day, a long workout, and a determination to finish the jug can get into trouble. The advice to drink to thirst exists for a reason: thirst is a finely tuned signal, and healthy kidneys and a working thirst mechanism are remarkably good at keeping sodium in range. The people who get into trouble are, almost without exception, people who overrode that signal on purpose.

## The Symptoms Everyone Misreads

The hardest thing about water intoxication is recognising it. The symptoms arrive in a sequence that mirrors dehydration almost exactly, and the overlap has killed people who reached for the wrong remedy.

Early on, there is headache, nausea, and vomiting. Then come muscle cramps or weakness, a feeling of low energy, and confusion. Mental status changes are the red flag that separates a bad day from a medical emergency: if someone who has been drinking heavily becomes confused, drowsy, or disoriented, or starts behaving strangely, that is not ordinary tiredness. Seizures can follow, and they can arrive with no warning at all — as they did for Brooke Shields, who went from feeling unwell to a full tonic-clonic seizure in minutes.

The resemblance to dehydration is the trap. An athlete who feels nauseous and headachy after hours of drinking will very often assume they are dehydrated and drink more. A person at a music festival, or on a long hike, can make the same mistake. The distinguishing clues are the context and the direction of change: if the person has been drinking large volumes, has gained weight during exercise, or has been urinating frequently and clearly, overhydration should be suspected — not underhydration. The Cleveland Clinic lists the warning signs plainly: muscle cramps or weakness, nausea and vomiting, low energy, headache, mental status changes including confusion, and seizures.

Treatment, once the condition is recognised, is a hospital matter. The treatment of choice is intravenous fluid containing sodium, given carefully: raising the sodium level too quickly can damage the nervous system, so doctors correct it slowly and under monitoring. Severe cases may need intensive care, as Shields did. What you should not do is what feels intuitive — drinking more water, or waiting for the symptoms to pass. If someone shows confusion, drowsiness, or a seizure after drinking a large amount of water, the correct response is emergency help, immediately. Some seizures resolve on their own within minutes. Others are fatal.

## How to Drink Safely

The good news is that avoiding water intoxication is easy, because the body's own signals are good enough for almost everyone.

**Drink to thirst.** Thirst is not a lagging indicator of dehydration, whatever the slogans say. For a healthy person doing ordinary activity, drinking when you are thirsty and stopping when you are not keeps sodium in range. The old warning that "by the time you feel thirsty, you're already dehydrated" is a myth — one that has done real harm by encouraging people to drink on a schedule instead of listening to their bodies.

**During long exercise, replace roughly what you sweat.** If you are sweating about 500 millilitres an hour, drink about that much. Weigh yourself before and after a long training run: if you finish heavier than you started, you drank too much, and a gain of three percent of body weight is a serious warning sign. For events lasting more than a few hours, include salt — salty food, electrolyte drinks, or oral rehydration salts — rather than relying on plain water alone.

**Be sceptical of volume targets.** The eight-glasses rule is not science, and gallon-a-day challenges are a social-media invention, not a health recommendation. Total water intake of 3.7 litres a day for men and 2.7 for women already includes the water in food, so most people need considerably less than that from the glass. For a closer look at how the eight-glasses idea holds up, see our earlier fact-check <a href="/blog/eight-glasses-water-rule-fact-check/">Do You Really Need 8 Glasses of Water a Day?</a>. And if you are curious about what is actually in the water you drink, our fact-check <a href="/blog/is-bottled-water-safer-than-tap-water-fact-check/">Is Bottled Water Really Safer Than Tap Water?</a> found that the bottle is no guarantee of purity.

**Know the risk groups.** If you are running a marathon, training in the military, caring for an infant, taking a diuretic or another drug that affects sodium, or living with kidney, heart, or liver disease, the ordinary rules shift, and a doctor's guidance beats any general advice — including this article's.

**Never force water on someone, and never drink it as a dare.** The deaths in this story share a pattern: somebody was pressured, or pressured themselves, to keep drinking past the point where the body said stop. Water is not a competitive sport. There is no prize worth the risk, and the Wii console at the centre of the most famous case of all is a grim reminder of how small the stakes can be when the consequences are irreversible. The same goes for drinking water as a weight-loss trick: the evidence for timing water around meals is modest at best, as we found in our fact-check <a href="/blog/does-drinking-water-before-meals-help-you-lose-weight-fact-check/">Does Drinking Water Before Meals Help You Lose Weight?</a>, and it is never worth pushing intake to extremes.

## The Verdict

**True.** You can die from drinking too much water, and healthy people have. Water intoxication — hyponatremia — is a documented, understood, and fatal condition. When water is swallowed faster than the kidneys can excrete it, blood sodium falls, brain cells swell inside a skull with no room to spare, and the result can be seizures, coma, and death. The cases are not ancient history or urban legend: a mother of three died after a 2007 radio contest, a runner died at the 2002 Boston Marathon, a student died in a 2005 fraternity hazing, a man died after a beer-drinking contest, and Brooke Shields suffered a grand mal seizure in 2023 after flooding her system with water.

The verdict comes with an important qualification, and it is one the evidence insists on. This is not a reason to fear water. It is a reason to respect dose and speed. Ordinary drinking — water with meals, sipping when thirsty, the amounts recommended by health authorities — is safe for healthy people, and the kidneys handle it easily. The danger begins at volumes most people will never approach by accident: more than about a litre an hour for several hours, or several litres in a single sitting, especially during exercise or under pressure to keep drinking. The line between safe and dangerous is wide, but it exists, and the people who crossed it did so because nobody told them it was there.

So drink water. Drink it when you are thirsty, drink it when you exercise, drink it because your body needs it. Just do not turn it into a contest, a challenge, or a test of willpower. The substance that keeps you alive is the same substance that can kill you — the difference is not what you drink, but how much, and how fast.
