Every runner has heard it, usually from someone who does not run. You mention a morning jog, a 10K, or a marathon plan, and the reply comes back with the confidence of settled science: enjoy it now, because running will wreck your knees. The image behind the warning is vivid and mechanical. Each footstrike hammers the joint, the cartilage wears down a little more with every mile, and one day, like brake pads on a car, there is simply nothing left. It sounds like common sense. It is also, for the ordinary recreational runner, contradicted by the largest bodies of evidence ever assembled on the question.

That does not mean every knee complaint from a runner is imaginary, and it does not mean running is harmless for everyone in every dose. Knees do get injured, runners do get arthritis, and some runners get it at higher rates than the rest of the population. But the evidence points to a much more interesting story than simple wear and tear: the people with the lowest rates of knee osteoarthritis in the big studies are not the people who avoid running. They are the recreational runners. The people with the highest rates are not the weekend joggers either. They are the sedentary non-runners and the elite, high-volume athletes at the two opposite ends of the activity spectrum. In between, where most runners live, the knees mostly hold up — and in some studies they look better than the knees of people who never run at all.

## Where the warning comes from

The fear is understandable because the numbers, taken on their own, sound alarming. Running loads the knee joint with forces several times greater than walking — physiotherapy reviews commonly put the peak load at around three times body weight, and higher when a tired runner's muscles stop absorbing shock as efficiently. Multiply that by thousands of footstrikes per run, hundreds of runs per year, and decades of running, and the arithmetic of destruction seems obvious. If a joint were a machine part, it would be.

But a knee is not a machine part. Cartilage is living tissue. It has no direct blood supply of its own, and it is nourished in part by the very loading and unloading that running provides, which pumps synovial fluid — the joint's lubricant and nutrient delivery system — in and out of the tissue. Muscle and bone both adapt to the loads placed on them, growing stronger under sensible, progressive training, and the research on cartilage points in the same direction: moderate, regular loading is not merely tolerated by the joint, it appears to be part of how the joint stays healthy. A narrative review in Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews offered two explanations for why runners do not show the expected damage: the stresses on the knee during running are not as extreme as the popular image suggests, and runners may condition their cartilage to withstand higher loads, much as training conditions everything else in the body.

That is the first crack in the wear-and-tear story. The second is that osteoarthritis itself is not simply a mileage counter. It is a whole-joint condition involving cartilage, bone, the joint lining, ligaments, and the muscles around the joint, and it is driven by age, genetics, body weight, metabolic health, muscle weakness, and above all previous injury. Running is only one input among many, and in the data it is not the input that separates the people who develop arthritis from the people who do not.

## What the biggest study found

The most-cited piece of evidence is a systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Orthopaedic and Sports Physical Therapy in 2017 by Eduard Alentorn-Geli and colleagues. The team searched the major medical databases for studies of hip and knee osteoarthritis among runners, and separated runners into two groups: competitive runners, meaning professional or elite athletes and people competing internationally, and recreational runners, meaning everyone running in a non-professional, amateur context. The review included 25 studies covering 125,810 people, and the meta-analysis pooled 17 studies covering 114,829 people. That scale matters, because small studies of a single running club or a single race can easily mislead. This one did not depend on any single group.

The headline numbers have been repeated so often that they risk sounding like slogans, but they are worth stating precisely. The overall prevalence of hip and knee osteoarthritis was 3.5 percent among recreational runners, 10.2 percent among sedentary controls, and 13.3 percent among competitive runners. Recreational runners, in other words, developed osteoarthritis at roughly one-third the rate of people who did not run. The odds ratio for recreational runners compared with controls was 0.86, and for competitive runners it was 1.34 — a pattern the authors described as a U-shaped relationship, with both a sedentary lifestyle and long exposure to high-volume, high-intensity running associated with osteoarthritis, and recreational running sitting in the protective-looking dip between them. Runners with less than 15 years of exposure also showed lower odds than controls, with an odds ratio of 0.6.

The authors were careful about what this could and could not prove. Observational studies like these cannot establish causation, and they could not fully separate the effect of running itself from confounding factors such as previous injury — the possibility, for example, that people who develop knee pain stop running and therefore end up counted as non-runners, or that people with healthy knees are simply more likely to keep running. That caution applies to every observational finding in this article, and it is why the verdict below is about the claim that running damages knees, not a promise that running prevents arthritis. Still, the direction of the evidence is unambiguous on the central question: if recreational running routinely destroyed knees, a pooled sample of more than 100,000 people would not show recreational runners with the lowest rates in the study.

A later, independent line of evidence points the same way. Research presented at the American College of Rheumatology, based on Baylor College of Medicine data from 2,683 participants across four age groups from 12 to over 50, found that 22.8 percent of people who had been a runner at some point in their life had knee osteoarthritis, compared with 29.8 percent of people who had never been classified as a runner. The average age of participants was 64.7 years, so this was not a study of young athletes with untouched joints. The researchers concluded that non-elite running at any time in life did not appear detrimental and might be protective — a sentence worth quoting in full because it is short and it captures the finding exactly: "Non-elite running at any time in life does not appear detrimental, and may be protective."

## The 2023 studies that tested the dose

Skeptics of the 2017 review had a reasonable objection: perhaps the recreational runners in those studies were barely running at all, and the real damage only shows up at serious weekly mileage. Two systematic reviews published in 2023 tested that objection directly, and neither found the dose-response damage the wear-and-tear story predicts.

The first, published in Physical Therapy in Sport, asked whether weekly running volume was associated with knee osteoarthritis. It pooled nine observational studies covering 12,273 participants, of whom 1,272 were runners, and compared runners with controls who ran less than 8 kilometres per week. The result was no difference in knee osteoarthritis prevalence between runners and controls, with an odds ratio of 0.97. Nor did higher volume bands show higher prevalence: runners covering 8 to 32.1 kilometres per week had an odds ratio of 1.17, those covering 32.2 to 48 kilometres had 1.04, and those running more than 48 kilometres per week had 0.62 — all statistically indistinguishable from the control group. The authors added an important caveat that honest reporting requires repeating: most of the included studies were rated as having a high or very high risk of bias, so the finding is best read as "no evidence of harm at any volume tested" rather than proof of safety at every conceivable mileage. But a dose-response theory that predicts steadily rising damage with rising mileage does not survive contact with those numbers.

The second 2023 review, from researchers at Houston Methodist and published in the Orthopedic Journal of Sports Medicine, pooled 17 studies covering roughly 14,000 people — about 7,194 runners and 6,947 non-runners. It found no evidence that running increased the risk of developing knee osteoarthritis or made existing osteoarthritis worse. More strikingly, it found a significantly higher prevalence of knee pain in the non-runner group than in the runner group, and a significantly higher risk of knee osteoarthritis progressing to total knee replacement among non-runners than among runners: 4.6 percent versus 2.6 percent. Multiple studies in the review found no significant differences in radiographic knee osteoarthritis or in cartilage thickness measured by MRI between runners and non-runners, although one study did find a higher prevalence of bone spurs, known as osteophytes, in the tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints of runners. The study's lead investigator, Dr. Patrick McCulloch, framed the motivation in terms any clinician will recognize: "Patients come into the clinic all the time asking if it's okay to run, or saying someone told them they shouldn't run."

A 2018 study summarized by Harvard Health adds a marathon-specific data point. Among 675 marathon runners, the rate of hip or knee arthritis was about half the rate expected within the United States population. And a 2024 survey of people registering for the Chicago marathon, also noted by Harvard Health, found no clear connection between running and arthritis — with a write-up of that survey population, covering nearly 4,000 runners, reporting that years of running, weekly mileage, and number of completed marathons were not linked to arthritis, while previous knee or hip surgery was associated with nearly six times the odds of arthritis. That last detail is the thread that runs through almost all of this research, and it leads to the most important distinction in the whole debate.

## Marathon knees under the MRI scanner

Questionnaire and X-ray studies can be challenged on the grounds that they miss early damage. MRI studies answer that challenge more directly, because they can see cartilage, bone marrow, menisci, and ligaments in detail — and several of them have now scanned runners before and after the hardest single effort most recreational runners ever attempt: a marathon.

The most detailed of these followed 82 healthy adults preparing for their first marathon, scanning both knees with high-resolution 3-Tesla MRI six months before the race and again about two weeks after it. Seventy-one completed the four-month standardized training program and the marathon itself; 11 dropped out during training and did not run. Before training, MRI already showed signs of damage, without symptoms, to several knee structures in the majority of these middle-aged volunteers — a reminder that middle-aged knees are rarely pristine, runner or not. After the marathon, the scans showed an improvement in the radiological score for damage to the subchondral bone beneath the cartilage in the shin bone and thigh bone, alongside a worsening, without symptoms, in the cartilage of the outer kneecap. The authors described this as the most robust evidence then available linking marathon running with knee joint health, and their conclusion cut against the wear-and-tear story: marathon training and completion improved the damaged bone beneath the cartilage even as it irritated some surface cartilage.

A follow-up study revisited 44 of those novice marathoners six months later — 37 who had completed the marathon and 7 who had dropped out during training. The improvements seen two weeks after the race were sustained at six months, and new improvements appeared: five pre-existing bone marrow lesions and three cartilage lesions that had been unchanged immediately after the marathon had shrunk by the follow-up visit. No further lesions appeared, and many of the short-term post-marathon changes showed signs of reversing, with 10 of 18 bone marrow swelling signals and 3 of 21 cartilage lesions decreasing on MRI. The authors concluded that the knees of novice runners achieved sustained improvement, for at least six months after the marathon, in the condition of their bone marrow and articular cartilage.

That pattern — short-term change, then recovery — is exactly what a separate 2022 analysis of 24 studies found for cartilage immediately after running: no evidence of significant harm to the cartilage lining the knee joints on MRI scans taken just after running. Cartilage does compress slightly during a run, and its thickness and volume dip temporarily, but the changes are small and appear to reverse with rest. A single bout of running, in other words, squeezes the sponge and then lets it re-expand. It does not grind it away. This is the same confusion that bedevils many exercise myths that sound like physiology but fail when tested — the kind of claim examined in <a href="/blog/wait-30-minutes-after-eating-before-swimming-fact-check/">No, You Don't Have to Wait 30 Minutes After Eating Before Swimming</a>, where a plausible-sounding mechanism turns out not to describe what actually happens in the body.

## The U-shape: where the risk actually rises

None of this means that no runner ever develops arthritis from running. The U-shape in the 2017 data is real, and its upper end deserves attention. Competitive and elite runners, training at volumes and intensities far beyond recreational running, showed the highest osteoarthritis prevalence in the pooled data at 13.3 percent — above even the sedentary controls at 10.2 percent. That gap is modest, and it is confounded by the fact that elite athletes are also more likely to have sustained the joint injuries discussed below, but it is a genuine signal that dose matters. Running 150 kilometres a week for a decade, year after year, is a different exposure from jogging 20 kilometres a week, and the body has limits that enthusiasm cannot repeal.

There is also an important distinction between osteoarthritis and knee pain. A runner can have sore knees without having osteoarthritis, and can have osteoarthritis visible on an X-ray without pain. The most common running-related knee complaint, patellofemoral pain syndrome — widely known as runner's knee — is pain around or behind the kneecap, often linked to training load, weak hip and thigh muscles, or running mechanics, and it is not the same thing as the cartilage loss and joint degeneration of osteoarthritis. Conflating the two is how a runner with a treatable, load-related ache ends up believing their joint is permanently worn out, and how a sedentary person with painless X-ray changes ends up believing they are fine. The diagnosis matters because the management is different: runner's knee usually responds to load management, strength work, and technique changes, while osteoarthritis management is a longer conversation about weight, strength, activity, and, in advanced cases, surgery.

This is also where a second joint myth is worth clearing up in passing. Just as running does not automatically destroy cartilage, cracking a joint does not automatically destroy it either — the evidence on that question is examined in <a href="/blog/cracking-knuckles-cause-arthritis-myth-fact-check/">No, Cracking Your Knuckles Won't Give You Arthritis — the 60-Year Experiment That Settled It</a>. Joints are more resilient, and more responsive to how they are used, than the machine-part metaphor allows.

## The real risk factors

If running itself is not the villain, what actually drives knee osteoarthritis? The research points to a short list, and previous injury sits at the top of it.

A prior knee injury — a torn anterior cruciate ligament, a meniscus tear, a fracture involving the joint surface, or a significant cartilage defect — is the strongest single risk factor identified in the literature. A clinical review summarized the scale plainly: a knee injury has been shown to confer roughly a four-fold increased risk of developing knee osteoarthritis, and around half of people with an anterior cruciate ligament or meniscus tear may develop knee osteoarthritis. The mechanism is not mysterious. The injury itself damages tissue, the healing process changes how the joint moves and bears load, and scar tissue and altered stability shift forces onto cartilage that was never designed to carry them that way. One study of more than 1,000 patients found that people who injured a meniscus between the ages of 17 and 30 showed radiological signs of osteoarthritis an average of 15 years after the original injury, while people injured after the age of 30 showed signs within about five years — a reminder that the clock after a serious knee injury runs for years, not weeks.

That matters for runners because it reframes the question they should actually ask. It is not "will running ruin my knees?" but "what is the state of my knees, and has anything already happened to them?" A runner with a previous ligament reconstruction or a meniscus repair is in a different risk category from a runner with no injury history, and the honest advice for the two runners is different. It also matters because the injuries that most often lead to osteoarthritis — ligament tears and meniscus tears — are far more common in contact and pivoting sports such as soccer and rugby than in straight-line running. Running rarely causes the injury that causes the arthritis; more often, running reveals a knee that was already vulnerable, or a training error that overloaded it.

After injury, the remaining risk factors are familiar from almost every chronic disease. Age is the largest non-modifiable factor: the joint's repair mechanisms slow with age, and risk rises steeply between the ages of 50 and 75. Genetics accounts for a substantial share of risk — physiotherapy reviews cite estimates of up to 40 percent of knee osteoarthritis incidence — and a family history of arthritis is worth knowing about. Body weight is the largest modifiable factor: heavier runners load their knees more with every step, and the metabolic and inflammatory factors associated with excess weight appear to damage joints independently of the mechanical load, which helps explain why osteoarthritis also affects non-weight-bearing joints such as the hands. Muscle weakness, particularly in the quadriceps, hip, and calf muscles that absorb force and guide the knee's tracking, leaves the joint less protected. And sudden changes in training — a rapid jump in mileage, a sudden block of hill repeats or speed work, a return from injury at the previous volume — can irritate joints and tendons in even the healthiest runners. That is a load-management failure, not evidence that the joint was doomed by running as such.

Sedentary behaviour belongs on the list too, and it is the factor the wear-and-tear story gets exactly backwards. In the 2017 data, the people who did not run had nearly triple the osteoarthritis prevalence of recreational runners. In the Houston Methodist review, non-runners had more knee pain and more progression to knee replacement. Inactivity weakens the muscles that protect the joint, tends to raise body weight, and deprives cartilage of the regular loading it needs to stay nourished. Telling a healthy person to avoid running in order to protect their knees may therefore achieve the opposite of its intention: it removes one of the few activities with evidence of association with lower osteoarthritis rates, and replaces it with the behaviour associated with the highest rates.

## Can you run if you already have arthritis?

The question that worries older runners most is different from the one that worries beginners: if arthritis is already present, does continuing to run make it worse? Here the evidence is thinner, because most of the big studies started with people free of osteoarthritis, but what exists is reassuring rather than alarming.

A study using data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative — a large, long-running cohort of people with or at risk of knee osteoarthritis — focused specifically on people who already had radiographic evidence of osteoarthritis in at least one knee. It found that running did not increase symptoms or structural progression in those runners, and that runners with knee osteoarthritis actually reported decreased pain symptoms. The authors noted the important caveat that these were self-selected runners, who likely ran at lower intensities and shorter durations shaped by their own symptoms, and they called for replication in other cohorts. But as the first evidence directly addressing whether people with established knee osteoarthritis should be told not to run, it pointed against blanket prohibition.

An overview of the evidence published in a peer-reviewed journal, summarizing 20 reviews and 12 additional original studies on walking, running, and recreational sports in knee osteoarthritis, reached a similar conclusion: common forms of physical activity, including running, were consistently not related to structural progression of knee osteoarthritis, and could be safely recommended to patients with, or at risk of, knee osteoarthritis. Harvard Health has given the same practical advice to a 68-year-old reader who had jogged for decades and developed mild arthritis: mild arthritis should not stop you from running, running probably did not create the problem, and keeping active while maintaining thigh muscle strength matters more than avoiding the activity. Softer surfaces, such as a school track or a flat dirt trail, were suggested as a gentler option — advice that applies to any runner whose knees are talking back.

That advice comes with a boundary that should not be blurred. "Running with arthritis is not automatically harmful" is not the same as "push through any knee pain." The distinction clinicians draw is between muscle soreness and joint pain, and between discomfort that settles within a day and pain that changes how you move. Pain that is sharp, that causes swelling, locking, catching, or the knee giving way, that forces a limp or a changed stride, or that is still worsening days after a run is not something to train through. It is something to get assessed. The same applies to pain that appears suddenly after a twist or fall, and to any knee that has previously been injured or operated on and starts behaving differently. A physiotherapist or sports medicine clinician can usually distinguish a load problem that needs a training adjustment from a structural problem that needs imaging, and that distinction is worth more than any general article — including this one — can offer an individual runner.

## How to run in a knee-friendly way

The research does not hand runners a guaranteed knee-protection program, but it does support a set of habits that keep load within the range joints adapt to rather than the range that injures them. The first is progression. Cartilage, tendons, and bones adapt to load over weeks, not days, so mileage, speed work, and hills should rise gradually, with easier weeks built in, rather than jumping from a month off to a marathon training block. The second is strength. The quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, and calves are the knee's shock absorbers, and targeted strength work for those muscles is the single most consistently recommended companion to running in the osteoarthritis literature — it is also, incidentally, the reason walking programs that build up gradually show benefits of their own, as examined in <a href="/blog/ten-thousand-steps-rule-fact-check/">No, You Don't Need 10,000 Steps a Day — the Science Says Fewer Will Do</a>.

The third is surface and shoes. Softer, even surfaces reduce peak impact compared with cambered roads and concrete, and worn-out shoes stop absorbing shock long before they look destroyed. Neither factor has been shown to prevent osteoarthritis on its own, and expensive shoes are not medicine, but both affect the load each footstrike delivers. The fourth is variety. Cross-training with cycling, swimming, or walking on non-running days maintains fitness while giving the knees a different loading pattern, and rest days are when adaptation actually happens. The fifth is weight. For runners carrying excess weight, even modest weight loss reduces the load on the knee with every step — one of the few interventions with a direct mechanical payoff that no shoe or supplement can match.

## Why the myth survives

If the evidence is this consistent, why does the warning survive? Partly because it feels true in a way that statistics do not. Everyone knows a runner with bad knees, and everyone knows a sedentary person whose knees seem fine, and human brains are built to learn from vivid examples rather than pooled prevalence rates. Partly because the warning contains a grain of truth that has been stretched past its evidence: elite athletes and previously injured knees really are at higher risk, and some runners really do develop arthritis. And partly because the warning is self-protecting. A person who avoids running because of the warning never discovers whether their knees would have been fine, while a person who runs and later develops arthritis — for any of the many reasons above — becomes a story that confirms the warning. The sedentary person who develops arthritis rarely becomes a story at all, because nobody blames sitting on the couch in the same vivid way.

There is also a subtler reason. The wear-and-tear metaphor borrows its authority from engineering, and engineering metaphors are persuasive because they are simple. A car's brake pads really do wear out with use, and a machine really does have a finite service life. But living tissue is not a brake pad. It repairs, remodels, and strengthens in response to use, and it deteriorates fastest when it is not used at all. The correct engineering metaphor, if one is needed, is not a brake pad but a bridge: a bridge that carries regular traffic, within its design load, lasts longer than a bridge that is abandoned, because maintenance and use keep its structure sound. The knee is maintained by movement, not preserved by idleness.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that running damages your knees is false as it is usually stated — as a general warning to ordinary runners. The largest pooled evidence available, covering more than 100,000 people, found recreational runners with a hip and knee osteoarthritis prevalence of 3.5 percent, against 10.2 percent for sedentary non-runners and 13.3 percent for competitive runners. A 2023 analysis of more than 12,000 people found no higher osteoarthritis prevalence at any weekly running volume tested. A second 2023 review of about 14,000 people found no evidence that running worsened osteoarthritis, found more knee pain among non-runners, and found progression to knee replacement more common among non-runners than runners. MRI studies of first-time marathoners found short-term cartilage changes that largely reversed, with bone and cartilage measures improving six months after the race. And in people who already had knee osteoarthritis, self-selected running did not increase symptoms or structural progression.

What is true, and what keeps this verdict from being a blanket endorsement, is narrower. Very high-volume elite running is associated with higher osteoarthritis rates than recreational running. A previous serious knee injury — a ligament tear, a meniscus tear, a fracture through the joint — multiplies the risk of later osteoarthritis several-fold, and runners with that history need individualized advice. Sudden spikes in training load, weak supporting muscles, and excess body weight all raise risk in ways that have nothing to do with running as such. And knee pain during running is always worth attention, even when it turns out to be a treatable load problem rather than arthritis. None of those qualifications rescues the original claim. They describe a joint that responds to injury, overload, and neglect — not a joint that is worn out by the ordinary act of running.

## A note before you lace up

If you are a healthy adult thinking about starting to run, the evidence says your knees are not a reason to stay on the couch. Start gradually, build strength alongside mileage, choose forgiving surfaces where you can, and treat pain that changes your gait as information rather than as something to run through. If you already run and your knees ache, the most useful question is not whether running in general is bad for knees, but what changed: the mileage, the surface, the shoes, the strength work you stopped doing, or an injury you never fully rehabilitated. And if you have arthritis already, or a knee that has been injured or operated on, talk to a clinician or physiotherapist before deciding that either running or quitting is the answer — the research supports continued activity for many people in that situation, but it supports it as a supervised, individualized decision, not as a slogan.

The warning will keep circulating, because it is simple, vivid, and half-remembered from a time before these studies existed. The data, though, have moved on. Across more than 100,000 people, the runners with the healthiest knees were not the ones who protected their joints by avoiding them. They were the ones who used them, sensibly, week after week, year after year. Your knees were not designed to be saved for later. They were designed to be run on.
