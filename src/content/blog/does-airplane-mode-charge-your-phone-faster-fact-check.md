It is the kind of advice that gets passed along in comment sections, group chats, and airport lounges with total confidence. Your phone is at 12 percent, your flight boards in twenty minutes, and someone tells you the trick: switch on airplane mode, plug in, and watch the battery climb far faster than it would normally. Some versions of the tip are modest, promising a small boost. Others are far bolder, claiming that airplane mode can cut charging time by a quarter, a third, or even half. The tip costs nothing, takes one second, and sounds like it should work, because it rests on an idea that is genuinely true: a phone that is doing less should charge faster than a phone that is doing more.

The trouble is that the distance between "doing less" and "charging dramatically faster" is where this claim falls apart. Airplane mode does change what your phone is doing while it charges, and careful tests have found a real, measurable effect. But the effect is small — a handful of minutes on a full charge, not the transformation the viral versions promise. The claim survives because it contains a kernel of truth, and kernels of truth are the hardest kind of claim to kill. They are true enough to feel right, and wrong enough to mislead everyone who repeats them.

To judge the tip fairly, it helps to separate two questions that the viral version mashes together. The first is whether airplane mode makes any difference at all. The answer to that is yes. The second is whether the difference is large enough to matter, or to justify cutting yourself off from calls, messages, and the internet while you charge. The answer to that is, for most people, most of the time, no. That combination — technically true, practically exaggerated — is exactly what the verdict of Misleading is for.

## The claim, as it circulates online

In its most common form, the claim is simple: turn on airplane mode before you plug in your phone, and it will charge faster. The reasoning offered alongside it is usually just as simple. Airplane mode switches off your phone's wireless radios, so the phone stops spending energy searching for signals, syncing data, and receiving notifications. With less energy going out, more of the energy coming in from the charger can go straight into the battery. So far, so logical.

But the claim rarely stops at that modest version. As it travels, the numbers grow. Social media posts and tip lists have claimed savings of 25 percent, 30 percent, and even 50 percent, as if a single toggle could turn an ordinary charger into a fast charger. Some posts present airplane mode as a secret that phone makers do not want you to know, or as a hack that "actually works" in contrast to the useless tips around it. Others pair it with a second claim, that charging in airplane mode is somehow safer for the battery or will extend its lifespan in a meaningful way. Both add-ons deserve checking, because they change what the tip is really promising: not just a faster charge, but a smarter, healthier way to charge.

It is also worth noting what the claim leaves out. Airplane mode does not change your charger. It does not increase the wattage flowing through the cable. It does not upgrade a slow 5-watt charger into a 25-watt one, and it does not change the way a lithium battery accepts charge as it fills up. What it does is reduce one kind of drain while charging is happening. Whether that reduction is big or small depends entirely on how much drain there was to remove in the first place — and on a modern phone sitting idle on a desk, there is not much drain to remove.

## Why it sounds plausible: charging is a balance, not a one-way flow

The reason the tip feels so convincing is that its underlying physics is correct. Charging a phone is not simply a matter of power flowing in; it is a balance between power flowing in and power being spent at the same time. A charger delivers a certain amount of power, and the phone divides that power between filling the battery and running everything else: the screen, the processor, the radios, the sensors, and the apps working in the background. If the phone is spending a lot, less is left over for the battery. If it is spending very little, almost all of the incoming power goes to the battery. That is the whole theory behind airplane mode, and it is sound as far as it goes.

Airplane mode targets a specific part of that spending. When it is switched on, the phone stops its cellular connection, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth, and on most devices it also pauses location-related radio activity. That matters because radios are not passive listeners. A phone with an active cellular connection is constantly maintaining contact with nearby towers, checking for incoming calls and messages, and syncing data in the background. In an area with weak coverage, the work gets harder still: the phone raises its transmission power to stay connected to a distant or obstructed tower, which draws more energy. Anyone who has watched a battery drain quickly in a basement car park, a rural valley, or a crowded stadium has seen this effect in action. The phone is not doing anything visible, but its radios are working overtime.

So the logic holds: silence the radios, and you remove one source of drain. The question is only how large that source is compared with everything else. On a phone that is idle, screen off, sitting on a nightstand, the radios are already quiet most of the time. Modern phones are designed to sleep efficiently, to batch background activity, and to limit how often apps can wake the device. That means the drain airplane mode removes is often a small slice of the total power picture — real, but not transformative. On a phone that is busy, by contrast — streaming, syncing, hunting for signal in a weak coverage area, lighting up with notifications every minute — the slice is larger, and airplane mode has more to save. The same toggle, in other words, produces very different results depending on the phone's state, and the viral claim's mistake is to quote the best case as if it were the typical case.

There is a second, larger drain that airplane mode does not touch at all: the screen. A lit display, especially one showing video or a game, can consume more power than all of the phone's radios combined. This is why the single most effective thing you can do while charging is also the simplest: leave the phone alone. A phone that is being used while it charges will always charge more slowly than one sitting untouched, airplane mode or not. Airplane mode's secondary benefit, which even sceptical reviewers acknowledge, is that it discourages exactly that kind of use. If you cannot receive messages or scroll feeds, you are less likely to keep waking the screen. But that is a behavioural trick, not a charging breakthrough, and it works just as well if you simply put the phone down.

## The CNET test: the original numbers behind the tip

The most-cited evidence for this claim comes from CNET, which tested the idea as far back as 2014. The setup was straightforward: charge phones with airplane mode on and off, and time the difference. The result has been quoted ever since, and it is worth quoting precisely, because precision is where the viral version first starts to drift. CNET found that airplane mode cut 4 minutes off a full charge, and 11 minutes off a charge to 50 percent. Those are the numbers that every honest version of the tip should carry with it.

Four minutes on a full charge and eleven minutes to half charge are not nothing. If you are standing at an airport gate with a dying phone, eleven minutes could be the difference between boarding with 50 percent and boarding with 40 percent, and that is a difference you might genuinely feel. But notice what those numbers are not. They are not a percentage that sounds impressive in a headline. They are not a doubling of speed. They are minutes, counted on one hand or two, on a process that takes well over an hour. The tip's defenders sometimes present the 11-minute figure for a partial charge as if it applied to every charge, or as if it scaled up to a full charge. It does not. A full charge takes longer precisely because the final stretch of charging is slower by design, as the battery management system tapers the current to protect the battery — and that tapering phase is where airplane mode has the least room to help, because the charger itself is already delivering less power.

The age of the CNET test matters too. Phones in 2014 were different machines from the phones of today: less efficient processors, less aggressive power management, and chargers that delivered far less power. If anything, one might expect the airplane mode effect to have shrunk since then, because modern phones waste less energy when idle. That is exactly what later retests found, which is why the CNET numbers should be treated as a starting point rather than a permanent verdict. They established that the effect is real and small. They did not establish that it is large.

## The modern retest: a Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra, timed to the minute

More than a decade after CNET, SlashGear ran its own version of the test on a modern flagship, a Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra, using a 65-watt charger and timing full charges from zero to 100 percent. The method was deliberately simple and repeated over several weeks: drain the phone to zero, plug it in, switch it on at 1 percent, and for the airplane mode runs, enable airplane mode after switching on. The results lined up remarkably well with CNET's decade-old findings. The shortest full charge in airplane mode took 1 hour and 2 minutes, compared with 1 hour and 9 minutes with full connectivity — a difference of just 6 minutes. Across repeated runs, the gap consistently hovered between 4 and 7 minutes.

That consistency across a decade, across different phones, and across very different chargers is telling. It suggests that the airplane mode saving is not a quirk of one device or one era, but a fairly stable property of how phones charge: radios account for only a small share of what a phone spends while it sits idle and charging. SlashGear's own conclusion was blunt. While charging in airplane mode may slightly improve charging times, the savings are not significant enough for most users to make it a habit — and the trade-off, losing calls and messages for the duration of the charge, makes it an even worse deal for most people.

The retest also surfaced a complication that the viral tip never mentions: ambient temperature. In some early runs, the test phone in airplane mode actually took noticeably longer to reach full charge, in one case still needing an additional 5 minutes after 1 hour and 21 minutes. Heat is one of the main things that slows charging, because a phone that gets warm will deliberately throttle its charging speed to protect the battery. A warm room, a phone left in sunlight, or a case that traps heat can easily wipe out a 4-to-7-minute saving, or reverse it entirely. That does not mean airplane mode causes overheating — it does not — but it does mean the saving is small enough to disappear into the noise of ordinary conditions. A tip whose benefit can be erased by a warm afternoon is not a tip you can count on.

## Why the saving is so small on a modern phone

If the physics says airplane mode removes drain, why is the saving only a few minutes? The answer lies in how little drain there is to remove on a modern phone that is already sitting still. When a phone is idle with its screen off, it is not a busy machine waiting to be silenced. It is already mostly asleep. Its processor drops to a low-power state, its apps are restricted from waking it constantly, and its radios spend most of their time in quiet, low-power standby rather than actively transmitting. Airplane mode turns off radios that were already spending most of their time doing very little. The saving is real, but it is a saving on a small number.

Contrast that with the factors that actually dominate charging time. The charger and cable matter far more than any toggle. A phone connected to a 5-watt charger will charge slowly no matter what mode it is in, while the same phone on a proper fast charger will charge quickly even with every radio switched on. The difference between a slow charger and a fast one is measured in tens of minutes or hours, not in the single-digit minutes that airplane mode offers. The battery's own charging curve matters too: lithium batteries charge quickly when they are empty and much more slowly as they approach full, because the system reduces current near the top to avoid stress and heat. No toggle changes that curve.

There is also a subtle point about what airplane mode does not switch off. It does not switch off the phone itself, and it does not stop the processor, the sensors, or the operating system from running. The phone is still awake enough to manage charging, monitor temperature, and run its battery management system. That is why reviewers consistently note that fully powering the phone down beats airplane mode: a powered-off phone eliminates almost all power draw, not just the radio portion. If the goal is genuinely the fastest possible charge, switching the phone off is the more effective version of the same idea — and even that, on a modern fast-charging phone, typically saves only a modest amount compared with simply leaving the phone alone.

None of this makes airplane mode useless. It makes it a minor optimisation, in the same category as closing an app you are not using, rather than a breakthrough. The viral claim's error is one of scale: it takes a real effect of a few minutes and presents it as if it were a different kind of charging altogether.

## When airplane mode helps the most

There are situations where the saving grows, and fairness requires naming them. The biggest is weak signal. When a phone struggles to hold a connection, its radios work much harder, raising transmission power to reach a distant tower. In that state, the drain is no longer a small slice, and silencing the radios removes a genuinely larger load. Someone charging a phone in a basement, a remote area, or a building with thick walls that block signal may see a bigger benefit than someone charging on a desk in a strong coverage area. Similarly, a phone that is actively busy — receiving a stream of messages, syncing large files, updating apps, or running location services in the background — has more drain for airplane mode to remove than a phone that is quietly idle.

The second situation is the one the tip's fans rarely admit: airplane mode helps most when the alternative is using the phone while it charges. If you are scrolling, streaming, or gaming while plugged in, the screen and processor dominate power use, and airplane mode alone will not rescue the charge time. But if airplane mode stops you from picking the phone up every two minutes to check a notification, the indirect saving — from a screen that stays dark — can be larger than the direct saving from the radios. That is a real benefit, but it is a benefit of self-discipline with a technological excuse, not of radio physics. You could get the same benefit by turning the phone face down and leaving it alone, and you would still receive your calls.

It is also worth saying plainly where airplane mode makes no meaningful difference at all. On a modern phone, charging overnight, idle on a nightstand with a decent charger, the saving is likely to be at the bottom of the tested range, or within the run-to-run variation of charging itself. Independent testers who tried the tip across many recent phone models, from flagship Android devices to recent iPhones, found that full charging times were often exactly the same, or nearly the same, with airplane mode on and off. That does not contradict CNET or SlashGear; it confirms the same point from the other side. When the phone is already quiet, there is almost nothing left for airplane mode to save.

## What about battery health and safety?

A second claim often travels with the charging tip: that airplane mode is somehow better for the battery, or safer. This is where the tip drifts furthest from the evidence. There is no credible evidence that charging with airplane mode on protects the battery in any meaningful way, or that charging with it off poses a safety risk. Certified phones include multiple layers of protection regardless of mode: thermal sensors that throttle charging if temperatures rise, voltage regulation, and automatic cut-off once the battery is full. Those systems operate whether airplane mode is on or off, and they are the reason modern phones do not overcharge.

What does wear a battery down over time is well understood, and it is not the presence of a cellular signal. Heat is the main culprit, along with the sheer number of charge cycles and the habit of keeping a battery at very high or very low charge for long periods. Airplane mode's effect on heat is small and indirect: by reducing radio activity, it may reduce heat very slightly, but testers who measured temperatures during full charges with and without airplane mode found very similar peaks, typically around 35 to 40 degrees Celsius in the middle of the charge, when the system is delivering the most power. The phone's own thermal management, not the airplane mode toggle, is what keeps those temperatures in a safe range.

This is also a good place to correct a related misunderstanding. Some readers worry that fast charging, or any trick that changes charging speed, must be damaging the battery. The evidence on that front is more reassuring than the warnings suggest: in controlled testing, the watts themselves are far less important than heat, and modern battery management systems are designed to handle fast charging without dramatic wear. The short version is that airplane mode is not a battery-health strategy. Keeping the phone cool, avoiding heavy use while it charges, and not obsessing over a perfect 100 percent every time will do far more for long-term battery health than any toggle.

## What actually charges your phone faster

If airplane mode is a minor optimisation, what are the major ones? The list is refreshingly boring, which is probably why it does not go viral. First, use the right charger and cable. A charger that matches or exceeds your phone's supported wattage, paired with a cable rated for that power, will change charging time far more than any setting. A damaged or uncertified cable can restrict power delivery, and a low-wattage charger borrowed from an old device will bottleneck even the newest phone. Second, leave the phone alone while it charges. A dark screen and an idle processor are worth more than a silenced radio, because the display is the single largest power draw on most phones. Third, keep the phone cool. Charge it out of direct sunlight, out of a thick case if it is getting warm, and away from heat sources, so the phone does not throttle its own charging speed to protect itself.

Fourth, if you genuinely need the fastest possible top-up, power the phone off entirely. A powered-off phone draws almost nothing, which beats airplane mode's partial saving, and it removes the temptation to keep checking the screen. The cost is the same as airplane mode's cost — you cannot receive calls or messages while it is off — but the saving is larger. And fifth, understand the charging curve and use it to your advantage. Because phones charge fastest when the battery is low and slowest near full, a short charge from 20 percent will add far more usable battery per minute than the same number of minutes spent crawling from 90 to 100 percent. If you have ten minutes before you leave, ten minutes at low charge is genuinely useful; ten minutes at nearly full charge is mostly waiting.

There is one more honest use for airplane mode that is worth mentioning, because it has nothing to do with charging speed and everything to do with why the mode exists. Airplane mode was designed for flights, to stop phones transmitting during take-off and landing, and the rules around it have a long and surprisingly interesting history. Our earlier fact-check on <a href="/blog/airplane-mode-phone-signals-fact-check/">Will Your Phone Really Crash the Plane? The Truth About Airplane Mode</a> traces that story from the earliest bans to the modern rules. Knowing that history does not make your phone charge faster, but it does explain why the toggle is there in the first place — and why treating it as a charging hack is a little like using a seatbelt as a fashion accessory. It can be pressed into a second service, but that was never its job.

## The Verdict

So, does airplane mode charge your phone faster? In the strictest sense, yes — but only by a little, and only sometimes. CNET's original tests found savings of 4 minutes on a full charge and 11 minutes to half charge. SlashGear's modern retest on a Samsung Galaxy S24 Ultra found a consistent saving of 4 to 7 minutes on a full charge, with the fastest airplane mode run finishing in 1 hour and 2 minutes against 1 hour and 9 minutes with full connectivity. Other testers across recent phone models have found the difference to be even smaller, sometimes zero, when the phone is idle and the signal is strong. The direction of the effect is real. The size of the effect is not what the viral claim promises.

That is why the verdict is Misleading. The viral tip takes a genuine but tiny effect and presents it as a dramatic shortcut, quoting best-case percentages that the controlled tests do not support. It also smuggles in a second, unsupported claim — that airplane mode meaningfully protects battery health or safety — for which there is no credible evidence. A reader who follows the tip expecting a phone that charges in half the time will be disappointed. A reader who follows it expecting a healthier battery will be misled. And a reader who follows it before a flight, or while waiting for an important call, will have paid for a few minutes with a period of total unreachability, which is a poor trade most of the time.

None of this means you should never use airplane mode while charging. If you are in a weak signal area, if your phone is unusually busy, or if you have a few minutes at an airport gate and no calls expected, the toggle will give you a small, genuine boost — and, just as importantly, it will keep you from waking the screen every thirty seconds. But if someone tells you that airplane mode is the secret to fast charging, the honest answer is that the secret was never secret, and it was never that powerful. The real secrets are a proper charger, a good cable, a cool phone, and the discipline to leave it alone.

## The kicker

The next time the tip crosses your feed, dressed up as a revelation, try a simpler test than any laboratory can offer: time it yourself, once, with your own phone and your own charger. Charge from the same starting percentage, once with airplane mode on and once with it off, and write down the minutes. You will almost certainly find what CNET and SlashGear found — a difference you can measure with a stopwatch, and a difference you will struggle to notice in real life. The most useful charging hack was never hidden in a settings menu. It was always the unglamorous combination of the right charger, a cool phone, and the willpower to stop checking it. Airplane mode can help at the margins, and the margins are exactly where it belongs: a footnote to a good charging habit, not a headline promising a revolution.
