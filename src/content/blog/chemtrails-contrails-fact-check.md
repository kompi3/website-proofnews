Look up on a clear afternoon and you will see them: white lines scratched across the blue, crossing and recrossing as planes stitch their way overhead. Some vanish in seconds, trailing off like chalk dust. Others linger for an hour or more, spreading and feathering until they blur into thin, milky clouds. For more than a quarter-century, a persistent claim has insisted that those long-lived streaks are not what science says they are. They are, believers argue, "chemtrails" — deliberate chemical spraying carried out by governments, the military, or shadowy cabals, laced with aluminum, barium, and strontium, meant to manipulate the weather, thin the population, or bend human minds.

The theory has lately moved from internet forums into actual lawbooks. In 2024, Tennessee became the first state to pass a law prohibiting intentional geoengineering and weather-modification spraying, signed into effect in April of that year. In June 2025, Florida followed with a far tougher measure — Senate Bill 56, criminalizing weather-modification operations as a third-degree felony with up to five years in prison — signed by Governor Ron DeSantis, who framed it as protection for Floridians from chemical experimentation. Louisiana enacted its own ban in 2025, Act No. 95, which also set up a public portal where citizens could report suspected spraying; officials later said more than 400 reports poured in during its first months. Reporting in 2025 counted at least eight states with legislation explicitly referencing "chemtrails" or anti-weather-modification language, and roughly two dozen more with broader geoengineering proposals.

So the claim is no longer just a message-board curiosity. It is shaping legislation, budgets, and airport reporting rules. That makes it worth examining with the seriousness it demands: what does the atmospheric science actually say? What did the landmark survey of atmospheric scientists find? Where did the theory come from — and what is the real, publicly debated geoengineering research that keeps getting confused with it?

## The Claim, As It Circulates

The modern chemtrails theory holds that the trails behind high-flying aircraft fall into two categories: normal contrails, which dissipate quickly, and "chemtrails," which persist, spread, and crisscross the sky because they are loaded with undisclosed substances. According to various versions of the claim, those substances include aluminum, barium, strontium, polymer fibers, and even biological agents. The stated motives shift with the telling: weather manipulation and climate engineering, population control, mind control, or profits for shadowy interests. A recurring tenet is that ordinary jet exhaust should vanish fast — so any trail that lingers must contain something extra.

Believers cite photographs of grid patterns in the sky, unusually colored sunsets, and do-it-yourself laboratory analyses: rainwater, soil, or snow samples that supposedly show elevated levels of metals. In 2011, an international survey found that nearly 17 percent of respondents believed the existence of a "secret large-scale atmospheric program" — the term researchers use for the theory — was true or at least partly true. That is not a fringe margin. It is roughly one in six people.

The theory's persistence is not simply a matter of ignorance. It is fueled by a genuine optical puzzle: why do some planes leave trails and others do not, and why do identical-looking trails behave so differently on different days? The answers turn out to be atmospheric, not conspiratorial — and they have been understood, measured, and published for decades.

## What Contrails Actually Are

A contrail is a condensation trail: a line-shaped cloud made of ice crystals. The recipe has three ingredients, all documented in elementary atmospheric science. First, water vapor: burning jet fuel produces water vapor and hot exhaust gases containing fine soot particles, because the hydrogen in the fuel reacts with oxygen in the air. Second, intense cold: at cruising altitudes — typically between 25,000 and 35,000 feet, or roughly 8 to 12 kilometers — the outside air is routinely below minus 40 degrees Celsius, sometimes far below. Third, tiny particles for the vapor to condense onto, supplied by the engine's own soot.

When the hot, moist exhaust mixes with that freezing air, the water vapor condenses and freezes within a second or so into millions of microscopic ice crystals. That is the white line you see. As physicist and science communicator Olivier Cleynen has noted, anyone who has exhaled into cold winter air has made a personal contrail: the cloud of condensing droplets on a cold morning is the same physics, at ground level. "The hot exhaust from a jet engine is mostly carbon dioxide and water vapor," one explainer notes, "and as the water vapor is exposed to cold temperatures it condenses into tiny droplets of liquid or solid ice crystals." The phase transition takes a moment to occur — which is why contrails begin a short distance behind the aircraft's engines rather than at the exhaust nozzle itself.

None of this is contested within the scientific community. The U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration describes contrails as "ice crystal clouds formed by the combination of carbon particles and water vapor emitted from the combustion of aviation fuel and atmospheric water vapor," occurring "at about the same altitudes as natural cirrus clouds." The Royal Meteorological Society puts it similarly: water vapor plus cold air plus tiny particles, with the vapor condensing "usually on soot particles also emitted from the engines" and freezing into ice.

## Why Some Trails Linger and Others Vanish

The single most common piece of "evidence" for chemtrails — trails that persist for hours — is exactly what the physics predicts. A contrail's lifespan is governed by the ambient air, not by the aircraft's payload. If the air at altitude is dry, the ice crystals sublimate — pass directly from ice back to vapor — and the trail disappears in seconds or minutes, leaving that short comet-tail look. If the air is humid enough, the crystals cannot sublimate, and the trail persists. Given enough moisture and wind, persistent contrails spread outward and merge with the sky, forming thin layers of ice cloud known as contrail cirrus, visually indistinguishable from natural cirrus clouds.

This is why a trail can start and stop along its own length, or why one plane draws a line across a blue sky while a nearby plane at a slightly different altitude draws nothing at all. The atmosphere is not uniform — which is why clouds exist — and patches of humid, supersaturated air sit beside drier patches. Scientists know, too, that fewer than one in five planes is thought to form contrails at all, because the air must be both moist and cold enough.

Meteorologist and climatologist Andrew Carleton, a physical geography professor at Penn State who has studied contrails for years using NOAA satellite data, explained to fact-checkers that the shape and visibility of contrails depend on factors such as wind conditions and flight paths, adding that "the greater the number of flight paths in a given region (such as the midwest USA, southeastern Australia), the greater the likelihood of multiple persisting contrails because of the additional particulates and water vapour emissions." The idea that contrails were used to intentionally spread chemicals, he said, was "fanciful" — and as a delivery system for anything nefarious, commercial flight paths would be "a decidedly inefficient one, only occurring when and where atmospheric conditions are favourable."

The persistence that believers find suspicious is also the property that concerns climate scientists — for entirely different reasons. Like natural cirrus, persistent contrails trap heat radiating from Earth's surface. Yale Environment 360 has reported that civilian aircraft cause about 2 percent of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions but, once contrail effects are included, roughly 5 percent of warming — and that contrail cirrus clouds cover around 0.6 percent of global skies at any moment, merging over busy corridors into ice-cloud decks as large as roughly 38,000 square miles, "roughly the size of Indiana," that can last for many hours or even days. The three-day grounding of commercial flights after September 11, 2001 gave researchers a rare natural experiment, and the result confirmed that contrails' net effect warms rather than cools. That is a genuine environmental story. It is not a spraying program.

## The Survey That Asked the Experts Directly

In 2016, a team of researchers did something that had never been done: they took the chemtrails claim to the people best qualified to judge it, systematically. Christine Shearer of the University of California, Irvine and the Carnegie Institution for Science, Mick West of the science-debunking site Metabunk, and Ken Caldeira and Steven J. Davis — atmospheric and Earth-system scientists — published a peer-reviewed paper in *Environmental Research Letters* titled "Quantifying expert consensus against the existence of a secret, large-scale atmospheric spraying program."

They surveyed 77 experts: atmospheric chemists with expertise in condensation trails, and geochemists working on atmospheric deposition of dust and pollution. They showed them the actual evidence chemtrails proponents cite — photographs of persistent trails and the laboratory analyses of pond sediment, airborne particles, and snow that believers present as proof — and asked whether the simplest explanation involved a secret spraying program.

The result: 76 of the 77 scientists — 98.7 percent — said they had not encountered evidence of such a program, and judged that the cited data could be explained through well-understood physics and chemistry associated with aircraft contrails and atmospheric aerosols. The one remaining respondent flagged high atmospheric barium levels in some locations as anomalous and worth investigating, but did not affirm the existence of a deployed deliberate-spraying program. More than 80 percent of the geochemistry respondents rejected each of the three elemental analyses as evidence of a secret program, offering ordinary alternative explanations instead.

Study co-author Steven Davis told reporters the conspiracy theory "maps pretty closely to the origin and growth of the internet," and said the survey "found little agreement in the scientific community with claims that the government, the military, airlines and others are colluding in a widespread, nefarious program to poison the planet from the skies." The researchers stated their goal plainly: not to convert the already convinced, who "often reject counter-evidence as further proof of their theories," but "to establish a source of objective science that can inform public discourse."

## Where the Theory Came From

The chemtrails theory has a documented birthdate. It began circulating after the U.S. Air Force published a 1996 research paper on weather modification — a speculative futures study titled "Weather as a Force Multiplier: Owning the Weather in 2025" — which explored possible future military uses of weather modification and mentioned releasing energy or chemicals into atmospheric processes. The paper was a think piece about 2025, not a program. But in the late 1990s, the Air Force was accused of "spraying the U.S. population with mysterious substances" from aircraft generating "unusual contrail patterns," as Wikipedia's documented history records. The theories were posted on early internet forums by writers including Richard Finke and William Thomas, and were popularized by late-night radio host Art Bell starting in 1999. As the theory spread, federal officials were flooded with angry calls and letters.

The response came in 2000, when the Environmental Protection Agency, the Federal Aviation Administration, NASA, and NOAA jointly published materials explaining that the supposed chemtrails were ordinary contrails. The EPA refreshed its posting in 2015. Many believers treated the agencies' fact sheets as further proof of a cover-up — a pattern that would repeat for decades, since every official denial can be recast as evidence of the conspiracy.

Believers also built a case from chemistry: amateur tests of rainwater, soil, and snow that purported to show elevated aluminum, barium, or strontium. Scientists who examined these claims found a consistent pattern of error. Barium readings that looked alarming were traced to instrument error or to the sampling itself — one analysis noted that amateur sample collection often contaminates the results, with metal jar lids introducing the very metals being measured. Natural sources, such as aluminum-rich soil and dust, accounted for the rest. In the 2016 survey, geochemists reviewed three specific elemental analyses submitted by proponents and found none of them pointed to a secret program.

## The Secrecy Problem

A covert spraying program of the scale the theory requires would be one of the largest logistical operations in human history: tens of thousands of flights a day across dozens of countries, specialized chemicals manufactured and loaded, ground crews, pilots, air traffic controllers, regulators, and scientists — all sworn to permanent silence, for more than two decades. Historically, genuine secret programs of far smaller scope have leaked. The NSA's mass surveillance was exposed by Edward Snowden; Cold War weapons programs surfaced through documents and defectors.

Snowden himself has addressed the chemtrails question directly. Having worked with access to a vast trove of classified U.S. government files, he said he specifically looked for evidence of such a program and found nothing, later summarizing his finding in an interview: "Chemtrails are not a thing... I had ridiculous access... I couldn't find anything." No credible whistleblower — no pilot, no ground crew member, no regulator, no chemical supplier — has ever come forward with verifiable evidence of a spraying program. The absence of leaks, after decades and at this scale, is itself strong evidence.

## The Real Geoengineering Debate — And Why It Gets Confused

Here is where the story earns its nuance, because the believers are pointing at something real — just not what they think. There genuinely is a field of research proposing that humans could deliberately inject reflective particles into the stratosphere to cool the planet: solar geoengineering, also called solar radiation management or stratospheric aerosol injection. It is not secret. It is debated in public, in journals, in parliaments, and in the streets — and it is deeply controversial.

The inspiration is volcanic. When Mount Pinatubo erupted in the Philippines in 1991, it injected an estimated 20 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it formed a hazy film of aerosol particles that spread around the globe and reflected sunlight — cooling the planet by nearly one degree Fahrenheit over the following year. Scientists and entrepreneurs have since studied whether a controlled version of that phenomenon could slow global warming. Proposals include sulfur compounds, calcium carbonate, alumina, and even diamond dust.

The most prominent proposed experiment, Harvard's SCoPEx (Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment), planned to fly a balloon some 12 miles up and release a small quantity of reflective particles — several kilograms — to study how they interacted with the stratosphere. It never happened. A planned 2021 engineering test flight in Sweden, from the Esrange Space Center near Kiruna, was scrapped after the Saami Council — representing the Indigenous Saami people — and Swedish environmental groups including Greenpeace and Friends of the Earth Sweden objected to research conducted without consulting local stakeholders. The project, launched in 2017, was formally shut down in March 2024. A separate small experiment to spray sea salt over San Francisco Bay to study cloud brightening was halted by Alameda city officials. The pattern is consistent: these are small, public, heavily scrutinized research proposals — and even the smallest ones struggle to get permission.

The scientific community is genuinely divided on whether stratospheric aerosol injection should ever be deployed. Critics warn it could damage the ozone layer, disrupt ecosystems and rainfall patterns, and create a "termination shock" if a deployed program were ever stopped abruptly. Supporters argue it deserves study as a possible emergency measure against catastrophic warming. What virtually everyone agrees on is the empirical record: as the science-review outlet Science Feedback has put it, "stratospheric aerosol injection is controversial in the scientific community, and nobody has ever tested it on a large scale."

That sentence is the hinge of the whole story. There is a real debate about whether to spray particles into the stratosphere someday. That debate is public, contentious, and unresolved — the opposite of a secret program. Conflating the two is like confusing a town-hall debate about whether to build a dam with a claim that the dam was secretly built twenty years ago.

A smaller-scale cousin also feeds the confusion: cloud seeding. Unlike stratospheric geoengineering, cloud seeding is a real, long-established practice in some regions — aircraft or ground generators release substances like silver iodide into suitable clouds to encourage rainfall. Louisiana's 2025 law explicitly targeted it, eliminating what reporting described as an outdated, unused state licensing system for weather modification. But atmospheric scientists note that cloud seeding is geographically limited: it requires the right cloud types and conditions, and as atmospheric scientist Robert Rauber observed, abundant rainfall and unsuitable clouds make it impractical on the Gulf Coast, including in Louisiana. Cloud seeding operates on individual cloud systems, kilometers across — it looks nothing like the crisscrossed contrail grids in chemtrails photographs, and it has no connection to commercial flight trails.

## Why the Belief Persists

If the evidence is this one-sided, why does the theory endure — and even win legislation? Psychologists of conspiracy belief point to several reinforcing mechanisms, all visible in the chemtrails story.

First, confirmation bias and selective memory. Anyone who believes the theory notices the long-lived trails and forgets the short ones, remembers the grids and ignores the empty blue days — even though both are predicted by the same atmospheric physics. Second, the "grain of truth" structure. The sky really does change more than it used to: there is more air traffic than a generation ago, modern engines produce more water vapor, and planes fly higher, where contrails form more readily — reasons that some of the surveyed experts themselves offered for why trails may linger longer now than in the early days of aviation. Add the genuinely public geoengineering debate, and the believers' intuition that "something is going on in the sky" finds endless material to misread.

Third, distrust converts debunking into evidence. When the EPA, FAA, NASA, and NOAA published their joint explanation in 2000, many believers treated the fact sheets as proof of a cover-up. The 2016 survey authors anticipated exactly this, writing that they did not expect to sway those already convinced, "who often reject counter-evidence as further proof of their theories."

And fourth, the theory has acquired political momentum. The 2024–2025 wave of state bills shows how a claim can migrate from fringe websites into committee hearings, where, as observers noted, lawmakers mixed technical questions with conspiratorial claims — lending official legitimacy to a fringe belief in the process. Florida's law, with its felony penalties and airport reporting mandates, was celebrated by supporters as accountability; legal analysts warned its broad language could unintentionally restrict legitimate atmospheric research while banning activities that, scientists say, are not actually happening. Louisiana's citizen-reporting portal collected hundreds of reports of "suspected" spraying — a public record that, as one legal commentator put it, ended up documenting the gap between belief and science rather than evidence of a program.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that airplane contrails are deliberate chemical spraying programs — "chemtrails" loaded with aluminum, barium, strontium, or other agents — is not supported by any credible evidence, and is contradicted by multiple independent lines of it.

The white lines are condensation trails: ice crystals formed when water vapor from burning jet fuel freezes in air below minus 40 degrees at cruising altitude. Their persistence — the observation believers treat as the smoking gun — is a straightforward function of humidity, temperature, and wind, exactly as atmospheric physics predicts. Long-lived trails spread into thin cirrus clouds; short-lived ones sublimate away in dry air. This has been measured, modeled, and taught for decades.

The most direct test of the claim, the 2016 peer-reviewed survey in *Environmental Research Letters* of 77 atmospheric chemists and geochemists, found that 76 had encountered no evidence of a secret large-scale atmospheric spraying program, and that the specific photographs and laboratory analyses cited by proponents were better explained by ordinary physics and chemistry. The elemental analyses touted as proof were traced to natural sources, instrument error, and contaminated amateur sampling. The alleged program's history traces back to a misread 1996 Air Force futures paper and a late-night radio audience in 1999, not to any documented operation. And a covert program of this scale, running for decades across thousands of daily commercial flights, has produced no documents and no credible whistleblowers — not even from intelligence leakers with every incentive and every access to find one.

What does exist is something the theory distorts: a real, public, and genuinely controversial debate over solar geoengineering research. Stratospheric aerosol injection is a published proposal, openly argued about by scientists, ethicists, Indigenous groups, and governments — and it has never been tested at large scale, precisely because the scientific community is divided and the governance questions are unsolved. Cloud seeding is real but geographically limited and unrelated to contrails. Conflating these public debates with a secret spraying campaign is the theory's central error, and it survives because it feels like connecting dots — when the dots belong to different pictures.

The sky is, as always, full of human fingerprints: soot, carbon dioxide, and contrail cirrus that measurably warm the climate. Those are worth regulating, reporting on, and legislating about. But the white lines themselves are ice — water, frozen in the coldest air on Earth, doing exactly what physics says water does. The next time the sky fills with crossing white streaks, the honest explanation is also the simplest one: it was a humid day at 35,000 feet.
