If you have natural red hair, you have probably heard the warning before an operation or a dental appointment: tell the anaesthetist, because redheads need more anaesthesia. The figure usually quoted is strikingly precise. Twenty per cent more. It circulates on social media, in patient discussion groups, in magazine health columns, and even in operating theatres, where it has passed into clinical folklore. It sounds like settled science — a number, attached to a gene, attached to a visible trait you can see in the mirror.

It is also, as stated, wrong. Or rather, it is a real number from a real study that has been stretched far beyond what that study could support. The famous figure comes from an experiment involving just twenty women, one inhaled anaesthetic drug, and a laboratory measurement that is not the same thing as keeping a patient safely asleep during surgery. When researchers later tested the idea in hundreds and then thousands of real surgical patients, the dramatic difference mostly vanished. At the same time, the underlying biology turned out to be genuine in a narrower, more interesting way: people with natural red hair really do process some kinds of pain and some drugs differently — just not in the simple, uniform direction the viral claim suggests. In some situations they need more of a drug. In others, they may need less.

This is a story about how a small, careful study became a big, careless slogan, and about what two decades of follow-up research actually found.

## The Claim: "Redheads Need 20% More Anaesthesia"

The claim, in the form most people encounter it, goes like this: people with natural red hair need about 20 per cent more anaesthesia than everyone else, because of their genes. It is often stated as a flat fact, sometimes with a confident explanation attached — the gene that makes hair red also changes how the body handles painkillers — and sometimes with a warning: redheads are more likely to wake up during surgery, or to feel pain at the dentist, unless the dose is increased.

Fewer than 2 per cent of people worldwide have natural red hair, which makes the claim easy to spread and hard for most listeners to check against personal experience. If you are not a redhead, you probably know at most one or two, and their stories become the evidence. And redheads do tell striking stories. Many describe waking up groggy but in pain after procedures, needing extra injections before a tooth goes numb, or feeling a dental drill long after the dentist expected them to be comfortable. Anaesthetists have repeated the observation for decades: long before any formal study existed, there was what researchers themselves describe as "an anecdotal impression that anaesthetic requirement is increased in redheads." The question was whether the anecdote would survive a controlled test.

It did, at first — spectacularly. Then the follow-up studies began, and the picture grew complicated in exactly the ways that make a viral slogan misleading.

## Where the Number Came From: Twenty Women and One Gas

The number everyone quotes traces to a single study published in August 2004 in the journal *Anesthesiology*, led by Edwin Liem at the University of Louisville. The design was small and tightly controlled. Twenty healthy women volunteers, aged 19 to 40, took part: ten with bright natural red hair and ten with dark hair. Every volunteer was genotyped for variants in the melanocortin-1 receptor gene, known as *MC1R*, and the researchers confirmed the genetic link in the laboratory — nine of the ten red-haired women carried two copies of *MC1R* variants, the pattern that produces red hair.

Each woman was anaesthetised with sevoflurane, then maintained on desflurane, a commonly used inhaled anaesthetic gas, at a randomly set concentration. The researchers then applied a standardised painful stimulus — a supramaximal electrical shock — and measured how much desflurane was needed to prevent the body from moving in response. Movement under anaesthesia is the standard laboratory measure of anaesthetic depth, and it is how researchers calculate a drug's minimum alveolar concentration, or MAC.

The result was clear and statistically strong. The red-haired women needed a desflurane concentration of 6.2 volume per cent, compared with 5.2 volume per cent for the dark-haired women — about 19 per cent more, with a p-value of 0.0004, meaning the gap was very unlikely to be chance. The authors concluded, in the paper's title, that "anaesthetic requirement is increased in redheads." A companion animal study published the same year found that rats carrying a disabled *MC1R* gene also needed slightly more inhaled anaesthetic to reach the same depth of sedation, which strengthened the case that the gene itself, not something else about the volunteers, was driving the difference.

Within a few years, the finding had escaped the journals. Nineteen per cent became "20 per cent" in retelling, "desflurane" became "anaesthesia" in general, and "ten healthy female volunteers in a laboratory" became "redheads" as a group. The study was genuine, the measurement was real, and the statistics were strong. But three limitations were built into it from the start, and the viral version of the claim quietly drops all three. The sample was tiny — twenty people. Every participant was a woman. And the experiment tested exactly one drug, one inhaled gas, against one kind of stimulus, under laboratory conditions rather than in an operating theatre. None of that makes the study wrong. It makes it narrow, and a narrow finding cannot carry a universal rule.

## The Gene Behind the Claim

To understand why the claim seemed so plausible — and why it partly holds up — you have to understand the gene at the centre of it. Natural red hair is strongly associated with variants in *MC1R*, the melanocortin-1 receptor gene, which sits on chromosome 16. The gene's best-known job is pigmentation. In most people, the MC1R receptor on the surface of pigment cells triggers production of eumelanin, the dark brown-black pigment that protects skin from ultraviolet light. In people with certain *MC1R* variants, the receptor does not work properly, and the pigment cells default to producing pheomelanin instead — the yellowish-red pigment responsible for red hair, pale skin, and freckles.

Three specific loss-of-function variants are firmly established as the main drivers of red hair in people of European descent, known by their scientific labels R151C, R160W, and D294H. A person typically needs two copies of a variant — one inherited from each parent — to have natural red hair. But MC1R is only one member of a family of melanocortin receptors, and the others are found in the brain, where they participate in signalling pathways involved in pain, stress, and the body's response to opioid drugs. That overlap is the biological reason the anaesthesia claim was never absurd. If the same signalling system that colours hair also helps process pain, then a variant that changes one might plausibly change the other.

The melanocortin system also connects to a molecule called POMC, which the body cuts into several hormones, including one that increases pain signalling and one — beta-endorphin, the body's own opioid — that blocks it. Research supported by the National Institutes of Health found that in mice engineered to carry *MC1R* variants, pigment cells released lower levels of POMC, shifting the balance between those two signals. The result was a higher baseline pain threshold, because of stronger opioid signalling, combined with greater sensitivity to certain kinds of acute pain. That dual effect is a preview of the whole story: redheads are not simply "more sensitive" or "less sensitive" to pain. They are different in ways that depend on the type of pain and the type of drug, and the direction of the difference can flip.

The gene also explains why the topic matters beyond curiosity. Because hair colour is visible, it is one of the only outward traits that might predict anything about drug response at all. As anaesthesia researcher Daniel Sessler of the Cleveland Clinic has put it, "Red hair is the only physical characteristic that indicates how much anaesthetic a person needs." That is a striking statement from a leading researcher — and it is also a statement about a tendency in a population, not a dosing formula for an individual. The distinction between those two things is where the viral claim goes wrong.

## What the Larger, Later Studies Found

If the 2004 study were the whole story, the viral claim would be roughly fair. It is not the whole story. Over the following two decades, researchers tested the redhead effect in bigger groups, in real operations, and with different drugs — and the headline difference kept shrinking or disappearing.

The first complication came from a second study by the same lead researcher. In 2005, Liem and colleagues tested 60 women — 30 with natural red hair and 30 with dark hair — for pain sensitivity and for the effectiveness of lidocaine, the local anaesthetic used to numb skin and gums. The red-haired women were more sensitive to thermal pain, and lidocaine injected under the skin was significantly less effective in the red-haired group. That supported the idea that *MC1R* variants change drug response. But it also showed that the effect was specific: it involved heat and cold pain and one local anaesthetic, not pain in general.

Then came the studies that tested the claim where it actually matters — in operating theatres, with real surgical patients. A prospective matched-cohort study by researchers at Monash University and the Alfred Hospital in Australia compared 468 adult surgical patients with natural red hair against matched patients with black or brown hair undergoing elective surgery. The team recorded the anaesthetic drugs and doses given, the depth of anaesthesia, recovery times, pain scores, and quality of recovery. They found no evidence that hair colour affected anaesthetic requirements or recovery.

An even larger analysis told the same story. A study comparing 319 red-haired surgical patients with 1,595 controls found no clinically meaningful differences in anaesthetic management, postoperative pain, recovery time, or risk of awareness during surgery — the feared outcome of waking up while supposedly asleep. The authors concluded that anaesthetic care should not be changed solely because someone reports having natural red hair. A separate analysis of 5,713 patients found only small statistical differences with no clinical significance. And a 2012 paper found no association at all between red hair and anaesthetic effectiveness.

The drug mattered, too. A 2013 study of 29 volunteers found that red hair did not change how the body handled propofol, the intravenous anaesthetic used in most modern general anaesthetics, or how it affected brain activity as measured by the bispectral index, the monitor anaesthetists use to track depth of sedation. That is an important detail, because the 2004 result concerned desflurane, an inhaled gas. Modern general anaesthesia usually relies on intravenous drugs like propofol, sometimes combined with inhaled agents — and for propofol, the redhead effect did not show up.

None of this erases the 2004 finding. The desflurane result has been replicated in animals and is consistent with real pharmacology. What the later studies show is that a laboratory difference in the dose of one gas, measured against electrical shocks in twenty volunteers, does not translate into a rule for real surgery. In a real operation, anaesthetists continuously monitor depth of anaesthesia and adjust the dose moment by moment. Any tendency a red-haired patient has toward needing slightly more of one drug is absorbed by that monitoring — which is why the large surgical studies found no difference in outcomes, including awareness. The 20 per cent figure describes a controlled experiment. It does not describe your operation.

## Pain Is Not One Thing

One reason the research looks contradictory is that "pain" is not a single phenomenon, and redheads do not respond to all kinds of pain the same way. The studies that looked closely found a pattern that is consistent once you split pain into types.

In 2003, researchers observed that people with red hair showed no increased sensitivity to electrical stimulation compared with people with dark hair — the same kind of stimulus used in the 2004 desflurane experiment. But the red-haired volunteers had a higher sensitivity and a lower tolerance for cold and heat pain. One widely cited comparison found that redheads began to perceive cold as painful at around 23 degrees Celsius, while non-redheads did not feel cold pain until about 13 degrees — a ten-degree gap in when an ordinary cold sensation turns into pain. Heat pain also registered sooner in the red-haired group.

A separate experiment using heat and pressure stimuli in red-haired women found no difference in pain tolerance thresholds compared with controls, which shows that even within thermal pain, the results depend on exactly how the pain is delivered and measured. And a large survey published in 2021, covering more than 25,000 participants, found that women with red hair reported higher pain sensitivity than others — self-reported, but at a scale no laboratory study can match.

The practical consequence is that a redhead's experience of pain is real and measurable, but it is selective. A red-haired person may feel a cold operating theatre, an ice pack, or a hot instrument more sharply than the person in the next bed, while responding to a needle prick or an electric shock exactly like anyone else. The viral claim flattens that nuance into a single number, and the number then gets applied to situations — like intravenous anaesthesia — where the evidence says it does not belong. Hair, like pain itself, turns out to be a poor subject for slogans. ## The Dentist's Chair: Where the Claim Feels Most True

If there is one place where redheads' reports of needing more anaesthetic are most consistent, it is the dentist's chair — and the evidence there is stronger than the evidence for general anaesthesia. The *Journal of the American Dental Association* reports that people with naturally red hair tend to fear dental care more than others, and the reason appears to be linked to pain and to how well local anaesthetic works for them.

A 2003 study found that both topical and injected lidocaine were less effective in people with red hair, with the injected form showing the most significant difference. That finding was reinforced by the 2005 Liem study, in which lidocaine injected under the skin was significantly less effective in red-haired women. Dental research since then has repeatedly found that people with natural red hair are more likely to report difficulty becoming completely numb, and more likely to need additional injections before a procedure can begin.

This matters because a dental visit is where most people actually encounter local anaesthetic, and where a failed numbing is immediately obvious — you feel the drill. It also matters because dental anxiety driven by past painful experiences can become a cycle: people who expect pain avoid the dentist, their dental problems worsen, and the eventual treatment is more invasive and more painful. Sessler's advice for redheads at the dentist is practical rather than dramatic: tell the dentist you may need more local anaesthetic, and wait longer for it to take effect, because local anaesthesia may take extra minutes to reach full strength in red-haired patients. An extra few minutes in the chair is a long way from the claim that every redhead needs a fifth more of every anaesthetic — but it is a real, usable difference, and it is the part of the claim with the best support.

The dental evidence also illustrates the limits of the slogan. Lidocaine is one drug, used in one way, for one purpose. Its reduced effectiveness in redheads says nothing about propofol, nothing about opioids, and nothing about whether a redhead is more likely to wake up during surgery. Each drug has to be tested on its own, and when researchers did that, the answers came back different for different drugs.

## The Opioid Twist: Sometimes Redheads Need Less

Here is the part of the story the viral claim never mentions, because it points in the opposite direction. For some painkillers, redheads may need less of the drug, not more.

The clue comes from the same POMC mechanism described earlier. Because *MC1R* variants shift the balance of hormones produced from POMC, red-haired individuals end up with stronger signalling from beta-endorphin, the body's own opioid. In the NIH-supported mouse research, that produced a higher baseline pain threshold — the animals were less sensitive to everyday background pain — even while they were more sensitive to certain kinds of acute pain. If the body's own opioid system is running stronger, then opioid painkillers, which work through the same receptors, may also work more strongly. Several lines of evidence point that way: redheads appear to respond more effectively to opioid medications, meaning a standard dose may go further for them than for someone else.

That possibility turns the slogan on its head. A claim that says "redheads need more anaesthesia" treats anaesthesia as one thing, delivered in one direction. In reality, anaesthesia and pain relief involve several drug families — inhaled gases, intravenous hypnotics like propofol, local anaesthetics like lidocaine, and opioids — and the redhead effect, where it exists, differs for each. More of one, the same of another, possibly less of a third. A patient who walks into surgery believing they need a blanket 20 per cent increase in everything could, in principle, end up overmedicated on the drug that works in the opposite direction. Nobody is suggesting that patients should adjust their own doses — that is the anaesthetist's job, and doses are titrated to the individual in real time. But the opioid twist is a reminder that a single number cannot describe a system with this many moving parts.

The same complexity shows up in how redheads experience cold. Our fact-check of whether <a href="/blog/do-ice-baths-help-muscle-recovery-fact-check/">Do Ice Baths Help Muscle Recovery?</a> found that cold eases soreness but can blunt the muscle growth training is meant to produce — a trade-off that depends on what you are trying to achieve. Pain and temperature work the same way: a trait that makes you feel cold sooner is not simply a disadvantage or an advantage. It is a difference, and its meaning depends on the situation.

## The 2020 Genomics Surprise

The most serious challenge to the simple version of the claim came not from an operating theatre but from genetics. In 2020, researchers from McGill University and Duke University published a study in the journal *PAIN* with the pointed title "Detangling Red Hair from Pain." They tested *MC1R* variants for links with pain sensitivity, then checked their findings in 500,000 participants from the UK Biobank, one of the largest genetic databases in the world.

Their conclusion was striking: the specific *MC1R* variants that cause red hair — R151C, R160W, and D294H — were not themselves linked to pain sensitivity. Instead, the pain connection came from different variants in and around the same gene, including one called rs3212361, which sits in a stretch of genetic instructions that does not become part of the protein at all. That variant was linked to lower *MC1R* activity in brain and nerve tissue, and its less common versions were associated with lower pain sensitivity and fewer persistent pain conditions. It was also associated with red hair — which means the two traits travel together in populations without being caused by the same genetic change.

The distinction matters. If the variants that make hair red are not the same variants that change pain sensitivity, then hair colour is an imperfect marker for pain response. Some redheads will carry the pain-linked variants and some will not; some people without red hair will carry them too. That is consistent with what clinicians see: red-haired patients vary enormously from one another, and the anaesthetist's experience of "redheads need more" is real for some patients and absent for others. A 2020 paper made the same point from a different angle, reporting that red hair and pain sensitivity may come from two unrelated genetic changes. The gene is relevant to pain — the McGill and Duke researchers were explicit about that — but the relationship is not the tidy one-to-one story the slogan implies.

This is also why the largest studies keep finding no difference that matters in practice. If only a subset of redheads carries the pain-linked variants, and the effect varies by drug and by pain type, then averaging across hundreds of red-haired surgical patients will wash the signal out. The signal is real in the laboratory, where researchers select volunteers with confirmed genotypes and test one drug against one stimulus. In the clinic, where patients are unselected and drugs are mixed and titrated, it disappears into the noise of individual variation — which is large for everyone, red-haired or not. As Sessler has noted, people with blond, brown, or black hair also vary in how much anaesthetic they need, and the dose must be personalised. Hair colour is one small signal among many, not a formula.

## The Verdict

The claim that redheads need 20 per cent more anaesthesia is **Misleading**.

The number itself is genuine. In a 2004 study published in *Anesthesiology*, ten red-haired women needed about 19 per cent more desflurane than ten dark-haired women to suppress movement in response to an electrical stimulus — 6.2 versus 5.2 volume per cent. The study was small but well conducted, the genetic link was confirmed in the volunteers, and animal research pointed the same way. Redheads also show measurably greater sensitivity to heat and cold pain, and lidocaine — the local anaesthetic dentists rely on — is demonstrably less effective in red-haired patients, which is why many redheads have genuinely difficult dental experiences.

But the claim as it circulates goes far beyond that evidence. It turns one drug into all drugs, one laboratory measurement into all surgery, and twenty volunteers into every redhead. Larger studies of real operations — 468 patients in Australia, 319 red-haired patients against 1,595 controls, an analysis of 5,713 patients — found no clinically meaningful difference in anaesthetic requirements, recovery, postoperative pain, or risk of awareness. Propofol, the intravenous anaesthetic most commonly used today, showed no redhead effect at all in a 2013 study. And genomic research on 500,000 people found that the variants causing red hair are not the same variants linked to pain sensitivity, which explains why the effect varies so much from person to person. On top of all that, the opioid evidence points the other way: redheads may respond more strongly to opioid painkillers, not less.

So the honest summary is this: natural red hair is associated with real, measurable differences in pain perception and in the response to certain drugs — notably desflurane and lidocaine, and notably for thermal pain. Those differences are genuine, and they are worth telling your doctor or dentist about. But there is no universal 20 per cent rule, no basis for increasing every anaesthetic dose by hair colour, and no evidence that redheads face a higher risk of waking up during surgery. The gene matters. The slogan does not.

## What Redheads Should Actually Do

The research points to a short, practical list rather than a scary number. If you have natural red hair and you are facing a medical or dental procedure, the experts' advice is consistent and reassuring.

First, tell your doctor or dentist. Many medical and dental students are never taught about the *MC1R* effect, and a provider who knows you are a natural redhead can plan accordingly — allowing extra time for local anaesthetic to take effect, for example, or being ready with an additional dose of lidocaine. Sessler's guidance is simple: "It's reasonable to inform or remind your dentist that you may need more local anaesthetic."

Second, wait longer for local anaesthetics to work. If you have had the experience of a dental injection wearing off too soon, or never quite taking hold, part of the explanation may be timing as well as dose. Waiting a few extra minutes can make the same amount of anaesthetic more effective and reduce dental pain.

Third, do not let fear of pain keep you from getting care. That is the advice Sessler stresses most, and it is the one with the highest stakes. Avoiding the dentist because of a bad experience in the past only guarantees worse problems — and more pain — in the future. The evidence says your anaesthetist will give you the amount that is right for you, whatever your hair colour. As Sessler puts it, "The good news is that red hair or not, you'll get the amount of general anaesthesia that's right for you." Anaesthetists do not give everyone the same dose. They watch, they measure, and they adjust — for redheads and for everyone else.

The story of red hair and anaesthesia is, in the end, a story about how science actually works. A small study finds something real. The finding gets simplified into a slogan. Bigger studies test the slogan and find it wanting — not because the first study was wrong, but because the human body is more complicated than a headline. The gene that paints hair red does reach into the nervous system, and it does change how some drugs work. It just does not come with a percentage attached. The next time someone tells you redheads need 20 per cent more anaesthesia, you can tell them the truth: the number is real, the study behind it involved twenty women and one gas, and the rest of the story is far more interesting than the slogan.
