It starts the same way almost every time. A scratch at the back of the throat on a Tuesday evening. A sneeze on Wednesday morning that feels less like an accident and more like an announcement. By Thursday you are negotiating with your own body — one more meeting, one more errand — while the cold settles in for its usual week-long stay. And somewhere between the throat tickle and the full-blown sniffles, somebody will say it: take zinc. Take it now, before it is too late. The lozenges by the pharmacy till, the ones that taste faintly of metal and old pennies, are supposed to cut the whole miserable episode short.

It is one of the most durable pieces of cold advice in circulation, and it has been repeated so often that it has taken on the weight of fact. Drugstore shelves are full of zinc lozenges, zinc syrups, zinc sprays and zinc gummies, all of them trading on the same promise. The claim is specific enough to test: start zinc within a day of the first symptoms, keep taking it, and your cold will end a day or two sooner than it otherwise would. Not prevented. Not cured on the spot. Just shorter — measurably, noticeably shorter.

The reason the claim has survived for four decades is that it is not made up out of nothing. There are real randomised trials behind it, and some of them produced striking results. There is also a large, careful review that gathered all of those trials together and found the evidence thinner and messier than the packaging suggests. The honest answer to the question sits between the enthusiasm of the supplement aisle and the scepticism of the doctors who point out that colds go away on their own anyway. Zinc probably does shorten a cold — a little, sometimes, under the right conditions — and the size of the benefit, the dose required to get it, and the unpleasantness of the side effects are all part of the story that the label leaves out.

## What the Claim Actually Promises

Stated plainly, the claim is that zinc lozenges, started within 24 hours of the first symptoms and taken regularly while the cold lasts, shorten the duration of the common cold by roughly one to two days. The most optimistic versions of the claim go further, suggesting colds can be cut by about a third. The claim is about treatment, not prevention: nobody serious argues that taking zinc every day through the winter will stop you catching colds, and the trials back that distinction up. It is also, crucially, a claim about lozenges specifically — tablets dissolved slowly in the mouth — rather than zinc in general. Swallowing a zinc pill with breakfast is not the same intervention as bathing the back of your throat in dissolved zinc every couple of hours, and the trials treat them as different things.

To understand why anyone would believe this in the first place, it helps to know how common the target is. The average adult catches two to three colds a year, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and children catch six to eight. More than 200 different viruses can cause a cold, most of them rhinoviruses, and most colds resolve on their own in about seven to ten days. That last fact is the trap at the heart of every cold remedy ever sold: because colds always get better by themselves, almost anything taken during a cold can look like it worked. The only way to know whether zinc genuinely shortens a cold is to run trials in which some people get zinc and others get an identical-looking dummy lozenge, and then compare how long each group stays ill. Over the last forty years, researchers have done exactly that, dozens of times, with results that refuse to line up neatly.

The claim also comes with a clock. Every version of it insists that timing matters: zinc must be started within a day of the first sniffle, ideally within hours. The logic is that the lozenge needs to act while the virus is still establishing itself in the nose and throat. Start on day three, when the cold is already at full strength, and the trials suggest you have missed the window. This detail matters because it is also the claim's built-in excuse. When zinc does not seem to work, the answer is always that it was started too late, at too low a dose, or in the wrong form — and as the evidence shows, there is real truth in all three of those defences, which is precisely what makes the claim so hard to kill.

## How Zinc Is Supposed to Work

The proposed mechanism is straightforward enough to explain in a sentence, even if the biology underneath it is still debated. The common cold is mostly caused by rhinoviruses, which infect the lining of the nose and throat by latching onto receptor molecules on the surface of cells. The leading explanation for zinc's effect is that zinc ions — the charged particles released as a lozenge dissolves — interfere with that process. They may block the virus from binding to cells, and they may inhibit the virus's ability to copy itself once inside. Because a lozenge dissolves slowly in the mouth, it keeps the throat bathed in zinc ions for far longer than a swallowed tablet would, delivering the mineral directly to the tissues where the infection is taking hold.

That mechanism, if it is right, explains why the details of the lozenge matter so much. Not all lozenges release the same amount of free zinc ions. The chemical form counts: zinc acetate and zinc gluconate release ions readily, while some other zinc compounds hold on to their zinc more tightly. Additives matter too. Citric acid and tartaric acid, added to some lozenges to improve the flavour, can bind to zinc ions and lock them away before they ever reach the throat — which is one reason some trials of pleasant-tasting lozenges found nothing at all. The dose matters for the same reason: too little zinc, and there simply are not enough free ions to interfere with the virus. The trials that found the clearest benefit used lozenges delivering roughly 75 to 150 milligrams of elemental zinc a day, dissolved one at a time every two to three waking hours. The trials that used much smaller doses, or lozenges designed more for taste than for chemistry, tended to find little or nothing.

There is a second, simpler way of thinking about the mechanism, and it is worth keeping in mind as the trial results pile up. Zinc is essential to the immune system. It helps coordinate the white blood cells that fight infection, and a genuine zinc deficiency — rare in wealthy countries, common where diets are poor — makes people more vulnerable to infections of all kinds. But correcting a deficiency and flooding a healthy person's throat with extra zinc are two very different interventions, and the fact that the body needs zinc does not by itself prove that extra zinc shortens a cold. The trials are the only way to settle that, and they have now been run often enough to pool together.

## The First Trial, and the Pattern It Set

The modern zinc story begins in 1984, with a trial that produced a result so dramatic it launched a forty-year industry. A researcher named George Eby gave 65 adults with colds either zinc gluconate lozenges — 23 milligrams every two waking hours, after a double first dose — or identical placebo lozenges, and then tracked who was still ill a week later. The result was stark: 86 percent of the people on zinc had no symptoms after seven days, compared with 46 percent of the people on placebo. In a field where most remedies produce differences so small they need statistics to detect, a gap of forty percentage points looked like a breakthrough.

It also set the pattern for everything that followed. Later trials kept finding effects, but rarely effects that large, and never consistently. A 1996 trial by Mossad and colleagues, using zinc acetate lozenges, found that colds lasted an average of 3.4 days on zinc compared with 7.6 days on placebo — again, a dramatic difference, more than halving the illness. But other trials, run with equal care, found far less. The inconsistency became the story: zinc sometimes worked impressively, sometimes barely at all, and nobody could quite predict which it would be in advance.

One of the most telling trials came much later, in 2020, in Finland. Harri Hemilä, a researcher who had spent years arguing that zinc lozenges do work, ran a trial designed to settle the question properly: 87 adults with colds, given either a commercially available zinc acetate lozenge or a placebo that had been carefully flavoured to disguise the taste of zinc — an important detail, because in earlier trials the metallic taste of zinc may have given away who was getting the real thing. The result was a null. The zinc group did not recover any faster than the placebo group, and in the two days after the lozenges were stopped, the zinc group actually recovered more slowly. It was a small trial, and one null result does not erase a dozen positive ones, but it landed hard precisely because it was run by a believer, with a commercial lozenge, under conditions close to real life. If zinc lozenges worked as reliably as the packaging claims, a trial like that should have found something.

The Finnish trial also illustrates a problem that haunts this whole field: the placebo problem. Zinc lozenges taste terrible — metallic, bitter, astringent — and a placebo that tastes of nothing is not really a blind trial at all. Participants who can taste metal know they are getting zinc, and people who know they are getting the real treatment tend to report feeling better sooner, whatever the treatment is. Trials that disguised the taste tended to find smaller effects than trials that did not, which is exactly what you would expect if some of the apparent benefit were expectation rather than chemistry.

## The 2024 Cochrane Review: 34 Trials, One Cautious Number

When individual trials disagree, the standard scientific answer is to pool them all together in a systematic review, weighting each trial by its size and quality, and see what the combined evidence says. For zinc and the common cold, the most authoritative such exercise is the Cochrane review published in 2024 by Nault and colleagues. Cochrane reviews are the gold standard of evidence synthesis: independent, methodical, and famously unwilling to overstate what the data support. This one gathered 34 randomised trials involving 8,526 participants — 19 trials of zinc as a treatment for a cold already underway, and 15 trials of zinc taken daily to prevent colds in the first place.

The headline number is the one that now appears in almost every serious discussion of the topic. When zinc was used to treat a cold, the average cold was 2.37 days shorter than with placebo. That is a real, meaningful-sounding benefit — nearly a third off a typical cold. But the review wrapped that number in caveats so heavy they nearly swallowed it. The estimate came from just eight of the nineteen treatment trials, covering 972 participants, and the trials disagreed with each other almost completely: the statistical measure of that disagreement, called heterogeneity, came in at 97 percent, about as high as the scale goes. Some trials found colds shortened by nearly a week; two found no difference at all. When trials disagree that wildly, the average of their results is a shaky thing to lean on, and the reviewers rated their confidence in the 2.37-day figure as low — the second-lowest grade on their scale.

The review's other findings were more consistent, and less flattering. On prevention, zinc did little or nothing: people taking zinc every day were barely less likely to catch a cold at all, with a risk ratio of 0.93 — a seven percent relative reduction whose confidence interval stretched from a fifteen percent reduction to a one percent increase, which is statistics for "no clear effect." When people taking daily zinc did catch colds, those colds lasted about the same length as everyone else's. On symptom severity — how bad the cold actually felt, rather than how long it lasted — the trials could not show a clear difference either.

Most strikingly, the one finding the reviewers graded with moderate confidence — a step above low — was not a benefit at all. It was the side effects. Across sixteen trials and 2,084 participants, zinc probably increased non-serious adverse events by about a third. Roughly 36 in every 100 people on placebo reported some side effect, against about 48 in every 100 on zinc. The events were mostly the ones anyone who has sucked a zinc lozenge will recognise: a bad or metallic taste in the mouth, nausea, stomach upset, a dry or sore mouth. In the Mossad trial, side effects were reported by 90 percent of the zinc group against 62 percent of the placebo group, driven mostly by bad taste — 80 percent against 30 percent — and nausea, at 20 percent against 4 percent. Nobody in the nineteen treatment trials reported a serious side effect, so the review has nothing to say about rare, serious harm either way. But the pattern is hard to miss: the harm is graded more confidently than the benefit.

There is one more piece of history that belongs in any honest account of this evidence, because it explains why some of the numbers quoted in older articles cannot be trusted. For years, the most-cited summary of the zinc evidence was a Cochrane review by Singh and Das, first published in 2011 and updated in 2013, which reported that zinc both shortened colds and prevented them. Cochrane withdrew that review in 2015, after errors were reported and an investigation into data plagiarism proved inconclusive, and the medical journal JAMA retracted the authors' 2014 summary of it. The numbers in the current debate come from the 2024 review and from independent meta-analyses — not from the withdrawn one. Any article or product page still quoting the old review's figures is citing evidence that the scientific record has formally set aside.

## The Dose Makes the Difference

If the Cochrane review is the cautious, big-picture view, the dose-response analyses are where the story gets more interesting — and more useful. Several researchers, most prominently Harri Hemilä at the University of Helsinki, have re-analysed the same trials while paying close attention to how much zinc each trial actually delivered, and a clear pattern emerges. Trials using lozenges that delivered at least 75 milligrams of elemental zinc a day found colds roughly a third shorter. Trials using lower doses found little or nothing. The threshold is not a magic number, but the pattern is consistent enough that it has survived multiple re-analyses: below about 75 milligrams a day, zinc lozenges do not seem to do much; above it, they seem to do something real.

The formulation matters as well. In Hemilä's 2017 analysis, zinc acetate lozenges shortened colds by about 40 percent, while zinc gluconate lozenges managed about 28 percent, across seven trials and 575 patients. Pooled across three adult trials of zinc acetate lozenges, 70 percent of people on zinc had recovered by day five, compared with 27 percent on placebo — a recovery rate more than three times higher. The trials in that analysis used 80 to 92 milligrams of zinc a day, started within 24 hours of the first symptoms, and continued for the duration of the illness. A broader 2021 review in BMJ Open, by Hunter and colleagues, reached a similar conclusion across a wider set of respiratory infections: zinc, mostly as lozenges, was associated with shorter illness and less severe symptoms in adults, though again with high variability between studies and uncertainty about the best way to deliver it.

This is the most defensible version of the claim, and it is worth stating plainly. Zinc acetate lozenges, delivering at least 75 milligrams of elemental zinc a day, started within 24 hours of the first symptoms and dissolved slowly every two to three waking hours, probably shorten a cold in adults by about a day or two — roughly a third of its length. That is not nothing. A day less of a cold is a day less of misery, and for people who catch several colds a year, those days add up. But the version of the claim sold in most shops is much looser than that: any zinc product, any dose, started whenever, for anyone. The evidence does not support the loose version. It supports a narrow one, with conditions attached, and the conditions are the whole game.

The narrow version also explains the Finnish null result without needing to dismiss it. The 2020 trial used 13-milligram lozenges taken six times a day — 78 milligrams a day in total, just above the threshold — but the lozenges were a commercial product, and the trial was small, with only 87 adults developing colds out of 253 randomised in advance. A trial that size can easily miss a real effect of a day or so, and it can also be unlucky. The honest reading is not that the Finnish trial disproves zinc, or that the positive trials prove it. It is that the effect, if it exists, is modest enough to vanish inside the noise of a small trial — which is itself useful information about how large it can be.

## What Zinc Cannot Do

The limits of the claim are as important as the claim itself, and they are where most of the marketing goes quiet. First, zinc does not prevent colds. That is not a matter of opinion: the Cochrane review's prevention trials, fifteen of them, found little or no reduction in the risk of catching a cold from taking zinc every day, and the colds that did occur lasted just as long. Anyone selling a daily zinc supplement as a shield against winter illness is selling something the trials have tested and not found. The same goes for the idea that zinc will keep a cold from developing if you take it after being exposed to someone ill. There is no trial evidence for that use, and the prevention data point the other way.

Second, the evidence is mostly about adults. The trials in children are fewer and thinner. In the Cochrane review, children given zinc every day who still caught colds were ill for about as long as children given placebo, across three trials and 740 children. A few individual trials in children found benefits, but the pooled picture is weak, and the dosing, safety and taste problems are all harder in children. Parents reaching for zinc lozenges for a sick child are extrapolating from adult evidence that is itself uncertain — and should talk to a doctor or pharmacist first, as even the most enthusiastic reviews of the evidence advise.

Third, zinc is not a treatment for flu, COVID-19, or any other respiratory illness that merely feels like a cold. The trials tested zinc against the common cold, mostly rhinovirus colds, in people whose illness was diagnosed as a cold. A sore throat and a cough in winter can be many things, and zinc has not been shown to shorten any of them except the common cold. This matters more than it sounds, because the first day of a cold, a flu and a COVID-19 infection can feel identical, and the 24-hour window the zinc claim depends on is exactly the window in which you cannot yet tell which one you have. Other cold remedies have the same problem, of course. As we found in <a href="/blog/does-vitamin-c-prevent-colds-fact-check/">Does Vitamin C Prevent Colds? The 11,306-Person Review, the Nobel Laureate, and What the Evidence Really Says</a>, vitamin C does not prevent colds for most people either, despite decades of confident marketing. And just as cold air itself does not give you a cold — viruses do, as we reported in <a href="/blog/does-cold-weather-make-you-sick-fact-check/">Does Cold Weather Make You Sick? What 20 Years of Research Actually Found</a> — a mineral cannot cure an illness it was never tested against.

Fourth, the nasal route does not rescue the claim. In the Cochrane review, the three trials of nasal zinc products produced a pooled estimate of 3.15 days shorter on paper, but with a confidence interval so wide it ran from 7.36 days shorter to 1.05 days longer — which is statistics for "these trials cannot tell us anything." And the nasal route carries a risk the oral route does not, which brings us to the part of the story the supplement aisle never mentions.

## Zinc From Food Is a Different Question

It is natural to ask whether eating zinc-rich foods could do the same job as a lozenge. Oysters, red meat, chickpeas, lentils, pumpkin seeds, cashews and yoghurt are all good dietary sources of zinc, and a balanced diet supplies the mineral in the form the body uses day to day. But food zinc and lozenge zinc are different interventions doing different things. The zinc in food is swallowed, absorbed in the gut, and distributed around the body. The zinc in a lozenge sits in the mouth and throat, releasing ions directly onto the tissues where the cold virus is replicating. No trial has shown that eating more zinc-rich foods during a cold shortens it, and there is no reason to expect it would: the dose reaching the throat from food is far lower, and it arrives in the wrong place.

That does not make zinc-rich food useless. A diet that supplies enough zinc keeps the immune system working as it should, and that is genuinely important — just not the same as a treatment. The distinction matters because it is easy to blur: a food that supports immunity in general is not the same as a remedy that shortens a specific illness, and the supplement industry has built an empire on blurring exactly that line. Chicken soup sits on the same borderline. As we found in <a href="/blog/does-chicken-soup-help-a-cold-fact-check/">Does Chicken Soup Help a Cold? What the Science Actually Says</a>, the comfort is real and the cure is imaginary — soup soothes symptoms without shortening the illness. Zinc lozenges are more promising than soup, but the gap between "supports the immune system" and "shortens this cold by a day" is the gap the whole debate lives in.

## The Risks Nobody Puts on the Packet

Every honest account of zinc has to include the side effects, because they are the most reliably demonstrated finding in the entire literature. The metallic taste is the famous one: in the Mossad trial, 80 percent of the zinc group reported a bad taste, against 30 percent on placebo. Nausea ran at 20 percent against 4 percent. Mouth irritation, stomach cramps and diarrhoea all appeared in the trial reports. These are not serious harms — nobody in the treatment trials reported a serious adverse event — but they are common enough that in several trials more people dropped out of the zinc arm than the placebo arm, and they are common enough to change the calculation. A remedy that might save you a day of a cold but guarantees you several days of a mouth that tastes of coins is a trade-off, not a free win, and plenty of people who try zinc lozenges once decide not to try them again.

The more serious risks come with higher doses and longer use. Taking 50 milligrams of zinc or more a day for weeks can lower the body's copper status, because zinc and copper compete for absorption in the gut. Copper deficiency is not a trivial thing: it can cause anaemia and nerve problems, and it develops quietly. That is one reason health authorities set the tolerable upper intake level for zinc at 40 milligrams a day for adults — a level the effective lozenge doses, at 75 to 150 milligrams a day, exceed by a wide margin. Short-term use during a single cold is a different matter from daily use across a whole winter, and the trials tested the short-term version. Anyone taking high-dose zinc for weeks on end is outside the evidence and inside the risk.

Zinc also interacts with medicines. It can reduce the absorption of certain antibiotics — tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones — and of penicillamine, a drug used for rheumatoid arthritis and Wilson's disease, if the doses are taken too close together. These interactions are manageable with timing, but they are the kind of detail that matters and that a pharmacy shelf never explains.

Then there is the one risk that turned zinc from a supplement story into a safety scandal. In June 2009, the US Food and Drug Administration warned consumers to stop using three nasal versions of Zicam Cold Remedy — the nasal gel, the nasal swabs, and a children's swab product — after receiving about 130 reports of people losing their sense of smell. The condition, anosmia, can be long-lasting or permanent, and the FDA noted that some people reported losing their sense of smell after a single dose. The manufacturer, Matrixx Initiatives, was later found to have received some 800 additional reports of smell loss that it had not passed on to the agency, and in 2006 it had already paid 12 million dollars to settle 340 lawsuits from users who said the product had destroyed their sense of smell. The warning applied only to zinc products put inside the nose, where the mineral sits close to the olfactory nerve, and it did not implicate lozenges or tablets. But it remains the starkest reminder in this story that the route a remedy takes into the body can matter more than the remedy itself — and that "natural mineral" is not a synonym for safe.

## Who the Evidence Actually Covers

Strip the claim down to what the trials actually tested, and the picture narrows considerably. The evidence covers healthy adults, with colds, taking zinc acetate or zinc gluconate lozenges, delivering at least 75 milligrams of elemental zinc a day, started within 24 hours of the first symptom, and continued every two to three waking hours until the cold ends. Change any of those conditions — a child instead of an adult, a lower dose, a different chemical form, a start on day three, a swallowed tablet instead of a lozenge — and you are outside the evidence, guessing.

That narrowness is not a flaw in the research. It is the research telling us something important: the effect, if it exists, is fragile. It depends on the right form, the right dose, the right timing, and the right patient. Robust remedies survive being used imperfectly. Antibiotics work even if you take them a few hours late. Painkillers work even if you take half the recommended dose. Zinc's apparent effect does not survive that kind of rough handling, which is consistent with an effect that is real but small — small enough to be swamped by the wrong lozenge, the wrong dose, or a day's delay.

It is also worth remembering who catches the most colds and who the trials mostly leave out. Children catch six to eight colds a year, far more than adults, and older adults — who average fewer than one cold a year, according to the same CDC figures — are the group for whom any cold carries the most risk, because their immune systems are weaker and complications are more likely. The trials say the least about exactly these groups. A remedy that has only been properly tested in healthy working-age adults is a remedy whose evidence base is thinnest where the need is greatest, and that gap should temper any enthusiasm.

## The Verdict

Does zinc shorten a cold? The best answer the evidence currently supports is: probably, by a little, under narrow conditions — and the claim as it is usually sold is truer than the sceptics allow and weaker than the packaging claims.

The case for the claim is real. Dozens of randomised trials have tested zinc lozenges against colds, and the largest synthesis of them, the 2024 Cochrane review of 34 trials and 8,526 people, found that treating a cold with zinc shortened it by an average of 2.37 days. Dose-response analyses point the same way: lozenges delivering at least 75 milligrams of elemental zinc a day, started within 24 hours, shortened colds by about a third, with zinc acetate performing best of all. In the strongest individual trials, the differences were dramatic — four days instead of seven, seven in ten people recovered by day five instead of fewer than three in ten. This is not a folk remedy with no evidence behind it. It is a folk remedy with a genuine, if modest, evidence base.

The case against taking the claim at face value is just as real. The Cochrane reviewers rated their confidence in the headline number as low, and the trials disagreed with each other almost completely. Two well-run trials of ordinary colds found no benefit at all. Zinc does not prevent colds, it has not been shown to help children, and it does nothing for flu or COVID-19. And the one finding the reviewers trusted most was the side effects: a bad taste, nausea and stomach upset, probably about a third more common on zinc than on placebo, affecting nearly half the people who take it. Add the copper risk of long-term high doses, the interactions with antibiotics, and the 2009 FDA warning over nasal zinc and lost sense of smell, and the picture is of a remedy whose costs are more certain than its benefits.

So the verdict is **Mostly True** — with the emphasis on the qualifier. Zinc lozenges, in the right form, at the right dose, started early, probably shave a day or two off a cold in adults. Zinc as it is usually bought and used — any product, any dose, started whenever, by anyone, for prevention as much as treatment — has not earned that claim. The difference between those two sentences is the difference between a fact-check and an advertisement, and it is worth keeping them apart.

## The Kicker

The common cold has defeated every remedy ever thrown at it, and zinc has come closer than most to surviving the encounter with the evidence. That is worth something. It is also worth less than the shelf full of lozenges suggests, and the reason is not that the trials were rigged or the reviewers were biased. It is that the truth about zinc is narrow and conditional, and narrow, conditional truths do not sell. The product on the shelf promises a shorter cold for everyone. The evidence promises a possibly shorter cold for healthy adults who start the right lozenge, at a high enough dose, within a day of the first sniffle, and who do not mind their mouth tasting of metal for the privilege.

There is a final, quiet lesson in the zinc story, and it is about the 2024 Cochrane review itself. Its authors, having gathered every trial they could find, concluded that the evidence was insufficient to recommend zinc for preventing or treating colds. That is not the same as saying zinc does not work. It is the sound of science refusing to upgrade a "probably, sometimes, a little" into a recommendation — and the gap between those two things is where most of the money in the cold-remedy aisle is made. The next time a cold starts and somebody presses a lozenge into your hand, you can take it, if you like, in full knowledge of what it might do: perhaps a day less of sniffles, very likely a mouthful of metal, and no protection at all against the next cold, which — at two or three a year — is already on its way.
