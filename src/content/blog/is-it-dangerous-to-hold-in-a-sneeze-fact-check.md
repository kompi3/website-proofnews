You know the moment. You are in a quiet meeting, a library, a cinema, or a car full of people, and a sneeze starts building. It feels enormous, unstoppable, and socially disastrous. So you do what millions of people do every day: you pinch your nose, clamp your mouth shut, and swallow the whole explosion back down. There is a strange, muffled pop somewhere behind your face, your eyes water, and then it is over. You feel slightly heroic and slightly unwell, and you carry on with your day.

Most of the time, that is the end of the story. But not always. In two published medical cases, that exact maneuver — nose pinched, mouth closed, sneeze stifled — tore a hole in a man's throat or windpipe and put him in hospital. Doctors who treated those patients have since been unusually blunt about the habit. The question is whether those dramatic cases mean that holding in a sneeze is genuinely dangerous for ordinary people, or whether they are freak accidents that have been inflated by the internet into a health scare. The honest answer sits between those two poles, and it is worth understanding properly, because the difference between a harmless stifled sneeze and a dangerous one comes down to a detail most people never think about: exactly how completely you block the exit.

## Where the warning comes from

The warning did not start as an internet rumor. It started in medical journals, and it has been repeated by ear, nose, and throat specialists ever since. The most widely cited case was published in BMJ Case Reports in January 2018. The patient was a 34-year-old man, described as previously fit and well, who was treated at Leicester Royal Infirmary in England. He had tried to stop a forceful sneeze by pinching his nose and holding his mouth closed.

What happened next reads like a medical curiosity until you realize how frightening it must have felt. He noticed a popping sensation in his neck. Swallowing became painful. His voice changed. His neck swelled, and when he moved it, it produced a crackling, popping sensation under the skin. In the emergency department, X-rays showed streaks of air trapped in the soft tissues of his neck, a condition called subcutaneous emphysema, along with air in the central compartment of the chest, known as pneumomediastinum. The pressure of the stifled sneeze had ruptured the back of his throat, allowing air to escape into tissues where air does not belong.

He spent about a week in hospital, was fed through a tube while the injured area rested, and received intravenous antibiotics to guard against infection. At a follow-up appointment two months later, he had recovered fully. The doctors' conclusion was direct: "Halting sneeze via blocking nostrils and mouth is a dangerous maneuver and should be avoided." They also warned that the same maneuver could perforate the eardrum or even rupture a cerebral aneurysm, a ballooned blood vessel in the brain.

That was not the only case. In November 2023, doctors in Scotland published a second BMJ Case Reports account, this time of a man in his 30s with a background of allergic rhinitis, the medical name for hay fever. He was driving when he stifled a sneeze by pinching his nose and closing his mouth. He felt severe neck pain immediately, his neck swelled on both sides, and moving it became difficult. A CT scan of his neck and chest revealed a tear in his windpipe measuring just 2 millimetres by 2 millimetres by 5 millimetres, located between the third and fourth thoracic vertebrae, along with pneumomediastinum and surgical emphysema of the neck — air trapped beneath the skin.

Remarkably, he did not need surgery. He was admitted for two days of monitoring to make sure his oxygen levels and other vital signs stayed stable, treated with pain relief and hay fever medication, and advised to avoid strenuous activity for two weeks. A follow-up scan five weeks later confirmed that the tear had healed completely. The authors noted that, to their knowledge, this was the first reported case of a spontaneous tracheal perforation — a tear in the windpipe — caused by holding in a sneeze. Tracheal tears are normally associated with physical trauma or medical procedures such as intubation or thyroid surgery, not with an everyday bodily function.

These two cases are the backbone of the claim, and they are real. They were examined, scanned, treated, and published in a peer-reviewed journal. Anyone who tells you that holding in a sneeze can never hurt you is ignoring documented evidence.

## What a sneeze actually does to your body

To understand why blocking a sneeze can cause damage, it helps to understand what a sneeze is for. Sneezing is a protective reflex. When irritants — dust, pollen, a virus, a sudden blast of cold air — stimulate the inside of your nose, nerves send a signal to the brain, and the brain orchestrates a violent, coordinated expulsion. Your chest and abdominal muscles contract, pressure builds in your airways, and then the air is released in a sudden burst through your nose and mouth, carrying mucus and irritants out with it.

That burst is fast. Ear, nose, and throat specialists commonly describe sneeze airflow reaching speeds of up to around 100 miles per hour, which is one reason a single uncovered sneeze can spray droplets across a room. The pressure involved is also substantial. According to the 2023 BMJ Case Reports paper, the pressure in the upper airways during an ordinary sneeze is normally around 1 to 2 kilopascals. That sounds small until you learn what happens when the exits are sealed: if the mouth and nose are both closed, the pressure can increase by up to 20 times.

Twenty times the normal pressure, with nowhere to go, is the key to the whole story. Pressure does not simply vanish because you have decided not to sneeze in public. It is redirected. It pushes against the walls of the throat, the sinuses, and the delicate passages that connect the throat to the middle ear. In most people, the tissues absorb that force without any trouble. But tissues have limits, and in rare circumstances — a particularly forceful sneeze, an airway already irritated by allergies, a throat wall with a vulnerable spot — something gives way. In the 2018 case, it was the back of the throat. In the 2023 case, it was the windpipe itself.

This is also why the precise technique matters so much. The dangerous version of the maneuver is the complete block: both nostrils pinched shut and the mouth held closed at the same time. That is the version both hospital patients used, and it is the version the BMJ authors specifically warned against. A sneeze that escapes partly through an open mouth, or one that is merely muffled rather than fully sealed, does not trap pressure in the same way. The distinction is easy to miss in a viral post that simply says "never hold in a sneeze," but it is the most useful part of the medical advice.

## The other injuries doctors have linked to stifled sneezes

Throat and windpipe tears are the most dramatic injuries on record, but they are not the only ones that doctors and medical writers have associated with suppressing a sneeze. Understanding the full list helps explain why specialists take the habit seriously, and also why it is important not to exaggerate any single item on it.

The eardrum is one of the most plausible targets. The throat is connected to the middle ear by the Eustachian tubes, narrow passageways that normally help equalize pressure on either side of the eardrum. When a sneeze is blocked, air and pressure can be forced up those tubes into the middle ear, stretching the eardrum. In severe cases the eardrum can perforate — it can tear. A perforated eardrum typically causes pain and hearing loss, and although it often heals on its own over weeks, it can also invite middle-ear infection, particularly if the trapped air carries bacteria or infected mucus from the nasal passages with it. The 2018 case literature and subsequent medical summaries list tympanic membrane perforation, the formal name for a torn eardrum, among the complications of halting a sneeze.

The sinuses can suffer in a similar way. A sudden spike of pressure can inflame or damage sinus tissues, and the redirected air can push mucus into places it would not normally reach. Some medical summaries also list damage to blood vessels in the eyes — the tiny vessels that can burst and leave the white of the eye bloodshot — as a possible consequence of the pressure surge, along with injury to the voice box and, in a summary of reported sneeze injuries, even fractured bones of the face. These are not everyday outcomes. They are entries in a catalogue of rare, documented harms, and they share the same mechanism: a large amount of pressure, suddenly, with no exit.

The most alarming item on the list is the rupture of a cerebral aneurysm, a weakened, ballooned blood vessel in the brain. It is important to be precise here. The BMJ authors listed aneurysm rupture as a potential complication of the pressure surge, not as something that was shown to have happened to either of the two throat-tear patients. There is a difference between a risk that doctors warn about because the physics makes it plausible and an event that has actually been observed in a stifled-sneeze patient. Conflating the two is one of the ways a legitimate warning turns into an exaggerated scare story online. The aneurysm warning deserves to be reported accurately: it is a theoretical but serious possibility raised by specialists, not a documented outcome of holding in a sneeze.

There is a related point that cuts in the opposite direction. Sneezing itself, even when it is not suppressed at all, can occasionally cause injuries. Forceful sneezing has been linked in medical literature to problems ranging from rib fractures to back pain and, in very rare cases, more serious events. Nobody suggests that this means you should try to stop sneezing altogether. It means that the human body, when it generates sudden pressure, occasionally pays a price for it — and that deliberately multiplying that pressure by sealing every exit is a poor trade for politeness.

## How rare is "rare," really?

This is the question that decides the verdict, and it deserves a straight answer. Serious injury from holding in a sneeze is extremely rare. Consider the arithmetic. Billions of people sneeze every day, and a large fraction of them stifle at least some sneezes in polite company. Against that background, the published literature contains a handful of severe cases: the 2018 throat rupture, the 2023 windpipe tear, and a scattering of reports involving eardrums, sinuses, and related structures. If stifled sneezes routinely caused serious harm, emergency departments would be full of them. They are not.

That rarity matters for two reasons. First, it means that if you have stifled sneezes in the past — as almost everyone has — there is no reason to panic retroactively. The overwhelming likelihood is that nothing happened because nothing was going to happen. The tissues of most healthy people absorb a stifled sneeze without injury, just as they absorb countless other everyday pressure changes, from coughing to blowing your nose too hard. Second, it means that the correct public message is not terror but a simple change of habit. You do not need to fear sneezes. You need to stop sealing them completely.

There are, however, reasons to think some people face a higher risk than others. The 2023 patient had allergic rhinitis, which means his nasal passages and airways were already irritated and inflamed. Irritated tissue may be more vulnerable to a pressure spike, and people with allergies also sneeze more often and sometimes more forcefully, giving them more opportunities for a bad outcome. People with existing ear problems, sinus disease, or weakened blood vessels may also be more vulnerable, although the case literature is too thin to quantify those risks precisely. The honest summary is that the risk is low for everyone and probably somewhat higher for people whose airways are already inflamed — which is, notably, exactly the group most likely to be sneezing in the first place.

It is also worth noting what the cases do not show. Neither patient died, and both recovered fully — one after about a week in hospital, the other after two days of observation and five weeks of healing. The injuries were serious enough to require hospital care, but they were not fatal, and both men were sent home with the same simple advice: do not block your nose when you sneeze. A viral post that implies holding in a sneeze is likely to kill you would be overstating the evidence badly. A post that says it is completely harmless would be understating it. The evidence supports a middle verdict, and that is where this fact-check lands.

## The myth tucked inside the warning

Every good health scare seems to attract a bonus myth, and this one is no exception. You may have heard that sneezing with your eyes open will make your eyeballs pop out of your head. It will not. Your eyes are held firmly in place by muscles and by the optic nerve that anchors each eyeball, and the airways of your respiratory system have no connection to your eye sockets that could push an eyeball out. The reflex that closes your eyes when you sneeze is automatic and protective, but it is not load-bearing in the way the myth suggests. If your eyes happen to stay open during a sneeze, nothing dramatic will happen to them.

That myth is useful because it shows how easily a real mechanism gets garbled. The real mechanism is pressure trapped in the airways. The myth takes that pressure and applies it to the wrong organ. The same garbling happens with the stifled-sneeze warning itself: the real risk is a rare tear in the throat or windpipe, and the garbled version is that every suppressed sneeze is a brush with death. Both versions start from the same grain of truth. Only one of them survives contact with the evidence.

A second piece of folk wisdom deserves a brief check as well. Some people believe that pressing a finger hard under the nose or staring at a bright light can safely stop a sneeze before it starts. These tricks sometimes seem to work because a sneeze that is interrupted early, before the pressure builds, is a different event from a sneeze that is sealed at full force. But there is no reliable evidence that any trick can safely abort a sneeze that is already underway, and attempting to block a full sneeze is precisely the maneuver doctors warn against. The safest approach is not a trick. It is letting the sneeze happen, in a hygienic way.

## What to do instead

The medical advice here is refreshingly simple, and it does not require you to be rude. When you feel a sneeze coming, let it out — but catch it. Sneeze into a tissue if you have one, and throw the tissue away. If you do not have a tissue, sneeze into the crook of your elbow. That keeps droplets off your hands, which means you are less likely to spread germs when you open doors, shake hands, or touch shared surfaces. It also keeps the pressure problem away entirely, because the air has somewhere to go.

What you should not do is the full seal: pinching both nostrils shut while keeping your mouth closed. If you are in a situation where a full sneeze feels impossible — a moment of silence, a recording studio, a sleeping baby in your arms — the least bad option is to let at least some of the air escape, ideally through your mouth, rather than blocking every exit. Even a muffled, partially released sneeze traps far less pressure than a fully sealed one.

There are also warning signs worth knowing, because on the rare occasions when a stifled sneeze does cause injury, the symptoms are distinctive enough to act on. Seek medical care promptly if, after suppressing a sneeze, you notice swelling in the neck, a crackling or popping sensation under the skin of the neck, pain when swallowing, a change in your voice, sudden hearing loss or ear pain, chest pain, or difficulty breathing. Those were, in various combinations, the symptoms that sent both BMJ patients to hospital, and they are the symptoms that should send anyone else. Most of the time, none of them will appear. But knowing them turns a scary story into a useful one: if something does go wrong, you will recognize it early, and early recognition is part of why both documented patients recovered fully.

Parents sometimes ask whether children should be taught never to hold in a sneeze. The same advice applies, framed gently: teach children to sneeze into their elbow, not to pinch their nose shut. Children sneeze often, especially during cold season, and the habit of blocking sneezes is usually learned from watching adults do it. Changing the adult habit is the most effective way to change the child's.

It is worth placing this advice alongside other cold-season myths that circulate every year. The idea that going outside with wet hair gives you a cold, for example, confuses a real virus with a folk explanation — colds are caused by viruses, not by damp hair, as our earlier fact-check on <a href="/blog/wet-hair-cold-weather-causes-colds-fact-check/">wet hair and colds</a> explains. Similarly, the belief that a <a href="/blog/flu-vaccine-gives-you-flu-fact-check/">flu vaccine can give you the flu</a> mistakes the body's immune response for an infection, and the claim that <a href="/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/">vitamin D prevents colds and flu</a> overstates what the supplement evidence actually shows. In each case, the pattern is the same: a small kernel of truth, wrapped in an explanation that goes too far. The stifled-sneeze warning follows the pattern in reverse — a dramatic claim that turns out to be smaller, and more specific, than it first appears.

## The Verdict

So, is it dangerous to hold in a sneeze? Mostly True — with a qualifier that matters as much as the verdict itself.

The danger is real. Two men, in cases published in BMJ Case Reports in 2018 and 2023, tore their throat and windpipe respectively by pinching their nose and closing their mouth during a forceful sneeze. The mechanism is well understood: sealing both exits can multiply airway pressure by up to 20 times, and that pressure has to go somewhere. Doctors who treated these patients explicitly advised against the maneuver, and related injuries — including eardrum perforation and sinus damage — have also been reported.

But the danger is also rare, and the claim becomes misleading if it is stated without that context. Billions of stifled sneezes happen without injury. Both documented throat-tear patients recovered fully. No one in the published cases died, and the most frightening complication on the doctors' list — the rupture of a brain aneurysm — was raised as a potential risk, not reported as an observed outcome. A claim that holding in a sneeze will probably hurt you would be false. A claim that it cannot hurt you would also be false.

The verdict, stated plainly: holding in a sneeze by blocking your nose and mouth is a genuinely dangerous maneuver that very rarely causes serious injury. That combination — real but rare — is exactly why the advice is worth following. The cost of following it is nothing: you sneeze into your elbow instead of into a sealed airway. The cost of ignoring it is small but not zero, and unlike most health risks, this one is entirely avoidable with a habit change that takes no effort at all.

## The last word

The next time a sneeze builds in an awkward moment, remember the two men in the case reports. Neither of them expected a trip to hospital. Both of them did the same polite thing millions of people do, and both of them discovered that politeness, applied with both hands clamped over the face, can have a physical price. Then remember the rest of the story: both recovered, the injuries are vanishingly rare, and the fix is trivially easy.

Let the sneeze out. Catch it in your elbow. Nobody in the room will remember it five minutes later, and your throat, your ears, and your windpipe will never have to find out what twenty times normal pressure feels like. There are plenty of health risks worth worrying about. This one is not among them — provided you stop giving it the chance.
