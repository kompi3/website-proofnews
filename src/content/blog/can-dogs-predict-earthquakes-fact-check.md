Your dog starts pacing at 2 a.m. She whines at the back door, stares at the wall, refuses her bed, and will not settle no matter what you try. An hour later, the ground shakes. Coincidence? Plenty of dog owners would swear it is not. The idea that dogs can predict earthquakes is one of the oldest and most stubborn beliefs in the long folklore of natural disasters, retold in every earthquake-prone country on Earth, from ancient Greece to modern California. And like many stubborn beliefs, it survives because it contains a grain of truth — wrapped in a much larger misunderstanding.

The question matters more than it might seem. If dogs really could forecast earthquakes hours or days ahead, that ability would be worth more than any seismometer ever built. A reliable animal warning system could save thousands of lives in places where high-tech monitoring is thin. That is exactly why scientists have spent decades testing the claim properly, with sensors, statistics, and controlled studies, instead of simply collecting stories. What they found is both more interesting and more humbling than the viral version of the story: dogs are genuinely better than humans at noticing an earthquake that has already started, and genuinely no better than humans at knowing one is coming.

This fact-check separates the two ideas that the myth carelessly merges — prediction and detection — and follows the evidence through the United States Geological Survey's verdict, the most famous animal-evacuation story in history, and the most rigorous sensor study ever conducted on farm animals before earthquakes.

## The Claim: Your Dog Knows Before the Ground Moves

The claim, as it circulates on social media, in pet-owner forums, and in local news segments after every major quake, goes roughly like this: dogs can sense an earthquake before it happens — sometimes minutes, sometimes hours, sometimes even days ahead — and their strange behavior is a warning that owners should heed. The behaviors cited are almost always the same: restless pacing, whining or barking at nothing, hiding under beds, digging at doors, clinging to their owners, or bolting outside for no apparent reason.

The claim feels plausible because it is built on real, observable experiences. After the 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan, researchers surveyed pet owners and found that more than 18 percent of dog owners and 16 percent of cat owners had noticed unusual behavior in their animals before the quake, with the changes appearing anywhere from one to 20 hours beforehand — and the closer the pets lived to the epicenter, the more likely they were to act strangely. Surveys after other quakes have produced similar numbers. In Taiwan, research from 1999 found dogs and cats reacting before large quakes in roughly 70 percent of cases. Those are not numbers you can simply wave away, and scientists have not waved them away. They have investigated them, carefully, for decades.

But there is a critical distinction hiding inside those surveys, and it is the distinction on which this entire fact-check turns. Every one of those reports is retrospective: owners were asked, after the earthquake, whether their pet had behaved unusually beforehand. Human memory is a generous editor. After a frightening event, we remember the odd things and forget the ordinary ones. A dog that paces on a random Tuesday is just a restless dog; a dog that paces on the Tuesday before an earthquake becomes a prophet. That does not mean owners are lying. It means the method of collecting the evidence is tilted from the start, and seismologists have known this for a very long time.

## What the USGS Actually Says

The United States Geological Survey, the agency that monitors earthquakes across America, has studied this question since the 1970s, and its answer is blunt. On its official page addressing the question, the USGS states that changes in animal behavior cannot be used to predict earthquakes. The agency acknowledges that there have been documented cases of unusual animal behavior before earthquakes, but adds that a reproducible connection between a specific behavior and the occurrence of an earthquake has never been made.

That word — reproducible — does the heavy lifting. Science does not ask whether something happened once, or even whether it happened many times. It asks whether it happens reliably, in a way that can be tested in advance, with a clear signal and a low enough false-alarm rate to be useful. By that standard, animal earthquake prediction fails. The USGS explains why the failures are so easy to miss: animals change their behavior for many reasons, and given that an earthquake can shake millions of people, it is likely that a few of their pets will, by chance, be acting strangely before it happens. In a city of a million dog owners, thousands of dogs will be pacing, barking, or hiding on any given night, for reasons that have nothing to do with geology. When the ground shakes, those thousands of ordinary restless dogs suddenly look like thousands of successful predictions.

The USGS also points to the other half of the story, the part the myth gets right: because of their finely tuned senses, animals can often feel an earthquake at its earliest stages, before the humans around them can. That feeds the myth that the animal knew the earthquake was coming. The animal did not know. It felt the quake a few seconds earlier than you did, because the quake had already begun.

## The P-Wave Secret: Seconds, Not Hours

To understand what dogs actually do, you need to understand how an earthquake travels. When rock breaks deep underground, it releases energy in waves. The first to arrive are the primary waves, or P-waves: fast, relatively small compression waves that race outward from the source. Seconds later come the secondary waves, or S-waves: slower, larger, and far more destructive, the waves that knock pictures off walls and make the floor roll under your feet. Most humans barely notice a P-wave. Many animals do.

Dogs hear far beyond the human range. While human hearing tops out near 20,000 hertz, dogs can hear frequencies commonly cited up to around 45,000 to 60,000 hertz, and they are exquisitely sensitive to low-frequency vibrations through the ground and through their paws. A dog lying on a wooden floor is, in effect, a living vibration sensor pressed against a sounding board. When the faint P-wave arrives, a dog may startle, jump up, bark, or run — seconds before its owner feels the S-wave and realizes what is happening. There is a well-documented video from the 2001 Nisqually earthquake near Seattle that captures exactly this: a dog sleeping on the floor gets up and runs moments before the building visibly shakes, because the dog felt the first wave arriving while the humans in the room felt nothing at all.

Modern earthquake early-warning systems work on precisely the same principle, just with machines instead of dogs. Systems such as ShakeAlert in the United States detect the fast P-waves with a network of seismometers and broadcast a warning before the slower, damaging waves arrive. Depending on distance from the epicenter, that warning can arrive 60 to 90 seconds before the strong shaking starts — enough time to drop, cover, and hold on, or to slow a train. A dog gives you the same warning, unreliably, for a few seconds, with no information about how big the quake will be or where it is centered. That is detection, and it is real. It is not prediction.

## Haicheng 1975: The One Time Animals Seemed to Save a City

No discussion of animal earthquake prediction is complete without Haicheng, China, the single most famous case in the field and the closest the idea has ever come to working in real life. In the dead of winter, in the weeks before February 4, 1975, hibernating snakes in the region around the city of Haicheng woke up, left their burrows, and froze to death on snowy roads. Rats appeared disoriented, chickens refused to enter their coops, and cows and dogs behaved strangely. Officials, already watching a sequence of foreshocks that had been rattling the region for months, ordered an evacuation. On February 4, a magnitude 7.3 earthquake leveled the city. More than 1,000 people were killed — a terrible toll, but in a densely populated city, the evacuation is widely considered to have saved tens of thousands of lives, and the animals were credited with the alert.

It is a magnificent story, and it is mostly true. But the version that circulates today leaves out the parts that matter most. The evacuation was not ordered because snakes woke up. It was ordered because seismologists had been tracking foreshocks in the region for months, authorities had suspected for years that a larger earthquake could strike an area with a history of strong quakes, and the animal reports arrived on top of a growing pile of geophysical evidence. The animals were reacting, in large part, to the near-daily tremors and foreshocks that humans could also measure — not foreseeing an earthquake out of a calm and silent Earth. The snakes were not oracles. They were the first to notice a swarm of small quakes that the instruments were already recording.

The contrast with what happened a year later is devastating. In 1976, animals around the city of Tangshan also behaved abnormally before a massive earthquake — but this time there was no evacuation, and more than 240,000 people died. The same kind of animal behavior, in the same country, produced a celebrated success in one city and a catastrophe in another, because animal behavior alone was never the deciding factor. The difference was the foreshock sequence, the monitoring, and the decision to act. Haicheng proves that animal behavior can be one clue among many. It does not prove that animals can predict earthquakes.

The history of the idea stretches back much further than 1975. The earliest documented case comes from the Roman historian Aelian, who recorded that five days before an earthquake destroyed the Greek city of Helike in 373 B.C., rats, weasels, snakes, centipedes, and bugs emerged from underground and fled the city. In 1976, one day before a magnitude 6.5 earthquake devastated the Friuli region of Italy, mice were seen running in open spaces and farm animals showed signs of restlessness. These stories have been retold for centuries, and they are genuinely intriguing. But as researchers who study the history of the field have pointed out, almost all of them are anecdotes reported after the earthquake, based on a subjective impression of what counted as unusual behavior at the time. The plural of anecdote is not data — although anecdotes can be used to form a hypothesis, and that is exactly what modern researchers have done.

## The Italian Farm Study: The Best Evidence Yet

The most serious attempt to turn those anecdotes into data came from a team led by Martin Wikelski of the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior, working with colleagues from the University of Konstanz and other institutions. Their study, published in the journal Ethology in July 2020, was the first to measure animal behavior before earthquakes with the precision that the question demands.

Over two periods totaling about four months in 2016 and 2017, the researchers fitted six cows, five sheep, and two dogs living on a farm in an earthquake-prone area of northern Italy with highly sensitive biologgers and GPS sensors, recording accelerated movements up to 48 times per second in any direction. More than 18,000 tremors occurred during the study periods, including a magnitude 6.6 earthquake and its aftershocks. The team's statistical analysis, which accounted for the animals' normal daily movements and their interactions with one another, found that the animals' activity increased significantly before earthquakes of magnitude 3.8 or greater — but only when they were housed together in a stable, not when they were out to pasture. The animals appeared to anticipate tremors anywhere from one to 20 hours ahead, reacting earlier when they were closer to the future epicenter and later when they were farther away. The pattern even had a social dynamic: the cows would freeze in place, the dogs would go crazy, and then the cows would grow even more agitated, amplifying one another's behavior in turn.

It was, by the standards of this field, a remarkable result, and Wikelski's team was careful about what it did and did not show. A definitive link has still not been proved, they said, and their single study could not distinguish among all the possible stimuli the animals might have been reacting to. The lead time shrank with distance from the epicenter, which is consistent with the animals sensing some physical precursor — perhaps low-frequency sounds from grinding rock, shifts in radon gas, static electricity, or changes in the local electromagnetic field — rather than possessing any mysterious sixth sense. But the study involved only 13 animals on one farm, the effect appeared only when the animals were indoors, and no other research group has yet replicated the result at scale. Seven out of eight strong quakes in one small study is a lead worth following. It is not a warning system.

Other researchers have pursued the same question with different animals and different tools. A review of 729 reports cataloged behaviors ranging from fish jumping out of ponds to toads abandoning a breeding site days before a magnitude 6.3 earthquake in Italy. Chinese authorities once considered 58 species of animals potentially useful for earthquake prediction and distributed booklets illustrating the unusual behaviors worth reporting, and a bureau in Nanning monitored 143 snake nests for 48-hour warnings. On Mount Etna in Sicily, goats wearing GPS trackers have shown bursts of agitation hours before volcanic eruptions, a pattern researchers link to subtle tremor and ground deformation. Each of these threads is interesting. None of them, alone or together, has produced a system that can tell a city, in advance, that a damaging earthquake is coming — with a date, a place, and a magnitude — and be right often enough, and wrong rarely enough, to justify an evacuation.

## Why the Evidence Still Falls Short

The obstacles to proving animal prediction are not mysterious. They are the ordinary, stubborn obstacles of any attempt to find a weak signal in a noisy world, and each one has been demonstrated in practice.

First is the false-alarm problem. Dogs bark at thunder, fireworks, sirens, strangers, squirrels, and nothing at all. They pace when they are anxious, bored, hungry, or need to go outside. Any behavior common enough to be noticed before an earthquake is also common enough to happen constantly when there is no earthquake. For a warning system to be useful, it needs not just hits but a tolerable rate of false alarms. Nobody has ever shown that any animal behavior clears that bar, because nobody has run the necessary test: recording behavior continuously, in advance, and counting both the hits and the misses. Almost every report in the literature counts only the hits.

Second is the recall problem. The most famous debunking of a specific animal-prediction claim came from a statistical analysis published in California Geology in 1988. A theory had circulated that the number of lost-pet advertisements in the San Jose Mercury News increased before earthquakes in the Bay Area — as if frightened animals were fleeing their homes ahead of the shaking. The analysis showed the pattern did not hold up. What looked like a signal was, once counted properly, noise. The same flaw haunts the owner surveys after major quakes: people remember the odd behavior that preceded the disaster and forget the identical odd behavior on the hundred quiet days before it. Memory is not a dataset.

Third is the replication problem. The Wikelski study is the strongest evidence the field has, and it is one study, on one farm, with 13 animals, in a region that happened to be seismically active during the study window. Extraordinary claims require replication in different places, with different animals, by different teams — and that replication has not arrived. Until it does, the responsible scientific position is the one the USGS has held since the 1970s: intriguing, unproven, and not usable as a prediction tool. Researchers continue to investigate, including projects that fit animals with wearable sensors and use artificial intelligence to look for patterns across thousands of animals at once. Wikelski himself is leading an effort to track GPS-tagged animals worldwide from satellites, in the hope that a large enough dataset will reveal what small studies cannot. That is how science is supposed to work: the anecdotes became a hypothesis, the hypothesis became an experiment, and the experiment produced a lead that now needs far more testing.

It is worth noting that the same folklore surrounds other animals, and it fails the same tests. The <a href="/blog/doomsday-fish-oarfish-earthquakes-fact-check/">oarfish, the deep-sea "doomsday fish" said to surface before earthquakes</a>, has no reliable predictive record either. The pattern is always the same: a striking story, a plausible mechanism, and evidence that dissolves under controlled counting — the same shape as the belief that <a href="/blog/can-the-government-control-the-weather-fact-check/">the government can secretly control the weather</a>, or that <a href="/blog/full-moon-behavior-myth-fact-check/">the full moon changes sleep and behavior</a>. Believing that dogs can predict earthquakes is not foolish — the stories are vivid and the animals really do sense things we cannot. But believing it without the counting is how folklore survives long past its sell-by date.

## The San Diego Elephants: Sensing, Not Predicting

If you want a vivid illustration of what animals actually do around earthquakes, the best recent example comes not from dogs but from elephants — and it shows the difference between prediction and detection more clearly than any study could. On Monday, April 14, 2025, a magnitude 5.2 earthquake struck near Julian, California, and was felt from San Diego to Los Angeles, some 120 miles away. At the San Diego Zoo Safari Park in Escondido, surveillance cameras captured five African elephants standing in the morning sun as the camera began to shake. Within seconds, the herd scattered in different directions — and then the three adult females, Ndlula, Umngani, and Khosi, quickly regrouped and formed a tight circle around the two 7-year-old calves, Zuli and Mkhaya, facing outward, ears spread, in a formation keepers call an "alert circle." They held the formation for about four minutes, and huddled again briefly when an aftershock hit about an hour later.

The video went viral, and many of the headlines implied the elephants had known the quake was coming. Watch the footage carefully, though, and the sequence tells a different story. The elephants reacted as the ground began to move — not before it. Elephants can feel sound through their feet, using sensitive receptors that pick up vibrations humans cannot, and the alert circle is a standard defensive response to any perceived threat, from a predator to a tremor. "When an elephant senses danger, the herd's instinct is to protect their youngest," the park's mammal curator Mindy Albright explained. The elephants did something extraordinary and real: they sensed the shaking sooner and responded to it faster than the humans watching them. What they did not do was predict it. Nobody who has studied the footage claims they began circling before the first wave arrived.

The same logic applies to the dog in your living room. If she bolts upright and stares at the wall a few seconds before you feel the floor move, she is not a prophet. She is a better seismometer than you are, for about five seconds, with no readout. That is genuinely impressive. It is also genuinely useless as a warning system, because you cannot tell — in the moment — whether she is reacting to a P-wave or to a squirrel.

## What Dogs Actually Hear and Feel

None of this diminishes dogs. If anything, the science makes them more remarkable, not less. A dog's hearing spans a range far beyond ours, extending into ultrasonic frequencies we cannot perceive, and their sense of smell is famously tens of thousands of times more sensitive than a human's. Their paws are packed with mechanoreceptors that detect minute vibrations in the ground. Before an earthquake, rocks deep underground grind and crack, releasing high-frequency and low-frequency sound waves that humans cannot hear. Shifts in radon gas seeping from stressed faults, static electricity building in the air, and tiny fluctuations in the Earth's magnetic field have all been proposed as signals that animals might detect. Some of these mechanisms are plausible. None of them has been shown to give an animal — or a person — reliable advance notice of a specific earthquake.

What dogs are genuinely good at is the thing the myth accidentally captures: noticing that something has changed, right now, in the environment around them. That is why search-and-rescue dogs can find people buried under rubble, why medical-alert dogs can detect the onset of a seizure, and why your dog knows you have opened the treat bag from two rooms away. Their senses are real, and they are extraordinary. The myth's error is to take a real superpower — sensing the present — and stretch it into a different superpower entirely: knowing the future.

It is also worth asking what a world that took the myth seriously would look like. If dogs could predict earthquakes, the rational response would be to station dogs at seismological observatories and let them do the work of billion-dollar sensor networks. Nobody does this, not because scientists are closed-minded, but because every attempt to make the idea work — including the USGS research program in the 1970s and the Chinese monitoring programs that tracked dozens of species — failed to produce a reproducible signal. The absence is not for lack of trying. It is the result of trying, carefully, for fifty years.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Dogs cannot predict earthquakes. No study has ever shown that dogs, or any animal, can reliably forecast an earthquake hours or days in advance, and the United States Geological Survey states plainly that animal behavior cannot be used for prediction because no reproducible connection has ever been established. The best scientific study on the question, the 2020 Max Planck Institute tracking of farm animals in Italy, found intriguing bursts of activity one to 20 hours before some tremors — but on one farm, with 13 animals, in a single study that has not been replicated, and its own authors stressed that a definitive link has not been proved.

The claim survives because it is built around a real phenomenon that it mislabels. Dogs genuinely do react to earthquakes before their owners do — by seconds, not hours — because their sharper hearing and sensitivity to ground vibrations let them feel the fast, faint P-waves that arrive before the destructive S-waves. The San Diego elephants did the same thing in April 2025, forming their protective circle as the ground moved, not before it. Sensing a quake that has already started is not the same as predicting one that has not, and confusing the two is exactly what makes this claim misleading rather than simply false.

## What To Do Instead

If you live in earthquake country, your dog is not your early-warning system — but you do have real ones. In the western United States, the ShakeAlert system sends alerts to phones seconds before strong shaking arrives, the same P-wave principle, automated and tested. Japan's J-Alert and Mexico's SASMEX do the same for millions of people. Learning the drop, cover, and hold on drill, securing heavy furniture, and knowing your building's evacuation routes will protect you in ways that watching your pet never will.

None of that means you should ignore your dog. If she suddenly bolts upright, stares at nothing, and will not settle, pay attention — she may have felt the first faint tremor of a quake that is already under way, and those few seconds of warning are worth having. Just do not mistake them for prophecy. She is not telling you the future. She is telling you the present, a few seconds before you can feel it yourself — and in an earthquake, a few seconds is still a gift.

The belief that dogs can predict earthquakes has lasted for more than two thousand years, from Helike to Haicheng to the viral elephant video of 2025, because it flatters something deep in us: the hope that the natural world is watching out for us, and that our oldest companions can warn us of dangers we cannot see. The science says something quieter and, in its own way, more wonderful. Your dog cannot see the future. But she can hear the Earth move a moment before you can — and she has been doing it all along, without any need for a myth to make her extraordinary.
