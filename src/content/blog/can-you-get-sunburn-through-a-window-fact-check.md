Picture the scene: it is a bright afternoon, you are sitting in a sunny spot beside a big window, and the warmth on your skin feels wonderful. No hat, no sunscreen, no worries — because you are indoors, and glass keeps the sun out. Right?

That comfortable assumption is one of the most widespread beliefs about sun safety, and it is wrong in a way that matters. The claim that windows fully protect you from the sun is misleading. A window does shield you from the worst of the burning rays, but it lets through a large share of the invisible radiation that ages your skin and raises your risk of skin cancer. The damage does not announce itself with redness or pain, which is exactly why it catches people off guard. The science of what glass does — and does not — stop is clear, measurable, and more surprising than most people expect.

## The Claim

The belief comes in a few different forms. Some people say you cannot get sunburned through a window at all. Others say you cannot tan through glass, or that sitting indoors is automatically safe from ultraviolet radiation. A related version holds that car windows, being specially made, block everything. These ideas get repeated in offices, in cars, and in online advice threads, and they feel like common sense: glass is a solid barrier, and if you cannot feel your skin burning, nothing bad must be happening.

The evidence tells a split story. The first part of the belief is mostly true: a classic, red, painful sunburn through an ordinary window is rare. The second part — that you are therefore safe — is false. Windows are selective filters, not walls. They block one kind of ultraviolet radiation almost completely and let much of another kind pass straight through. To understand why that matters, you have to know the two kinds of ultraviolet light that reach the Earth's surface.

## UVA and UVB: Two Different Kinds of Damage

Sunlight contains two types of ultraviolet radiation that reach the ground in meaningful amounts. UVB, with wavelengths of roughly 280 to 315 nanometres, is the shorter, more energetic kind. It strikes the outer layer of the skin, damages DNA directly, and triggers the redness, pain, and peeling we call sunburn. It also stimulates melanin production, which is what creates a lasting tan, and it is the wavelength the skin uses to make vitamin D.

UVA, with wavelengths of roughly 315 to 400 nanometres, is longer and less energetic per photon, but it makes up about 95 percent of the ultraviolet radiation that reaches the Earth's surface — UVB accounts for only about 5 percent. UVA penetrates much deeper into the skin, reaching the dermis, the living layer beneath the surface where collagen and elastin fibers give skin its firmness and elasticity. It does not usually cause an immediate burn, so it works quietly: over years it breaks down those structural proteins, producing wrinkles, sagging, dark spots, and uneven pigmentation. It also causes indirect DNA damage and contributes to skin cancer, including melanoma. Dermatologists sometimes use a simple memory aid: UVB is the "burning" ray, UVA is the "aging" ray.

This distinction is the key to the whole question. A window's effect on the two kinds is dramatically different, and that difference is what makes the popular belief misleading rather than simply true or false.

## What Glass Actually Blocks

Ordinary window glass is remarkably good at stopping UVB. Laboratory measurements of multiple glass types, thicknesses, and colors have found that every sample tested completely blocked UVB radiation at every distance from the light source. Other measurements put the figure at roughly 97 percent of UVB absorbed by standard glass. Either way, the practical result is the same: the rays that cause a classic sunburn almost never make it through a window.

UVA is a different story. The same experiments found that smooth ordinary glass — the kind in most residential windows — transmitted about 74 percent of the UVA that hit it. Tempered glass was close behind at around 72 percent. Textured or imprinted glass performed somewhat better, dropping to roughly 45 percent, probably because its uneven surface scatters and absorbs more light. In other words, a single pane of ordinary clear glass lets roughly three quarters of the aging, cancer-linked ultraviolet radiation straight through.

That is not a laboratory curiosity. It is the difference between feeling safe and being safe. Sitting beside a sunny window, you will not feel the sting of a sunburn, because the UVB is gone. But the UVA is still reaching your skin in a substantial dose, and it is doing its slow damage without any warning signal at all.

## Car Windows: The Windshield Is Not the Side Window

Cars make the point even more sharply, because the glass in a car is not all the same. A car's front windshield is made of laminated safety glass: two layers of glass bonded together with a plastic interlayer. That interlayer is an excellent ultraviolet filter. A study of 29 automobiles across a range of makes and model years found that windshields blocked an average of 96 percent of UVA, with a tight range between 95 and 98 percent. A separate 2016 study published in JAMA Ophthalmology by Brian S. Boxer Wachler measured the same thing and found windshields blocked up to 96 percent of UVA rays.

Side and rear windows are a different matter. They are made of tempered glass, which is designed to shatter safely, not to filter ultraviolet light. The same 2016 study found that a driver's side window blocked only about 71 percent of UVA — meaning nearly a third got through. The 29-car study found an even wider range: between 4 percent and almost 56 percent of UVA passed through side and rear windows, depending on the type of glass, its color, and whether it had been tinted or coated. The protection had nothing to do with the car's age or price; it depended entirely on the glass itself. Gray or bronze colored glass and window tinting improved the numbers considerably, with tinting blocking around 95 percent of UVA.

The practical consequence is visible in the real world. Researchers in Saudi Arabia fitted drivers with wearable radiation monitors and found they were exposed to ultraviolet index ratings of up to 3.5 inside their cars — a level at which fair skin can burn in under an hour outdoors. And a study in the United States, where drivers sit on the left, found more skin cancers on the left side than the right for the face, scalp, arm, and leg — including twenty times more cancers on the left arm. The window in front of a driver protects well; the window beside a driver protects far less.

## The Truck Driver Whose Face Told the Story

The most famous single piece of evidence is a photograph that has circulated in dermatology textbooks and news articles for years. It shows a 69-year-old delivery-truck driver who had spent 28 years behind the wheel. The left side of his face — the side closest to the driver's window — is deeply wrinkled, with thickened skin, open comedones, and small nodules. The right side of his face looks decades younger. The contrast is so stark that it looks like two different people stitched together.

The case was documented in a clinical report in the New England Journal of Medicine. Doctors diagnosed unilateral dermatoheliosis, a form of long-term skin damage caused by uneven ultraviolet exposure, and noted findings consistent with Favre-Racouchot syndrome, a condition associated with heavily sun-damaged skin. The man reported that the thickening and wrinkling on the left side of his face had developed gradually over about 25 years. The report connected the one-sided damage directly to his long history on the road and the ability of UVA to penetrate glass.

He was not sunbathing. He was not sitting on a beach. He was simply driving, day after day, beside a closed window. The medical report did not identify skin cancer in the driver, but the damage was severe, measurable, and entirely one-sided. It remains the clearest human demonstration that window-filtered sunlight is not harmless sunlight.

A separate study of habitual drivers, published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2010, found the same pattern in a group rather than a single person: the window-exposed side of drivers' faces showed significantly more wrinkles on the cheek, deeper crow's feet, greater skin laxity, and measurably deeper wrinkle volume than the opposite side, along with differences in skin hydration and color uniformity. These were ordinary people who had not been deliberately sunbathing; they had simply spent enough time beside glass for the cumulative UVA dose to leave a visible, measurable imprint.

## Can You Tan Through a Window?

The same physics answers the tanning question. A real, lasting tan requires UVB to stimulate melanocytes to produce new melanin, and UVB is blocked by glass — so a deep, lasting tan through a window is not really possible. But UVA does cause a form of immediate darkening by oxidizing the melanin that is already in the skin. That effect is weak and short-lived, and it does not produce the lasting tan that UVB-stimulated melanogenesis creates. So if you spend a long time sitting by a window in strong sun, you might notice a slight darkening from UVA, but you will not get the tan you would get outdoors, and the American Cancer Society notes that typical home, office, and car windows block most UVB rays but only a smaller amount of UVA rays.

That asymmetry is the trap. People judge sun exposure by whether they tan or burn, and through glass neither happens in the usual way — so they assume nothing is happening. In reality, the most dangerous part of the exposure is the part that produces no visible signal at all.

## What About Vitamin D?

One more common question follows naturally: if sunlight comes through the window, can you make vitamin D indoors? The answer is no, and the reason is the same physics. Vitamin D synthesis in the skin is triggered specifically by UVB radiation, and as every glass sample tested has confirmed, UVB does not pass through window glass. Sitting in a sunny window seat will not raise your vitamin D levels, no matter how bright the light feels. The warmth you feel is visible light and infrared radiation, which pass through glass easily; the specific ultraviolet wavelength your skin needs for vitamin D does not.

This is worth knowing because it punctures both sides of the misunderstanding at once. Glass does not let through the rays you want, and it does let through the rays you do not want. It is a bad filter in both directions.

## Home and Office Windows

The numbers vary with the kind of glass in the building. Most glass used in residential windows lets a lot of UVA pass through, between 45 and 75 percent. Single-pane glass lets through the most, while thicker, tinted, or coated glass blocks more. The best options — laminated glass or double-glazed, tinted windows — allow less than 1 percent of UVA through. Most office and commercial window glass has better UVA protection than residential windows, allowing less than 25 percent of UVA transmission, because modern commercial glazing often includes low-emissivity coatings that also cut ultraviolet light.

That means the risk depends heavily on where you sit. Someone working for years at a desk beside an old single-pane window in a sunny room is getting a steady daily dose of UVA. Someone in a modern office tower with coated glazing is getting far less. Most people have no idea what kind of glass their windows are made of, which is part of the problem: the protection is invisible, and so is the lack of it.

The timing matters too. UVA is present all year round, from morning to evening, and it passes through clouds as well as glass. Unlike UVB, which peaks at midday in summer, UVA is a steady background exposure. A sunny window seat in winter still delivers UVA, and the damage accumulates over years of ordinary, unnoticed exposure.

## So Can You Actually Get Sunburned Through a Window?

Here is the honest, precise answer. A classic red sunburn through ordinary window glass is very unlikely under normal conditions, because sunburn is a UVB effect and glass blocks nearly all UVB. Healthline's medical review puts it plainly: it is unlikely, but it really depends on the window, how long you sit there, and the strength of the sun. With prolonged exposure on a day of very high UV index — sitting for hours pressed against a thin single pane in strong tropical sun, for instance — a slight burn is possible, and some sources note that it can happen in rare cases of very long exposure. But for most people, in most situations, the window will spare them the burn.

That is why the verdict here is Misleading rather than False. The statement "you can't get sunburned through a window" is close enough to true for the narrow question of sunburn that calling it flatly false would be dishonest. But as a statement about safety, it is dangerously misleading, because it invites people to believe that glass makes them safe from the sun. It does not. It removes the warning signal — the burn — while leaving most of the long-term risk in place. The belief is wrong in the way that matters most: it tells people not to protect themselves in exactly the situation where protection still helps.

## What You Should Actually Do

The good news is that the fixes are simple, and none of them requires giving up your sunny window seat. Dermatologists and the Skin Cancer Foundation offer the same practical advice for indoor and in-car ultraviolet exposure, and it comes down to a handful of habits.

First, wear broad-spectrum sunscreen on exposed skin if you spend long periods beside a sunny window or driving, especially on the side facing the glass. Sunscreen is not only for the beach. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 broad-spectrum as a minimum, and the same logic that applies to <a href="/blog/can-you-get-sunburn-on-a-cloudy-day-fact-check/">can you get sunburn on a cloudy day</a> applies here: ultraviolet radiation does not switch off just because you are under a roof. If you drive for a living or sit at a sunny desk every day, daily sunscreen on the face, neck, and arms is a reasonable habit, not an overreaction.

Second, think about the glass itself. Window film and tinting can block around 95 percent of UVA, and laminated glass is dramatically better than ordinary tempered glass. If you are renovating, choosing laminated or UV-coated glazing for the sunniest windows is a genuine health investment. In a car, the windshield already protects you well; the side windows are the weak point, and legal window tint on side windows closes much of the gap.

Third, use distance and shade. Ultraviolet intensity falls off quickly as you move away from the window, and a sheer curtain or blind cuts the dose substantially. You do not need to sit in the dark — just not pressed against the glass in direct sun for hours. And remember that the side of your body facing the window is the side getting the dose, so switching sides, or rotating your desk, spreads the exposure.

Fourth, do not expect the window to help with vitamin D. If you need more vitamin D, you need direct outdoor sun on your skin, or a dietary source, or a supplement — not more time in the window seat. The glass that blocks the vitamin-D wavelength is the same glass that lets the aging wavelength through, so sitting longer indoors gains you nothing and costs you skin health.

Finally, pay attention to your skin the way you would after years outdoors. The truck driver's case is a reminder that the damage is cumulative and one-sided, and that it shows up as asymmetry: one side of the face aging faster than the other, one arm more spotted than the other. If you notice that pattern, it is worth mentioning to a dermatologist — and worth changing where you sit.

## The Verdict

Can you get sunburn through a window? Almost never in the classic sense: ordinary glass blocks roughly 97 percent of the UVB rays that cause sunburn, so a red, painful burn through a window is rare. But the belief behind the question — that windows protect you from the sun — is misleading. The same glass lets roughly half to three quarters of UVA radiation through, and UVA penetrates deep into the skin, breaks down collagen and elastin, causes premature aging and dark spots, and contributes to skin cancer. The evidence is not theoretical: a 69-year-old truck driver developed severe, one-sided facial aging after 28 years beside a driver's side window, studies of drivers show measurably more damage on the window-facing side, and measurements of real cars and homes confirm that most side windows and residential panes transmit a large share of UVA. Laminated windshields are the exception, blocking about 96 percent of UVA; side windows, rear windows, and ordinary house glass are not.

So the honest answer has two parts. No, you will probably not get sunburned through a window. But yes, the sun is still reaching your skin through that window, and the damage it does is the slow, silent kind — the kind you only notice years later, when one side of your face looks older than the other. The window takes away the warning and leaves the risk. That is not protection; that is a false sense of security, and it is exactly why the claim is misleading.

If the question is whether you need sunscreen indoors, the answer is the same one that applies to <a href="/blog/sunscreen-prevents-aging-fact-check/">does sunscreen prevent aging</a> and to <a href="/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/">does vitamin D prevent colds and flu</a>: the science is clear, the dose is real, and the habit is cheap. A little sunscreen, a little distance from the glass, and a little respect for the invisible part of sunlight will do more for your skin over twenty years than any window ever will.
