It is one of the creepiest "facts" in circulation, and it has survived for generations precisely because it is creepy. Open a coffin after a few days, the story goes, and you will find that the dead person's beard has grown, their fingernails have lengthened, and the body has quietly kept up a little of its grooming routine without them. The image is powerful, it is disturbing, and it is completely wrong.

Hair and nails do not keep growing after death. They cannot. The apparent growth that people have reported for centuries is an illusion, produced not by new growth but by the shrinking of everything around the hair and nails. The skin dries out, pulls back, and exposes more of the hair shaft and the nail plate that were already there. Nothing has grown. The frame has simply got smaller.

That is the short answer, and it is one of the rare cases in fact-checking where the scientific consensus is about as close to unanimous as it gets. Forensic pathologists, anthropologists, dermatologists and anatomists all say the same thing. But the myth is worth taking apart properly, because it is a beautiful example of how a real observation — dead bodies really do look different after a few days — can be attached to a completely wrong explanation, and then carried forward by novels, films, and pub quizzes for a hundred years.

## What hair and nails actually are

To understand why growth stops at death, it helps to start with what hair and nails are made of, and where the living part sits. The hair you can see, hold, and cut is not alive. It is a shaft of keratin, a tough protein, built from cells that have already died by the time they are pushed out of the follicle. The same is true of the nail plate, the hard, translucent structure we call the nail. It is keratin too, produced by a group of dividing cells called the nail matrix, which sits under the skin at the base of the nail.

The living machinery is hidden. For hair, it is the follicle, sunk into the skin, fed by tiny blood vessels. For nails, it is the matrix, tucked under the fold of skin at the base of the nail. Those living cells divide, and each new layer pushes the older material forward and outward: hair lengthens from the follicle, nails lengthen from the base toward the fingertip. Stop the supply lines to those cells — the oxygen, the glucose, the hormones, the raw materials carried by the blood — and the whole production line stops with them.

In a living person, that production line runs at a surprisingly steady pace. Scalp hair grows on average about one centimetre per month. Fingernails grow just over three millimetres per month; a study of healthy young American adults measured an average fingernail growth rate of 3.47 millimetres per month, against 1.62 millimetres per month for toenails. Those numbers vary with age, genetics, season, and health — younger people tend to grow hair and nails faster, and fingernails grow faster than toenails — but the order of magnitude is stable. A beard does not add a visible centimetre overnight, and a nail does not add a visible millimetre overnight either. That matters, because it sets a limit on what anyone could plausibly observe in the days after a death. Even if growth somehow continued, a corpse examined two days after death would show at most a fraction of a millimetre of new nail and well under a millimetre of new hair. The "growth" people report seeing is far larger than that, and it appears far too quickly. Something else must be going on.

## The moment the supply stops

Death, for the purposes of this claim, is the moment the circulation stops. When the heart stops beating, blood no longer reaches the follicles or the nail matrix. Without blood, there is no fresh oxygen and no fresh glucose. Without oxygen and glucose, cells cannot make adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, the energy currency that powers cell division. Cell division is not a passive process that coasts on momentum. It is expensive, organised work, and it halts when the energy runs out.

This is the same reason muscles stiffen after death in rigor mortis: ATP is needed to break the bonds between the actin and myosin filaments in muscle fibres, and when ATP is depleted, those bonds lock in place. The body after death is not a machine winding down gently over days. In the tissues that matter for growth, it is a machine whose power has been cut. The follicle and the matrix do not keep dividing for a while out of habit. They stop, because the conditions that make division possible have ceased to exist.

There is a nuance here that is worth stating honestly, because it is the kind of nuance that myths feed on. Researchers have found that some cellular and genetic activity can linger briefly in certain tissues after death, and forensic scientists study those traces to help estimate time of death. But scattered, short-lived activity in dying cells is not the same thing as growth. Growth means new hair cells being produced in an organised sequence, pushing a shaft outward, and new nail cells being produced in an organised sequence, pushing a plate forward. That organised production requires a living, supplied, regulated tissue. A corpse does not have one. No study has ever measured genuine post-mortem lengthening of a hair shaft or a nail plate, and the reason is straightforward: there is no mechanism left by which it could happen.

## Why corpses look like they have grown a beard

If nothing grows, why have so many people, including people who handled bodies professionally, sworn that they have seen it? Because the observation itself is real. A body examined a day or two after death can genuinely look as though its beard stubble is longer and its nails more prominent. The mistake is in the explanation, and the explanation is skin.

The human body is roughly 60 percent water, and after death it begins to lose that water. The skin, which is soft tissue, dehydrates and retracts. Think of a grape turning into a raisin: the skin tightens and pulls back. On a corpse, the skin of the scalp pulls back from the base of each hair, and the skin around the nail beds pulls back from the edges of each nail. Hair shafts and nails do not move, but more of them becomes visible, because the tissue that used to cover their bases has retreated.

The effect is most convincing exactly where people look most closely. Beard stubble is the classic case. In life, a short beard is partly sunk into the skin of the face; the visible part is only the portion that clears the surface. When the facial skin shrinks back, a millimetre or two of previously buried shaft is exposed, and a close shave can suddenly look like a day or two of growth. Nails work the same way. We are used to seeing a nail framed by its surrounding skin, and when that frame pulls back, the nail looks longer even though the plate itself has not changed by a fraction of a millimetre. The illusion is strongest in the first day or two after death, while dehydration is advancing and before decomposition has gone far enough to undo it.

Forensic pathologist Lester Adelson addressed this directly in his book The Pathology of Homicide, published in 1974. He explained that the apparent growth of hair and nails after death is purely illusory, the result of dehydration and shrinkage of the skin exposing parts of the hair and nails that were previously covered. Forensic anthropologist William Maples put it even more bluntly. As he said, "It is a powerful, disturbing image, but it is pure moonshine. No such thing occurs." That quotation is worth keeping in mind, because it captures the whole story: the image is powerful, which is why the myth survives, and it is moonshine, which is why it should not.

The timeline of the illusion also fits the skin-shrinkage explanation and does not fit the growth explanation. Skin dehydration and retraction begin within the first few hours after death and are most noticeable within the first day or two. After that, decomposition takes over: microbial activity, gas formation, and skin slippage begin, typically within 24 to 72 hours depending on temperature, humidity, and other conditions. As soft tissue breaks down, follicles loosen and hair begins to shed rather than appear to grow. In other words, the corpse does not keep getting hairier. It first appears to, briefly, because the skin shrinks, and then it loses hair, because the tissue holding the hair falls apart. That sequence — apparent growth, then shedding — is exactly what the dehydration explanation predicts, and the opposite of what continued growth would predict.

## Where the myth came from

Nobody knows precisely where this story started, but its fingerprints are all over the literature of war and horror. Erich Maria Remarque's novel All Quiet on the Western Front, first published in 1929, contains a famous passage describing the dead with hair and nails that seem to keep growing, and the image lodged itself in the popular imagination. Decades later, Vincent Price's character in the 1959 horror film The Tingler repeated the claim on screen, giving it a new audience and a new lease of life. A myth that appears in a beloved novel and a horror film has two powerful advantages: it is memorable, and it feels like the kind of thing an author would only write if it were true. Neither advantage has anything to do with evidence.

There is also a quieter, more respectable source of the myth: the genuine difficulty of observing bodies. For most of history, ordinary people saw corpses rarely, briefly, and under emotional circumstances. A body laid out for viewing, a soldier recovered days after a battle, a relative glimpsed in a funeral parlour — these are not controlled observations. They are moments in which grief, dim light, and an unfamiliar face make small changes look large. If a dead man's beard looks longer than it did in life, the simplest story available to a grieving observer is that it grew. The real story — that the skin of his face has lost water and pulled back — requires knowledge of post-mortem physiology that most people, understandably, do not carry around. The myth filled a gap in understanding, and it filled it with a story that felt complete.

Embalming and funeral practice may have helped too. Bodies prepared for viewing are washed, and sometimes the skin is treated in ways that change its appearance. But the core mechanism needs no funeral parlour. Dehydration alone, in any body left unrefrigerated for a day or two, is enough to produce the effect. That is why the reports come from so many different settings — battlefields, hospitals, mortuaries, and exhumations — and why they agree on the direction of the change while disagreeing on its size. Skin shrinkage is a universal process. Growth after death is not a process at all.

## What actually happens to hair and nails after death

Once the illusion has passed, the real post-mortem story of hair and nails is one of loss, not growth. As decomposition advances, the follicles that anchor hair weaken, and hair begins to loosen and shed. The first shedding can begin within days, and significant loss follows over the following weeks as soft tissues break down. Nails, too, eventually loosen as the surrounding tissue decomposes, and they can detach entirely in later stages.

Hair itself, however, is remarkably durable. Because the shaft is made of keratin, a tough and chemically resistant protein, it resists decay far better than skin, muscle, or organs. That is why archaeologists recover hair from burials that are centuries or even thousands of years old, long after everything else has gone. It is also why the myth can feel plausible in a museum: a mummy with a full head of hair and long-looking nails seems to prove that hair and nails outlast the body. They do outlast the soft tissues, in the sense that they survive when softer parts decay. But surviving is not growing. A mummy's hair is not longer than it was in life. It is simply still there, while the skin that once framed it has shrunk and hardened around it, making it look more prominent than ever.

The same durability makes hair and nails useful to forensic science in life and after death. In living people, hair and nails grow steadily enough that they can record a timeline: a segment of hair corresponds roughly to a period of growth, and nail clippings can reflect exposure over months. Forensic toxicologists use that steadiness to look back at drug exposure, nutrition, and trace elements. The irony is that the very steadiness that makes hair and nails good biological recorders in life is what makes post-mortem growth impossible to miss if it existed. If nails grew after death at anything like their living rate of about three and a half millimetres a month, a body exhumed after a year would have nails several centimetres longer. Exhumations show nothing of the kind. They show nails that are, at most, the same length, often detached, and surrounded by shrunken, desiccated tissue.

## The hair-growth cycle, and why it cannot restart

A closer look at how hair grows in life makes the impossibility clearer. Hair does not grow continuously from every follicle at once. It grows in cycles. The anagen, or growth, phase lasts between two and eight years, during which the follicle actively produces new cells. Then comes the catagen, or transition, phase, lasting around two weeks, in which growth slows. Then comes the telogen, or resting, phase, lasting two to three months, in which there is no growth at all and the hair eventually sheds. Each follicle cycles through these phases roughly ten to thirty times in a lifetime, and at any given moment most scalp follicles are in the growth phase while a minority rest.

That cycle is regulated. Hormones, nutrition, age, and overall health all influence it, and the regulation itself depends on a living body: a bloodstream to carry hormones, a metabolism to supply energy, and a nervous and endocrine system to coordinate the whole affair. Death removes all of it at once. There is no residual cycle to wind down, because the cycle was never a property of the hair shaft. It was a property of the living follicle, and the follicle is dead. The same logic applies to nails, whose growth is steadier — the matrix divides continuously rather than cyclically — but equally dependent on a living, supplied tissue. A nail matrix without blood is not a slow nail factory. It is dead tissue.

This is also why the myth fails a simple plausibility check that has nothing to do with laboratories. If hair and nails grew after death, they would presumably grow in the same way they grow in life: slowly, steadily, and for as long as the tissue lasted. But the reported "growth" is always a small, one-off change noticed in the first days, and it never continues. Nobody reports a corpse with a beard that has grown to its waist, or nails that have curled into spirals, after months in the ground. The observation stops where skin shrinkage stops, because skin shrinkage is what caused it.

## Why this myth is so hard to kill

The persistence of the hair-and-nails myth is itself interesting, because it has been debunked in print for at least half a century, by forensic pathologists and anthropologists, and it still circulates as a "did you know" fact. Part of the reason is the vividness of the image. A corpse that keeps growing is a story that tells itself, and stories that tell themselves travel well. Part of the reason is that the debunking requires a small piece of physiology — skin retracts as it dehydrates — that is less vivid than the myth it replaces. And part of the reason is that most people never get to check. The claim is about something that happens behind closed doors, in mortuaries and graves, where ordinary curiosity cannot easily follow.

It also belongs to a family of body myths that feel true because they are built on a half-remembered fact. Readers who enjoy this kind of claim may recognise the pattern from our earlier fact-checks: <a href="/blog/shaving-makes-hair-grow-back-thicker-fact-check/">does shaving make hair grow back thicker</a>, <a href="/blog/humans-only-five-senses-myth-fact-check/">the idea that humans have only five senses</a>, and <a href="/blog/swallowed-chewing-gum-seven-years-myth-fact-check/">the claim that swallowed chewing gum stays in your stomach for seven years</a>. In each case, a real observation — shaved hair feels coarser, we have senses beyond the famous five, gum is hard to digest — is attached to a wrong conclusion, and the wrong conclusion is more memorable than the correction. The hair-and-nails myth is the same trick, played on the largest possible stage: the boundary between life and death.

There is one more reason the myth survives, and it is worth naming because it is not a failure of reasoning at all. Death is frightening, and a story in which something about us keeps going — even something as mundane as a beard — softens the finality of it, or at least makes it stranger and therefore more interesting to talk about. Myths about the dead often say more about the living than about the dead. This one says that we would rather imagine a body still faintly busy than accept that it has stopped completely. That is a human impulse, and it deserves sympathy. It just does not deserve to be mistaken for biology.

## The Verdict

Do hair and nails keep growing after death? No. The verdict is False, and it is False without much room for qualification. Hair and nails are made of dead keratin, produced by living cells in the follicle and the nail matrix that depend on blood supply, oxygen, glucose, and ATP. When circulation stops, that production stops. The longer beard and the more prominent nails that observers have reported for generations are real observations with a different cause: the skin dehydrates, shrinks, and pulls back, exposing more of the hair shaft and nail plate that were already there. Forensic pathologists have explained this since at least the 1970s, and no measurement has ever shown genuine post-mortem growth.

What happens next is the opposite of growth. As decomposition advances, follicles loosen, hair sheds, and nails eventually detach. The hair that survives in old burials survives because keratin is durable, not because it kept growing. The corpse does not need a barber. It needs, and gets, only the physics of drying skin.

So the next time this "fact" comes up in a quiz, a forwarded message, or a conversation about the strange things bodies do, you can answer it with confidence — and with the explanation that makes the observation make sense. The dead do not grow beards. Their skin shrinks, their hair stands a little prouder, and for a day or two, the living mistake the frame for the picture. That is strange enough, and it is true, which is more than the myth can say.
