If you write with your left hand, you have probably heard the warning at some point: left-handers die nine years younger than right-handers. It sounds like the kind of thing that could be true. The world is plainly built for right-handed people. Scissors, can openers, computer mice, spiral notebooks, power tools, car gear sticks — almost everything is designed for the other 90 percent. It feels plausible that a lifetime of wrestling with a right-handed world would shave years off a life.

The claim is not a piece of internet folklore with no source. It comes from a real study, published in one of the most prestigious medical journals in the world, by two respected psychologists. It was reported in newspapers around the globe. And for more than three decades, it has been repeated in books, articles, listicles, and dinner-party conversations as though it were settled science.

It is not settled science. It is, by the verdict of the researchers who have examined it most carefully, wrong. The nine-year gap was a statistical mirage — an artefact created by the way the original study was designed, in a century when left-handed children were routinely forced to write with their right hand. When researchers finally ran the numbers properly, tracking living people forward in time instead of counting the dead backwards, the gap vanished. Left-handers do not die younger. They never did.

## The Claim That Scared a Generation of Lefties

The claim, in its most common form, is simple: left-handed people live, on average, nine years less than right-handed people. Sometimes the number is softened to "several years." Sometimes it is sharpened into a warning about accidents — left-handers, the story goes, are up to five times more likely to die in accidents because they live in a world designed for right-handers.

The figure has a specific origin, and it is worth naming it, because the whole story turns on a single study. In April 1991, psychologists Stanley Coren of the University of British Columbia and Diane Halpern of California State University, San Bernardino, published a paper in the *New England Journal of Medicine* titled "Left-handedness: a marker for decreased survival fitness." Their conclusion was stark: left-handed people die, on average, nine years earlier than right-handed people.

The study was not obscure. It was published in the NEJM, the most widely read medical journal on the planet, and it was picked up by newspapers and television around the world. For parents of left-handed children, it was alarming. For left-handed adults, it was the kind of statistic that lodges in the back of the mind and never quite leaves. And because the authors were serious researchers and the journal was serious, the finding was treated as serious — for years, even after the criticism began.

The claim survives today in a softer, more durable form. Search for it now and you will find health blogs, trivia sites, and social media posts repeating the nine-year figure as though it were a medical fact. Some of them hedge. Some of them do not. Almost none of them mention that the study was challenged within weeks of publication, or that the challenge has since been settled decisively in favour of the critics.

## The 1991 Study: What Halpern and Coren Actually Did

To understand why the study went wrong, you have to understand what it actually did. Coren and Halpern did not follow a group of left-handed and right-handed people through their lives and record when each died. That would have been the direct way to answer the question, and it would have taken decades. Instead, they did something quicker: they looked at people who had already died.

They obtained records of 987 deaths in two counties in Southern California in 1989. Then they sent questionnaires to the families of the deceased, asking whether the dead person had been right-handed or left-handed. From the answers, they calculated the average age at death for each group. Right-handers, they found, had died at an average age of 75. Left-handers had died at an average age of 66. The difference was 8.97 years — the famous nine-year gap.

The authors offered two explanations. First, they argued that left-handers are more vulnerable to accidents in a world built for right-handers: power tools, machinery, and vehicles designed for the majority, used by people whose dominant hand is on the wrong side of the equipment. Coren had separately argued that left-handers faced up to five times the risk of fatal accidents. Second, they suggested that left-handedness might be linked to underlying physiological factors — differences in brain organisation that could carry health costs of their own.

Both explanations sounded plausible. Both were, as it turned out, beside the point. The problem was not the explanations. The problem was the design. Coren and Halpern never established how many left-handers and right-handers there were in those two counties to begin with, or how old they were. They counted the dead, but they had no denominator — no picture of the living population from which the dead had come. And that missing denominator is exactly where the mirage was hiding.

## The Flaw Nobody Noticed at First — and the Century That Created It

The flaw in the 1991 study was pointed out almost immediately, in letters to the editor of the NEJM and in the press. It is easiest to see with a thought experiment.

Imagine a town where, for cultural reasons, almost nobody admits to being left-handed among people born before 1920 — because in those days, left-handed children were forced to write with their right hand, rapped on the knuckles, or simply retrained until the left hand was abandoned. Now imagine that among people born after 1960, left-handedness is freely reported, because nobody is forced to switch any more. In such a town, the old people who die will almost all be recorded as right-handed, and the young people who die will include a much larger share of left-handers. If you then take a sample of everyone who died in a single year and compare the average age of death of the "left-handers" with that of the "right-handers," the left-handers will look younger — not because they died younger, but because the only left-handers you can identify are the young ones. The old left-handers are hiding in your data, mislabelled as right-handers.

That is precisely what happened in the twentieth century. Reported left-handedness rose steadily through the century as the pressure to switch faded. In the early 1900s, only a small percentage of the population was recorded as left-handed. By the end of the century, the figure had roughly tripled, settling at around 10 to 11 percent. A large survey of more than 1.1 million readers of *National Geographic* found that non-right-handedness was most common among the young — about 14 percent of young men and 12 percent of young women — and least common among the old. The trend was not a biological mystery. It was a cultural one: the older the person, the more likely they had grown up in an era when being left-handed was treated as something to be corrected.

This matters because of how Coren and Halpern collected their data. They asked bereaved relatives whether the deceased had been left-handed or right-handed. For a person who died at 85 in 1989 — someone born around 1904, who had been forced to write with their right hand at school — the family would very likely answer "right-handed," even if that person had been naturally left-handed. For a person who died at 30 in the same year, the family would answer accurately. The result is a systematic distortion: the left-handed dead in the sample are disproportionately young, and the right-handed dead are disproportionately old. The nine-year gap is, to a large extent, a measurement of that distortion.

The criticism came fast and from heavyweight quarters. Within weeks of publication, the *New Scientist* reported that scientists who had reviewed the research considered it fatally flawed. Tricia Hartge, an epidemiologist at the National Institutes of Health, said the results "mean absolutely nothing" without knowing how many left-handers and right-handers there were in the population to start with. The American Academy of Actuaries issued a statement calling Coren and Halpern's methods "faulty." Hartge's point was blunt and correct: the average left-handed person in the United States was significantly younger than the average right-handed person — and that fact alone, with nothing to do with lifespan, could produce exactly the pattern Coren and Halpern had found.

Chris McManus, a professor of psychology at University College London and the author of a major book on handedness, put the implausibility of the claim in vivid terms. A nine-year drop in life expectancy, he said, would be comparable to "smoking 120 cigarettes daily plus doing a number of other dangerous things simultaneously." If left-handedness really were that dangerous, it would be one of the largest risk factors in all of medicine — larger than smoking, larger than obesity, larger than almost anything epidemiologists study. And yet nobody had noticed it before 1991, and no hospital, insurer, or public health agency had ever detected it in their own data. That, on its own, should have been a warning.

## What Better Studies Actually Found

The decisive test of any claim about lifespan is a prospective study: take a group of living people, record their handedness, and follow them forward in time to see who dies when. That is the design Coren and Halpern did not use — and when other researchers used it, the nine-year gap disappeared.

In 1993, psychologist Lauren Harris published a detailed commentary in *Psychological Bulletin* examining Coren and Halpern's two studies and the evidence they had invoked. Her conclusion was unequivocal: "the case for the 'decreased survival fitness' hypothesis cannot be sustained." The cross-sectional pattern — fewer left-handers among older age groups — was real, she agreed, but it reflected the historical rise in reported left-handedness, not a shorter lifespan. New studies that tested longevity more directly, she found, did not support the claim.

Studies of athletes, whose handedness and dates of birth and death are a matter of public record, found no significant difference in lifespan between left-handed and right-handed players. A study of 118 pairs of same-sex twins with opposite handedness found that slightly more right-handed twins died before their non-right-handed twin — the opposite of what the 1991 claim would predict. These were not small, obscure findings. They were direct tests of the hypothesis, using data that could not be distorted by the reporting bias that had poisoned the original study.

The most elegant demonstration came much later, in 2023, when researchers published a modelling study in *Archives of Public Health* that asked a simple question: if left-handers and right-handers actually died at exactly the same ages, would the 1991 study's method still produce a nine-year gap? The answer was yes. Using the actual United States mortality data for 1989 — the same year Coren and Halpern's study was based on — the researchers calculated all 2,019,512 deaths that year by birth year, sex, and handedness. They assumed, as their starting point, that there was no difference in mortality between left-handers and right-handers at all. Then they applied the historical pattern of reported left-handedness — the fact that older generations reported far less left-handedness than younger ones — and asked what gap the 1991 method would find.

The model produced a gap of 9.3 years, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 4.3 to 14.4 years. That is statistically indistinguishable from the 8.97 years Halpern and Coren reported. In other words, the changing rate of left-handedness reporting over the twentieth century, entirely on its own, explains the original finding — even if handedness has no effect on lifespan whatsoever. When the researchers ran the same model but assumed that reported left-handedness had stayed constant over time, the survival advantage for right-handers collapsed to 0.02 years — about a week. When they compared people within the same birth cohort, the mean difference was 0.43 years, driven by differences in handedness between men and women rather than by any lifespan effect.

That is as close to a definitive answer as science usually gets. The nine-year gap was not a discovery about left-handed people. It was a discovery about the twentieth century — about a world that forced left-handed children to write with their right hand, and then forgot that it had done so.

## The Accident Argument: A Real Friction, Not a Death Sentence

One part of the original story deserves a fair hearing, because it is not entirely invented. Left-handers really do live in a world designed for right-handers, and that really does cause friction — and sometimes injuries.

A 1994 study in the *New England Journal of Medicine*'s clinical journal compared 267 children aged 6 to 18 who had suffered unintentional injuries with 496 uninjured children treated in the same emergency department. Significantly more of the injured children were left-handed — 18.1 percent, against 10.5 percent of the uninjured group — and the parents of left-handed children were more likely to describe their children as clumsier than average. Other research has found higher rates of minor accidents among left-handers in driving, sports, work, and tool use, and one analysis reported that the risk of finger amputation from electric saws was nearly five times higher for left-handed users. Power tools, in particular, are overwhelmingly designed for right-handed operation, and a left-handed worker using a right-handed angle grinder or circular saw is working against the design of the machine.

But minor injuries and fatal accidents are very different things, and the evidence for a large fatal-accident gap has not held up. A large prospective cohort study of Japanese workers, published in 2023, followed 9,451 workers and compared occupational accident rates by handedness. The incidence rate ratio for occupational accidents among left-handed workers, compared with right-handed workers, was 1.13 — and the 95 percent confidence interval, 0.63 to 2.04, comfortably included 1.0, meaning no statistically significant difference at all. Interestingly, it was mixed-handed workers, not left-handed workers, who showed a significantly elevated rate, at 1.84. The study also reproduced the familiar age pattern: left-handedness was reported by 6.0 percent of workers aged 39 and under, but only 3.7 percent of those aged 40 and over — a reminder that the reporting gap is alive and well even in recent data.

An earlier analysis, by Salive and colleagues in 1993, reported that left-handers were more than twice as likely to die as a result of injury than right-handers. That finding is real, but it is a long way from the claim that left-handers die nine years younger. Even if left-handers faced double the risk of injury death, injury deaths are a small fraction of all deaths, and doubling a small risk does not move average life expectancy by nine years. The arithmetic of the original claim never added up, which is exactly what McManus was pointing out with his 120-cigarettes comparison. A risk factor that cut nine years off life expectancy would be visible everywhere — in insurance tables, in hospital data, in population statistics. It is visible nowhere except in the one study whose design guaranteed it would appear.

## Why the Myth Survives

If the claim was debunked in the early 1990s and decisively refuted in 2023, why does it keep coming back? Part of the answer is that the original study was published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, and corrections rarely travel as far as the original headline. The nine-year figure was news. The refutation was a methodological argument, and methodological arguments do not make the front page.

Part of the answer is that the claim feels true. Anyone who has watched a left-handed person struggle with a right-handed can opener, smudge a line of fresh ink across a page, or sit at a lecture-hall desk built for the other hand can believe that the world is harder on left-handers. And it is harder, in small ways, every day. The mistake is to leap from "the world is inconvenient for left-handers" to "left-handers die nine years younger." The first is obvious to anyone who looks. The second is a claim about mortality, and mortality claims need mortality data — the kind that follows living people forward, not the kind that counts the dead backwards.

There is also a deeper reason the myth endures: it flatters a certain kind of storytelling. The left-hander as a tragic figure, battling a hostile right-handed world, is a compelling narrative. It has a villain (the right-handed world), a victim (the left-hander), and a shocking twist (nine years of life, stolen). The true story — a statistical artefact caused by changing cultural norms, discovered by careful modelling — is more interesting to a statistician than to a storyteller. But it is the true story, and it is the one the evidence supports.

The claim also benefits from a kernel of real science that gets misread. It is true that left-handedness is associated with some differences in brain organisation, and with slightly higher rates of certain conditions. Left-handers are somewhat overrepresented among people with dyslexia and ADHD, and some studies have found modest associations with other neurodevelopmental traits. None of this translates into a lifespan gap. The largest and best-designed studies of handedness and mortality — the prospective cohorts, the athlete records, the twin studies, and the 2023 simulation — all point the same way: handedness does not determine how long you live.

## Timeline: How a Statistical Mirage Became a "Fact"

- **Early 1900s:** Left-handed children are routinely forced to write with their right hand in schools across the United States and Europe. Reported left-handedness in the population is low.
- **Mid-1900s:** The pressure to switch gradually fades. Reported left-handedness rises steadily through the century, roughly tripling by its end.
- **1989:** Coren and Halpern collect records of 987 deaths in two Southern California counties and survey the families about the deceased's handedness.
- **April 1991:** Their paper is published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, claiming left-handers die nine years younger. Newspapers around the world report it.
- **April 1991:** Within weeks, the *New Scientist* reports that the study's methods have been called "faulty" by the American Academy of Actuaries, and an NIH epidemiologist says the results "mean absolutely nothing."
- **1993:** Lauren Harris publishes a detailed commentary in *Psychological Bulletin* concluding that the case for left-handers' "decreased survival fitness" cannot be sustained.
- **1990s:** Studies of athletes and of opposite-handedness twins find no lifespan difference by handedness.
- **2023:** A modelling study in *Archives of Public Health* shows that the changing rate of left-handedness reporting alone reproduces the nine-year gap — even when handedness has no effect on lifespan at all. A Japanese prospective cohort study the same year finds no significant excess of occupational accidents among left-handed workers.
- **Today:** About 10.6 percent of the world is left-handed, according to a meta-analysis of nearly 2.4 million people — and no credible population data show a life expectancy penalty for any of them.

## What the Claim Gets Right — and What It Gets Wrong

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Left-handers die nine years younger than right-handers | False. The nine-year gap is a statistical artefact of changing reporting rates, reproduced exactly by a 2023 simulation that assumed no mortality difference at all |
| Left-handers are up to five times more likely to die in accidents | Not supported. Large controlled datasets and a 2023 prospective cohort found no significant excess of occupational accidents among left-handers |
| The world is designed for right-handers | True. Tools, desks, and equipment are overwhelmingly right-handed, and left-handers do suffer more minor injuries |
| Fewer left-handers appear among older age groups | True — but it reflects the history of forced switching, not shorter lives. Older generations were retrained as children and recorded as right-handed |
| Left-handedness itself shortens life | No evidence. Prospective studies, athlete records, twin studies, and modelling all find no lifespan penalty |

## The Verdict

**False.** Left-handed people do not die nine years younger than right-handed people. They do not die younger at all, as far as any credible evidence can tell.

The nine-year figure came from a real study, published in a real journal, by real researchers — and that is exactly why it is worth understanding rather than merely dismissing. Coren and Halpern's 1991 paper was not a hoax. It was a study with a fatal design flaw, published in an era when the history of forced hand-switching had not yet been recognised as a confounder. The flaw was spotted within weeks. It was dismantled in detail in 1993. And in 2023, a modelling study using the entire United States mortality record for 1989 showed that the gap appears even when handedness has no effect on lifespan whatsoever — a gap of 9.3 years, conjured out of nothing but changing cultural norms.

What is true is smaller and less dramatic, and it is worth saying plainly. The world is built for right-handed people, and left-handers pay a daily tax in inconvenience and minor injuries. That is a design problem, not a death sentence, and it is one that better tools and a little awareness can fix. What is not true is that the tax is paid in years of life. Roughly one person in ten writes with their left hand — around 800 million people worldwide, among them Leonardo da Vinci, Barack Obama, Paul McCartney, and Oprah Winfrey — and the best available evidence says they can expect exactly as many years as anyone else.

The nine-year claim is a reminder of something larger than handedness: a single striking number, published in a prestigious journal and repeated for decades, can outlive the evidence against it by a generation. The correction exists. It has existed since 1991. It just never got the headline.
