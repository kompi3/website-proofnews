You have a cold. Your throat is sore, your nose is running, and someone — a parent, a grandparent, a well-meaning friend — tells you to skip the milk. Dairy makes mucus, they say. It will coat your throat, thicken the phlegm, and make everything worse. So you drink water instead, and you feel virtuous about it, as if you have done something clever for your recovery.

It is advice almost everyone has heard, and almost everyone has followed at least once. It is also wrong. Not mostly wrong, not wrong in a complicated technical sense, but wrong in the way that matters: scientists have tested it directly, more than once, including in a study where volunteers were deliberately infected with a cold virus and then told to drink milk, and the milk made no difference at all to how much mucus they produced. The belief has survived for roughly eight centuries anyway, passed from a medieval physician's treatise to a bestselling baby-care book to your kitchen table, because it feels true in your mouth even though it is false in your lungs.

That gap between feeling and fact is what makes this myth worth a full investigation. The milk-mucus claim is not a random piece of folklore. It is a case study in how a real sensation can be mistaken for a real effect, how a respected authority can freeze a mistake into medical advice, and how a belief can persist even after the experiments that should have killed it have been published in peer-reviewed journals. It also has a cost: children with asthma and cystic fibrosis are routinely denied milk — their main source of calcium and calories — because of a claim that has no evidence behind it.

## The Claim

The claim is simple and specific: drinking milk — and by extension eating dairy products — increases the production of mucus in the nose, throat, and lungs. The extra mucus, the story goes, worsens congestion, makes coughs heavier, and prolongs colds and respiratory infections. The practical advice follows immediately: avoid milk and dairy whenever you have a cold, a cough, asthma, or any respiratory illness, and keep children away from it too.

It is worth pausing on how confidently this is stated. It is not presented as a hunch or a folk remedy. It is presented as a fact of physiology, the kind of thing a doctor would tell you. Parents in respiratory clinics report being told it by relatives, by friends, and sometimes by health workers. And the behaviour it produces is real: in one American study of parents at a paediatric respiratory clinic, half of the parents said they stopped giving their child milk when the child was ill — including a fifth of that group who did not even believe milk caused mucus. The belief is so strong that it changes behaviour even in people who do not hold it.

There is a genuine sensation at the heart of the claim, and any honest fact-check has to start by admitting it. After drinking milk, many people feel a coating in the mouth and throat, a sense that their saliva has become thicker and harder to swallow. That feeling is real, repeatable, and measurable. The question is what it means. The claim says it means the body is making more mucus. The evidence says it means something else entirely — and the difference matters, because the feeling is in your mouth while the mucus the claim worries about is supposed to be in your lungs.

## Where the Myth Came From: An 800-Year-Old Warning

The milk-mucus idea is old enough to have a paper trail, and the trail leads to one of the most respected physicians of the Middle Ages. It is often written that the belief started with Moses Maimonides, the Jewish philosopher and court physician in Egypt, who died in 1204. In his Treatise on Asthma, written for an asthmatic relative of Saladin the Great, Maimonides warns against foods that generate phlegm: fatty food, scalding hot gas-generating foods such as black beans and peas, food made from coarse wheat flour, and heavy meats. He warns against cheese, especially if very old. His only mention of milk is that all kinds cause "a stuffing in the head" and that it is best to keep away from them.

That is a thin foundation for eight centuries of dietary advice, but it was enough. Traditional Chinese medical texts have also linked dairy consumption with a humidifying effect and thicker phlegm, although in reality most of those texts are positive about drinking milk. The idea passed from medical text to medical text, each generation citing the last, until it reached the book that carried it into nearly every home in the English-speaking world: Dr Spock's Baby and Child Care.

Benjamin Spock's book was first published in 1946 and had sold more than 50 million copies by the time of his death in 1998. It was, for most of the twentieth century, the default authority on raising children. And the 2011 edition still states that "dairy products may cause more mucus complications and more discomfort with upper respiratory infections." A claim that began as a medieval physician's caution about cheese had become, through the most trusted parenting book ever written, a standing instruction to millions of parents.

This history matters because it explains the myth's staying power. The belief does not survive on evidence. It survives on authority — first Maimonides, then traditional medicine, then Dr Spock — and on the fact that it feels true every time someone drinks a glass of milk with a cold. Each generation inherits it as received wisdom, and received wisdom is rarely checked against experiments. Until, that is, someone actually runs the experiments.

## What the Public Believes

Before looking at the experiments, it helps to measure the size of the belief. If only a handful of people thought milk caused mucus, the myth would be a curiosity. In fact, it is a majority view in some of the populations that matter most.

An Australian study asked 345 random shoppers what they thought about the health qualities of milk. Among whole-milk drinkers, 46 percent agreed that milk causes mucus. Among reduced-fat milk drinkers, the figure was 25 percent; among soy milk drinkers, 11 percent. The pattern is telling: the people most likely to believe the claim are the people who drink the most milk, and the people least likely to believe it are the people who have already switched away. Belief and behaviour feed each other. A fifth of whole-milk drinkers also believed milk caused asthma.

The numbers are higher still among parents of sick children. A study in New Zealand asked 100 parents of children with chronic asthma what they added to or avoided in their child's diet. Of those who were avoiding something solely because of the asthma, 58 percent avoided dairy products — and just 14 percent said they were doing so on medical advice. The rest were acting on the belief itself.

The most striking figure comes from a United States study of 330 parents in a paediatric respiratory clinic. Fifty-nine percent believed that drinking milk increased mucus production, 22 percent thought it did not, and 20 percent were uncertain. There was no difference according to the child's diagnosis — asthma, allergies, or cystic fibrosis made no difference to the belief. And when their child was ill, half of the parents avoided giving the child milk, despite a fifth of that group not believing milk caused mucus. The advice had become a reflex, detached from the belief that supposedly justified it.

These surveys were published in the peer-reviewed literature and summarised in a 2019 review in the journal Archives of Disease in Childhood by Dr Ian Balfour-Lynn of the Royal Brompton Hospital in London, a specialist in children's respiratory disease. His department, he wrote, had "repeatedly been told by parents that drinking milk increases mucus production from the lungs, and so they stop their child having milk." The review set out to answer a simple question: is there any evidence for it?

## The Evidence: Three Studies That Tested It Directly

The first attempt to get real evidence was published in 1948 in California. The author asked 200 patients a direct question: does milk make mucus for you? Twenty-five percent said yes. That is the kind of result that keeps a myth alive — a quarter of patients reporting exactly what the folklore predicts. But the author did not stop there. He divided 647 patients according to their daily milk consumption — zero to five glasses a week, six to nine, or ten or more — and found no difference between the groups in whether they complained of mucus in the throat. Nose and throat examinations were carried out on 157 of the subjects, and there was no excess mucus noted in those who drank milk versus those who did not. A quarter of people believed it, but the people who drank the most milk had no more mucus than the people who drank none.

The most direct test came in 1990, and it was designed to settle the question once and for all. An Australian group recruited 60 adult volunteers and deliberately infected them with rhinovirus-2, one of the viruses that causes the common cold. For ten days, the volunteers kept detailed records of their symptoms and of how much milk and dairy they consumed, and they weighed the nasal mucus they blew into tissues. Almost half of them believed dairy products were bad for colds, and 80 percent of those said it was because milk produced more mucus or phlegm. Across the study, milk consumption ranged from zero to eleven glasses a day. The result: no correlation between milk or dairy intake and the symptoms of upper or lower respiratory tract congestion, and no correlation with the weight of nasal secretions produced. People fighting an active cold did not make more mucus when they drank more milk. The researchers did find a non-significant increase in the looseness of cough with higher dairy intake — a hint, not a finding — and the study was partly sponsored by the Australian Dairy Research Council, a fact the researchers disclosed and that later reviewers have noted without finding fault in the methods.

The third study attacked the question from a different angle: not whether milk makes mucus, but whether the feeling people report is specific to milk at all. The same Australian group studied 169 adults, of whom 41 percent believed milk produced mucus. The volunteers were given either a flavoured ultra-heat-treated cow's milk drink or a soy milk drink flavoured identically with cocoa and peppermint, so that the two could not be told apart. Immediately after drinking 300 millilitres, three sensations were significantly increased: a coating or lining over the mouth, throat, or tongue (in 39 percent of participants), a need to swallow a lot (31 percent), and saliva that felt thicker and harder to swallow than before (42 percent). But there was no difference between those who had the cow's milk and those who had the soy drink. The mucus-like feeling was not specific to cow's milk at all — it was a property of drinking a flavoured, creamy liquid. Prior belief in the milk-mucus theory did not affect the outcomes, and there was no impact four hours or 24 hours later. The sensation was real, immediate, and gone within hours, and it had nothing to do with milk as such.

These three studies are the core of the evidence, and they point the same way. Milk does not increase the amount of mucus the body produces. What it does is create a temporary sensation in the mouth and throat that feels like extra mucus. The distinction is the whole story.

## The Studies at a Glance

The evidence is easier to judge when the key studies are set side by side. Each one asked the question differently, and each one reached the same answer.

| Study | Year | Who was studied | What they did | What they found |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| California clinic study | 1948 | 647 patients | Compared throat-mucus complaints across three levels of weekly milk intake; examined nose and throat of 157 patients | No difference in mucus complaints or visible mucus between heavy and light milk drinkers |
| Pinnock et al., *American Review of Respiratory Disease* | 1990 | 60 adults deliberately infected with rhinovirus-2 | Tracked milk intake and weighed nasal mucus for 10 days during an active cold | No correlation between milk intake and mucus weight, cough, or congestion |
| Wüthrich et al., *Journal of the American College of Nutrition* (double-blind) | 2005 | 169 adults, 41% of whom believed milk caused mucus | Gave identical-tasting cow's milk or soy drinks (300 mL) and measured sensations | Same coating and thickness sensations from soy as from milk; no effect at 4 or 24 hours |
| Balfour-Lynn review, *Archives of Disease in Childhood* | 2019 | Review of all published evidence | Weighed every study on milk, mucus, and asthma | "No evidence (and indeed evidence to the contrary) that milk leads to excessive mucus secretion" |

One detail in the table deserves emphasis: the 1990 study is the only one that measured actual mucus during an actual cold, and it found nothing. The 2005 study is the only double-blind test, and it showed the sensation is not milk-specific. The 2019 review, written by a specialist who hears the claim from parents every week, concluded that the myth "needs to be rebutted firmly by healthcare workers."

## Why Milk Feels Like Mucus When It Isn't

If milk does not increase mucus, why does it feel so much as though it does? The answer is in the physics of what milk is. Milk is an emulsion — a suspension of tiny droplets of fat in water. When you drink it, the emulsion mixes with your saliva, and saliva contains a high-molecular-weight mucin called MUC5B. Mucins are the molecules responsible for the viscoelastic, slightly sticky properties of saliva. When salivary mucins meet a fat emulsion, they quickly cause the droplets to clump together — a process called flocculation — forming aggregates that increase the viscosity and the volume of the mixture.

That is what you feel. The aggregates of milk emulsion linger in the mouth and throat after swallowing, coating the surfaces and changing the after-feel of every swallow. It is not mucus from your airways. It is milk and saliva, temporarily thickened by their own chemistry, sitting exactly where you can feel it. The size of the lipid droplet aggregates and the speed at which they form vary between individuals, which may explain why only some people notice the sensation strongly. It also explains why the soy drink in the 2005 study produced the same effect: any creamy emulsion will do it.

A second mechanism has been proposed, involving calcium. Increased extracellular calcium on the surface of the respiratory mucosa inhibits the hydration of mucin and increases the viscosity of mucus — an effect documented in cystic fibrosis. But as Balfour-Lynn's review notes, it is unlikely that drinking milk would lead to an immediate increase of calcium lining the respiratory tract. The emulsion explanation fits the timing of the sensation — immediate, and gone within hours — far better.

This is also why the claim is so hard to kill by argument alone. Telling someone the sensation is not mucus does not make the sensation go away. The coating is real; only its interpretation is wrong. And a belief that is confirmed by your own body every time you drink a glass of milk does not need a textbook to survive.

## The Beta-Casomorphin Theory: A Real Finding That Proves Nothing Yet

There is one published hypothesis that seems, at first glance, to give the myth a scientific foundation — and it is worth examining, because it shows how a genuine laboratory finding can be stretched into a claim it does not support.

When cow's milk protein is digested, one of the breakdown products is a small peptide called beta-casomorphin-7, or BCM-7. In a laboratory study, researchers exposed goblet cells — the mucus-producing cells — from rats and from human intestines, grown in culture dishes, to BCM-7. The peptide stimulated the cells to ramp up production and secretion of certain mucins. In the rat cells, mucin gene expression more than doubled at peak stimulation; in the human cells, a similar increase was observed. The effect was blocked by an opioid receptor antagonist, meaning BCM-7 acts through the same type of receptor that opioid drugs target. The specific mucin involved was MUC5AC, one of the main mucins of the airways as well as the gut.

It sounds like a smoking gun. It is not. The cells in the study were intestinal goblet cells, not airway cells. The mucus they produced was in the colon, not the lungs. For BCM-7 to increase mucus in the respiratory tract, the peptide would have to cross from the gut into the bloodstream in meaningful quantities, which would require increased intestinal permeability during an inflammatory state. As the 2019 review puts it, there is no reason a viral cold should lead to increased intestinal permeability, so the theory "seems unlikely" — although it may be conceivable in people with cystic fibrosis, where gut inflammation is part of the disease. A laboratory effect in a dish is not evidence of an effect in a person, and no study has ever shown BCM-7 increasing respiratory mucus in a living human. The hypothesis remains unproven, and it is not a reason to avoid milk during a cold.

## Milk and Asthma: The Fear Behind the Fear

The claim is most consequential where it meets asthma. Many asthmatics believe drinking milk worsens their asthma, and dairy is often the first food eliminated from an asthmatic child's diet. The New Zealand study found that among parents avoiding foods solely because of a child's asthma, 58 percent avoided dairy — and only 14 percent were following medical advice.

The evidence here is as clear as it is for mucus. Double-blind milk challenges — in which asthmatic adults were given milk or a placebo without knowing which — did not cause bronchoconstriction or respiratory symptoms in two studies of non-milk-allergic asthmatic adults, including the half of the subjects who believed that milk worsened their asthma. A small study of 11 asthmatic and 10 non-asthmatic adults compared the effect of drinking whole milk, skimmed milk, or water on lung function. There was no change in airflow parameters. The researchers did note that pulmonary diffusing capacity — a measure of gas exchange — fell by a mean of 21 percent over three hours in the asthmatics who drank full-fat milk, and speculated that milk lipids might affect gas exchange. But a later study showed that ingesting a gram of fat per kilogram of body weight, in the form of ice cream, had no effect on resting or exercise diffusing capacity, which undermines that speculation. A 2010 review in Clinical and Experimental Allergy found no improvement in symptoms when dairy was eliminated from the diet of asthmatic children.

There is an important exception, and honesty requires stating it plainly: true milk allergy can cause respiratory symptoms, including nasal congestion and wheezing. But that is an allergic immune response, not mucus overproduction, and it affects a small minority. For the large majority of people with asthma, milk does not worsen their breathing. What worsens their nutrition is the avoidance.

That distinction is exactly the kind of nuance the milk-mucus myth erases. It takes a real but narrow fact — some people are allergic to milk — and inflates it into a universal rule. ## What the Myth Costs

A false belief about food is not harmless when it changes what children eat. Milk is the principal source of calcium for children and adults, and a good source of several vitamins. Adequate calcium intake is critical for the development of normal bone health and the prevention of future osteoporosis. One study of 50 children who avoided milk found they were shorter and had reduced bone mineral density compared with 200 milk-drinking children — and only 12 percent of the milk-avoiders were taking calcium supplements. Childhood fractures are more common in children who avoid milk than in the general population. The risk is particularly serious for children with respiratory disease who need frequent courses of oral corticosteroids, which themselves weaken bones.

Milk is also an important energy source. Dutch children aged one to four derive about one-fifth of their total energy intake from milk products. Omitting that calorie source is particularly harmful for young children with cystic fibrosis, who have increased energy requirements and already struggle to maintain weight. These are the very children whose parents are most likely to withhold milk — the 2019 review notes that the belief is strongest in families dealing with conditions associated with excess mucus, such as cystic fibrosis and primary ciliary dyskinesia.

The cost is not only nutritional. There is a quieter cost in anxiety: parents who believe they are harming their child every time they hand over a glass of milk, and children who learn to associate a normal food with illness. None of it is necessary. As the review's author wrote, the milk-mucus myth "needs to be rebutted firmly by healthcare workers" — not because the sensation is imaginary, but because the conclusion drawn from it is wrong, and the dietary restriction it produces is harmful.

## The Verdict

Does drinking milk cause mucus? No. The claim is **False**.

The evidence against it is direct, repeated, and specific. A 1948 study of 647 patients found no link between how much milk people drank and how much mucus they reported. A 1990 study that deliberately infected 60 volunteers with a cold virus and weighed their nasal mucus for ten days found no correlation between milk intake and mucus production, cough, or congestion. A 2005 double-blind study showed that a soy drink produced exactly the same throat-coating sensation as cow's milk — proving the feeling is not specific to milk at all. A 2019 review in Archives of Disease in Childhood, written by a specialist who hears this claim from parents every week, concluded there is no evidence that milk leads to excessive mucus secretion, "and indeed evidence to the contrary."

The claim survives because it has a genuine sensation behind it. Milk is a fat emulsion; when it mixes with the mucin in your saliva, the droplets clump together and coat your mouth and throat, and the result feels like thicker mucus. That feeling is real, immediate, and gone within hours. What it is not is extra mucus in your lungs. The beta-casomorphin hypothesis — the one laboratory finding that seems to support the claim — was demonstrated in intestinal cells in a dish, not in human airways, and remains unproven as a cause of respiratory mucus.

The myth began with a medieval physician's warning about cheese, was carried into modern homes by the bestselling parenting book of the twentieth century, and is now sustained by a sensation that everyone who drinks milk has felt. Eight centuries is a long run for a claim with no evidence. It is time to let it go — and to give the glass of milk back to the children who need it most.

*There is no hidden flood of mucus behind the glass. The coating you feel is milk and saliva doing chemistry in your mouth — not your lungs making more phlegm.*
