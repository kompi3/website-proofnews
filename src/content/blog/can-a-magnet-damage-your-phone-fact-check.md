It is one of the oldest warnings in consumer technology, and most of us absorbed it before we ever owned a phone worth worrying about: keep magnets away from your electronics. A fridge magnet on the television, a magnetic clasp on a bag, a phone mount in the car — each one, the warning implied, was a small disaster waiting to happen. Your photos would vanish. Your screen would warp. Your device would never quite work right again.

The warning was not invented out of thin air. It was true once, for the machines people actually owned when the warning took shape. The problem is that the machines changed, and the warning did not.

 A modern smartphone is built from fundamentally different parts than the television sets and computers that made magnets dangerous, and that single change in engineering turns most of the old fear into a myth — though not, as we will see, into a complete one. There are still a few corners of your phone where a magnet can cause trouble, and one category of device nearby where magnets genuinely deserve respect.

## Where the Warning Comes From

To understand why the warning existed, it helps to picture the living room of the 1980s and 1990s. The television in the corner was almost certainly a cathode-ray tube set, a heavy glass box in which an electron beam was steered across a phosphor-coated screen by magnetic fields. Bring a strong magnet near that screen and the beam bent off course: colors smeared, the picture warped, and in bad cases the shadow mask inside the tube became magnetized and the distortion stayed. Television makers knew this, which is why CRT sets were fitted with degaussing coils that fired a decaying alternating field every time the set switched on, scrubbing away stray magnetization.

Storage was magnetic too. Floppy disks recorded data as tiny magnetized regions on a spinning plastic disc, and early hard drives did the same on metal platters. A magnet strong enough, held close enough, could scramble those regions and take the data with them. The image became so familiar that it entered popular culture: in one famous television scene, a giant electromagnet mounted in the back of a truck is used to wipe the hard drives inside a police evidence room. The drama was exaggerated, but the underlying physics was real. If your data lived as magnetism, a bigger magnet could rewrite it.

That was the world the warning grew up in. And because warnings are sticky — parents pass them to children, offices print them on posters, and nobody wants to be the person who tested one and lost a term paper — the warning survived long after the hardware it described had been replaced.

## What Your Phone Actually Stores, and Why That Matters

Here is the decisive difference between your phone and the machines of the magnet-fearing era: a modern smartphone does not store anything magnetically. Photos, messages, apps, contacts, and videos live in NAND flash memory, a form of solid-state storage in which data is held as electrical charge trapped in tiny transistor gates. There are no spinning platters, no magnetized regions, no read-write arm hovering over a disc. A static magnetic field simply has nothing to grab onto.

That is why every major explainer that has tested the question reaches the same conclusion about storage.

 Consumer magnets, including the ones in car mounts and cases, pose no meaningful threat to a phone's data, and even far stronger fields do not change the picture: magnetic resonance imaging machines operate at field strengths vastly greater than any fridge magnet, and they do not corrupt the flash storage in a phone. The storage medium changed, and with it the risk evaporated.

The same logic covers the battery. A lithium-ion battery stores energy through electrochemical reactions — lithium ions shuttling between electrodes through an electrolyte — not through magnetism. It contains no ferromagnetic material doing the work, and a static magnetic field does not speed, slow, or degrade that chemistry. There is no credible evidence linking magnetic mounts or cases to faster battery wear, and the claim that a magnet will drain your battery has no mechanism behind it. The display is equally safe: LCD and OLED screens are driven by voltage, not by electron beams steered with magnets, so there is nothing for a magnet to distort.

In short, the three things people most worry about losing — their data, their battery, and their screen — are exactly the three things a magnet cannot touch in a modern phone. That is a strong result, and it is the reason the blanket version of the warning is wrong.

## The Parts a Magnet Can Actually Bother

Saying the warning is wrong, however, is not the same as saying magnets do nothing at all to a phone. A phone is full of small components that deliberately respond to magnetic fields, and a strong external magnet can confuse them. The effects are almost always temporary — remove the magnet and the function returns — but they are real, and they explain why some people swear their phone acted strangely near a magnet.

The most sensitive part is the digital compass. Your phone finds north with a magnetometer, a sensor that measures the Earth's geomagnetic field, which is extraordinarily weak. A nearby magnet easily overwhelms that faint signal, and navigation apps that rely on the compass can point in the wrong direction while the magnet is close. Engineers who tested this with an iPhone found that magnets held near the device threw the compass readings off, and in the worst case magnetized iron components inside the phone could make the compass hard to recalibrate afterward until the field was removed and the sensor settled. Annoying, certainly, but not damage in the sense of a broken device.

The camera is the second sensitive area. Modern phone cameras use optical image stabilization, which shifts the lens or sensor with tiny electromagnets to cancel hand shake, and closed-loop autofocus, which uses magnetic sensors to position the lens precisely. A powerful external magnet can interfere with those sensors, temporarily disabling stabilization or autofocus and producing blurry photos until the magnet is moved away. This is one reason phone makers warn against using certain magnetic accessories with some camera systems, and it is the most practically visible effect a strong magnet can have on an ordinary phone.

Speakers and microphones contain small magnets of their own, by design, to convert electrical signals into sound and back again. A strong magnet pressed directly over a speaker can distort the output while it is there, and a magnet placed over the wrong spot can interfere with wireless charging by sitting between the phone and the charging coil or by misaligning it. Some phones also use magnetic sensors to detect when a case is closed or an accessory is attached; a stray magnet can trigger those sensors and wake the screen or launch an accessory mode unexpectedly. None of this breaks the phone. All of it disappears when the magnet does.

This distinction — temporary interference versus permanent damage — is the key to the whole question.

## The Experiment That Settled the Hard-Drive Question

If the storage claim still sounds doubtful, consider the people who tried hardest to prove it. Engineers at the magnet retailer K&J Magnetics set out to destroy a hard drive with magnets, the older storage technology that should have been far more vulnerable than any phone. They placed large neodymium magnets on either side of a running hard drive until the magnets were bending internal parts enough to produce audible mechanical rubbing — and the files remained completely intact. They tried again with the drive powered down. When it was switched back on, the files were still unaffected.

The lesson generalizes. Hard drives already contain powerful neodymium magnets inside them, operating the arm that reads and writes the platters, and they are engineered to tolerate that internal field. An ordinary magnet stuck to the outside of a computer case does not come close to the field needed to corrupt a drive, and a phone's flash storage is immune even to fields that would trouble a hard drive. The experiment is a useful corrective to the movie version of the story: wiping data with a magnet is, in practice, a job for industrial equipment or a shredder, not a souvenir magnet.

It is also worth noting what else changed around the phone. The old fear assumed a world of magnetic media everywhere — floppy disks in desk drawers, cassette tapes, VHS cassettes, and credit cards with magnetic stripes. Those media really could be corrupted by magnets, and the warning made sense for them. Today the magnetic stripe on a bank card is the last common survivor of that world, and even cards are now far more resistant than the flimsy stripes of the past; keeping them a short distance from strong magnets remains sensible advice, but it is a card problem, not a phone problem.

## The One Warning That Is Real

There is one place where magnets and modern phones genuinely demand caution, and it has nothing to do with the phone itself. Implanted medical devices such as pacemakers and defibrillators can contain sensors that respond to magnets, and a phone held close to one can interfere with how the device behaves.

This became a public issue when Apple introduced MagSafe, the ring of magnets built into iPhones to align chargers and accessories. Apple updated its support guidance to say that iPhones and MagSafe accessories contain magnets and components that emit electromagnetic fields, and that these might interfere with medical devices. Its advice was specific: keep an iPhone and its MagSafe accessories more than 6 inches (15 cm) away from an implanted medical device, or more than 12 inches (30 cm) away while wirelessly charging, and consult a physician and the device manufacturer for guidance specific to the implant.

The concern was not purely theoretical. Researchers reported that an iPhone 12 placed near a defibrillator could suspend the device's therapies while it remained close, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration later tested the issue itself, concluding that the risk to patients was low and that it was not aware of adverse events linked to the issue — while still advising patients to keep consumer electronics about six inches from implanted devices and not to carry them in a pocket over the implant. The practical message is balanced rather than alarming: the phone is not dangerous to its owner in the ordinary sense, but someone with an implanted cardiac device should treat a magnet-bearing phone the way they would treat any magnet — at arm's length, not pressed against the chest.

This is also the clearest proof that the magnet question is not a single yes-or-no issue. The same magnetic field that is harmless to a phone's storage can matter enormously to a different device a few inches away. Context, as always, is everything.

## What About Magnetic Mounts, Cases, and MagSafe?

Given all of this, the accessories most people actually worry about are the easiest to clear. Magnetic car mounts use neodymium magnets paired with a thin metal plate, typically generating fields far below the levels associated with electronic damage, and phones are built with shielding that deflects stray fields away from sensitive parts. Millions of drivers use these mounts daily without data loss, battery damage, or screen problems, and manufacturers would have heard about it loudly if the mounts were quietly killing phones.

MagSafe goes further: Apple builds magnets into the phone itself, deliberately, and designs the entire accessory ecosystem around them. If everyday magnets damaged modern phones, the company shipping a magnet ring inside every iPhone would be the first to know. The same applies to magnetic cases, folio covers with magnetic clasps, and magnetic wallets that snap to the back of a phone. These products work because the phone was designed to tolerate them.

There are a few sensible precautions worth keeping, and they are about function rather than fear.

## What About the Rest of Your Tech?

The same reasoning that clears the phone also clears most of the other gadgets in a modern home. Flat-screen televisions and monitors use LCD or LED panels with no electron beam to deflect, so a magnet beside them does nothing. Laptops with solid-state drives are as immune as phones; laptops that still use spinning hard drives are harder to hurt than the myth suggests, for the reasons the K&J experiment demonstrated. USB flash drives and memory cards are solid-state too.

The exceptions are the older or more specialized devices that still rely on magnetism. A vintage CRT television or monitor can still be distorted by a strong magnet, though even there the built-in degaussing coil usually repairs the picture when the set is switched on. Old floppy disks, cassette tapes, and VHS tapes remain genuinely vulnerable, as do magnetic-stripe hotel key cards and older bank cards. And anything with a mechanical compass — the analog kind, not the sensor in your phone — will swing toward a nearby magnet, which is exactly what a compass is supposed to do.

For a sense of scale, it helps to remember how quickly magnetic fields weaken with distance. Double the distance from a magnet and its field strength falls to roughly a quarter, which is why a magnet stuck to the fridge does nothing to the phone charging on the counter across the room, and why the warnings that matter are always about close, sustained contact rather than mere presence in the same house.

## The Verdict

So, can a magnet damage your phone? The claim as most people hear it — that a magnet will erase your data, ruin your battery, or break your screen — is <strong>Misleading</strong>. The fear is a leftover from an era when televisions steered pictures with magnets and computers stored data on magnetized discs. A modern smartphone stores its data as electrical charge, powers itself with chemistry rather than magnetism, and lights its screen with voltage. Everyday magnets, magnetic mounts, magnetic cases, and MagSafe accessories do not damage it, and there is no evidence that they shorten its life.

What a strong magnet can do is narrower and more temporary: confuse the digital compass, interfere with camera stabilization and autofocus, distort a speaker while it sits on top of it, or block wireless charging if placed badly. Those effects end when the magnet is removed. The one hard exception sits outside the phone entirely: if you or someone near you uses an implanted pacemaker or defibrillator, keep magnet-bearing phones and accessories at least six inches away, and twice that while wirelessly charging, exactly as Apple and the FDA advise.

The old warning deserves a retirement, not a reprieve. Keep magnets away from your floppy disks, if you still own any, and away from implanted medical devices. Your phone, though, can sit next to the fridge magnet without a second thought — it was built for a world the warning never caught up with.
