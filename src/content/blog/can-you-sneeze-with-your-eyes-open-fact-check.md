Everybody has heard the warning, usually delivered on a playground with total confidence: if you sneeze with your eyes open, your eyeballs will pop out of your head. Some versions add a flourish — they will roll across the floor like marbles, or dangle from their sockets like a cartoon. It is one of the most durable pieces of playground science in circulation, repeated by parents, teachers, and older siblings for generations, and it has survived into adulthood largely unchallenged, because almost nobody has ever actually seen a person sneeze with their eyes open. The reflex that slams our eyelids shut a fraction of a second before the sneeze arrives feels so automatic, so obviously built-in, that it is easy to assume it must be there for a dramatic reason — that the body is protecting the eyes from a violent internal explosion.

The truth is both less dramatic and more interesting. You can sneeze with your eyes open. People have done it on television, in front of high-speed cameras, with their eyelids held open by their own fingers, and their eyes stayed exactly where they belonged. Your eyeballs are held in place by six muscles each, cushioned by fat, and sealed into sockets that are not even connected to the air passages a sneeze uses. The reason your eyes close is not to stop them from flying out. It is a reflex — a piece of neural wiring that fires as part of the sneeze itself — and scientists still debate exactly why evolution bundled the two actions together. The eyeball-popping story, meanwhile, traces back to a single newspaper anecdote from 1882 that was probably never true in the first place.

## The Short Answer

Yes, you can sneeze with your eyes open, and no, your eyeballs will not pop out if you do. That is the verdict of allergists, optometrists, and anatomists who have been asked the question for years, and it is also the verdict of the most famous experimental test the claim has ever received. David Huston, an allergist and associate dean at the Texas A&M College of Medicine, has described sneezing with the eyes open as "absolutely possible," explaining that closing the eyes is an autonomic reflex rather than a hard-wired requirement of the sneeze. Bert Moritz, an optometrist with the Mayo Clinic Health System, has pointed out that six extraocular muscles firmly hold each eye in its socket, making it almost impossible for a sneeze to dislodge one, and that the pressure of a sneeze does not even increase inside the eyes. Healthline's medical review reaches the same conclusion: the eye-closing is an autonomic reflex, and overriding it takes a conscious effort, but it can be done.

None of this means the reflex is meaningless. Your body closes your eyes for reasons that probably matter — most likely to shield the delicate surface of the eye from the spray of irritants, mucus, and microbes that a sneeze throws into the air. But protection against flying germs is a very different thing from protection against flying eyeballs, and the distinction matters, because the myth has scared people for more than a century. The claim that you cannot sneeze with your eyes open is false, and the claim that trying will pop your eyes out is false too. Both halves of the playground warning fail, and they fail for reasons that reveal how elegantly — and how strangely — the human nervous system is wired.

## Anatomy of a Sneeze: What Actually Happens in Half a Second

To understand why the eyes close, it helps to understand what a sneeze is. Medically, a sneeze is called a sternutation, and it is one of the most violent and most coordinated reflexes the human body performs. It begins when sensory nerve endings in the lining of the nose detect something that does not belong there: dust, pollen, pet dander, pepper, smoke, a virus, or simply an excess of mucus during a cold. People try all sorts of home remedies to clear a blocked nose, some of them unwise — as our fact-check of <a href="/blog/does-putting-garlic-up-your-nose-clear-congestion/">Does Putting Garlic Up Your Nose Clear Congestion?</a> explains — but the sneeze itself is the body's own clearing mechanism, and it needs no help. Those nerve endings belong to the trigeminal nerve, cranial nerve V, the large nerve that carries sensation from the face. The signal races along the trigeminal nerve to the brainstem, specifically to the medulla oblongata, where a cluster of neurons sometimes called the sneeze center takes over.

What follows is a sequence so fast and so precisely choreographed that it is easy to miss how many systems are involved. First comes a deep inhalation, drawing a large volume of air into the lungs. Then the vocal cords close tightly — the glottis shuts — while the chest and abdominal muscles contract, building pressure in the chest like a bellows being squeezed with the nozzle blocked. The soft palate rises to seal off the back of the nose from the throat in some accounts, and the throat closes, forcing the air toward the nasal passages. Then the glottis suddenly opens, and the trapped air erupts outward through the nose and mouth in a single explosive exhalation. The air is commonly cited as travelling at around 100 miles per hour, and some popular sources put the figure as high as 200 miles per hour; either way, it is fast enough to carry droplets of mucus and the irritants that triggered the whole event well clear of the airways.

While all of this is happening, a whole set of muscles that have nothing to do with breathing also contract. The muscles of the face tense. The muscles of the esophagus flex. The muscles around the eyes — above all the orbicularis oculi, the ring-shaped muscle that closes the eyelid — squeeze shut an instant before the air is released. Some sneezes are forceful enough to make the eyes water afterward. The eyelid closure is not a separate decision your brain makes; it is part of the same burst of neural activity. The sneeze center in the medulla sends signals not only to the diaphragm, the intercostal muscles, and the abdominal wall, but also, through the facial nerve, cranial nerve VII, to the muscles that close the eyes. The blink is bundled into the sneeze the way the recoil is bundled into firing a gun: it is part of the mechanism, not an afterthought.

That bundling is the key to the whole myth. Because the eyes always close, people assumed they must close for a reason directly connected to the danger of the sneeze — and the most dramatic reason anyone could imagine was that the eyes needed holding in. But a reflex can be automatic without being load-bearing. You blink when something flies toward your face, and nobody supposes the blink is what keeps your eyes from falling out. The sneeze blink is, in the words of one Mayo Clinic explanation, similar to a deep tendon reflex, the knee-jerk tap a doctor tests with a small hammer: a hard-wired response that fires without conscious thought, and that does not imply the thing it protects is in any danger of escaping.

## Why Your Eyes Snap Shut

If the eyes do not need to be held in, why does the body bother closing them at all? The honest answer is that science does not know for certain. Healthline is blunt about this: there is no definitive clinical data explaining why we close our eyes when we sneeze. Several theories compete, and they are not mutually exclusive. The leading explanation is protection — not from the eyes popping out, but from the contents of the sneeze itself.

A sneeze is, after all, an expulsion. Its entire purpose is to throw irritants, pathogens, and mucus out of the nasal passages at high speed. The eyes are delicate, exposed mucous membranes sitting inches from the launch site, and many of the same substances the nose is trying to eject could irritate them. Closing the eyelids puts a physical barrier between the eyes and the spray. David Huston of Texas A&M makes exactly this point: by automatically shutting the eyelids when a sneeze occurs, the body can potentially prevent more irritants from entering and aggravating the eyes. The same logic explains why the reflex is so universal. Even infants, who have had no time to learn the behaviour by watching others, close their eyes when they sneeze. The pattern is built into the wiring, not copied from culture.

A second explanation comes from the plumbing that connects the eye to the nose. Follow the lower eyelid toward the nose and you will find a tiny black dot called the punctum, a small opening that drains tears from the surface of the eye, down the nasolacrimal duct, and into the nasal cavity. It is, in effect, the eye's plughole. When a sneeze sends a huge pressure wave through the nose, an open duct would be a problem: some of the mucus, germs, and debris being blasted out of the nose could be forced backward up the duct and into the eyes. The Naked Scientists' explanation of the reflex leans on this: screwing the eyes shut compresses the duct closed, keeping the pressure where it belongs — in the nose — and making sure everything rushes out the front rather than taking a wrong turn into the eye. It is an elegant piece of engineering, if that is indeed what evolution intended.

A third explanation is simpler and less purposeful: synchronisation. Sneezing involves intense, simultaneous contractions across the whole face, and the orbicularis oculi shares neural pathways with the other facial muscles that fire during a sneeze. The eyelid closure may simply be part of a coordinated facial contraction, a side effect of the same neural burst rather than a separately selected adaptation. Similar patterns appear elsewhere in the body: a sudden loud noise or a bright flash can trigger a blink for the same reason, as part of a general startle response, and some reflexes are so contagious that they spread through a whole room, as our fact-check of <a href="/blog/is-yawning-contagious/">Is Yawning Contagious?</a> shows. The fact that a sneeze makes the eyes water, and that the facial muscles tense before the air is released, fits this picture of a whole-face event rather than a single-purpose safety device.

What all three theories share is that none of them requires the eyes to be in danger of leaving the head. The protection hypothesis, the duct hypothesis, and the synchronisation hypothesis all treat the eye as something to shield from spray and pressure, not something to restrain from ejection. And that is consistent with the anatomy: the air spaces of the nose and throat are not connected to anything behind the eye, so there is no route by which the pressure of a sneeze could push an eyeball forward out of its socket. The eyelids, for their part, do not even have the muscle power to hold an eye in place — that job belongs to the six extraocular muscles and the cushion of orbital fat behind the globe. If your eyes were going to pop out, closed eyelids would not stop them.

## The 1882 Story That Started It All

If the anatomy makes the myth impossible, where did it come from? The trail leads, again and again, to a single newspaper story. On April 30, 1882, The New York Times reportedly published an account of a woman riding a streetcar who was, in the words later quoted by researchers, "seized with a sudden fit of sneezing and burst one of her eyeballs." The story, as retold in the Texas A&M explainer, described the eyeball becoming unhinged from aggressive sneezing. The IFLScience account of the legend traces it to the same report, and paediatric researcher Rachel Vreeman of the Indiana University School of Medicine has cited it as the apparent origin of the tale.

The story is unlikely to be true, or at least unlikely to have happened the way it was told. There is, as Huston puts it, little to no evidence to substantiate such claims, and he has described the eyeball-popping tales as far-fetched. A sneeze does raise pressure in the blood vessels of the head — that much is real. Straining of any kind can burst tiny capillaries, which is why a hard sneeze, a bout of vomiting, or even childbirth can leave the whites of the eyes bloodshot or the face bruised. Huston himself notes that during childbirth, excessive straining can cause small veins to haemorrhage, leaving a mother's eyes or face red or markedly bruised. But a burst capillary is not a dislocated eyeball, and vascular pressure is not the same as pressure behind the eye. As Huston puts it, it is irresponsible to claim that such pressure could dislodge the eye from its socket.

There is a second, stranger coda to the 1882 story, preserved in a medical textbook called The Woman Who Swallowed a Toothbrush. According to that account, an elderly woman with Graves' disease — a thyroid condition that can cause the eyes to bulge forward, a condition called exophthalmos — was being examined by a doctor who had forced her eyelids open to inspect her eyes. She sneezed, and one of her eyeballs reportedly popped out of its socket, to the horror of her family, before the doctor pushed it back in. Even if the story is accurate, it describes a patient whose eyes were already protruding from their sockets because of disease, whose eyelids were being held open by force, and whose eyeball was then restored by hand. It is a very special case, not evidence about healthy eyes. In a normal eye, held in place by six muscles and a cushion of fat, a sneeze has no mechanism to do what the story describes. The 1882 streetcar tale, by contrast, has no medical detail at all — no name, no doctor, no follow-up — and reads exactly like the kind of sensational filler that nineteenth-century newspapers printed to fill a column. A frightening anecdote, repeated for 140 years, became a fact in the public mind without ever passing through a laboratory.

## The MythBusters Test

The myth might have stayed in the realm of playground lore forever if a television show had not decided to test it in public. In 2007, the Discovery Channel series MythBusters devoted part of its Viewers' Choice Special — episode 84, which premiered on August 15, 2007 — to the question its audience had requested more than any other. More than 10,000 viewers had written in asking whether sneezing with your eyes open would pop your eyeballs out. Adam Savage and Jamie Hyneman took the challenge on, and the result was both anticlimactic and definitive.

The problem, the hosts discovered immediately, was not the eyes but the sneeze. It was hard for either of them to sneeze with their eyes open at all, because the reflex fought them at every attempt. Adam eventually managed it by forcing his eyelids open with his fingers while the sneeze arrived, with a high-speed camera running to make sure he did not blink. His eyes were fully intact. The show's verdict was "busted," and the episode guide's explanation was straightforward: it would be impossible for your eyes to pop out because of the number of natural attachments that keep the eye inside the socket.

The MythBusters test is not a peer-reviewed clinical trial, and it should not be mistaken for one. Two television presenters and a high-speed camera do not constitute a medical study, and the sample size was, charitably, one sneeze. But the test's value is not statistical; it is demonstrative. It showed, in a form anyone could watch, that the thing the myth predicts simply does not happen when the conditions are created. Combined with the anatomical evidence — six muscles, no pressure pathway, no cases in the medical literature — the demonstration closes the case. It is also worth noting what the show's hosts looked like while doing it: by every account, Adam Savage looked hilariously foolish sneezing with his eyes held open, which is itself a useful data point. The body does not want to do this. It feels wrong, it looks wrong, and the reflex exists for a reason. It is just not the reason the myth claims.

There is a related habit worth mentioning here, because it is genuinely dangerous in a way the open-eye sneeze is not. Some people, trying to be polite or to suppress a sneeze entirely, pinch their nose shut and clamp their mouth closed while the sneeze builds. That traps the pressure inside the head instead of letting it out, and the consequences can be serious: ruptured eardrums, torn throat tissue, and in rare cases, damage to blood vessels. Our own fact-check of <a href="/blog/is-it-dangerous-to-hold-in-a-sneeze/">Is It Dangerous to Hold in a Sneeze?</a> covers that risk in detail. The safe way to sneeze is to let it out, into a tissue or your elbow, with your eyes doing whatever they do naturally — which, for almost everyone, means closing.

## Could a Sneeze Ever Hurt Your Eyes?

Saying that a sneeze cannot pop your eyes out is not the same as saying a sneeze can never hurt your eyes at all. The pressure a sneeze generates is real, and in rare and specific circumstances it has caused real injuries — just not the injury the myth describes. Being precise about the difference is the whole point of a fact-check, so here is what the medical literature actually contains.

The most common sneeze-related eye problem is also the mildest: broken blood vessels. The sudden rise in pressure can rupture tiny capillaries in the white of the eye, producing a subconjunctival haemorrhage — a bright red patch that looks alarming but is usually painless and clears on its own within a week or two. The same thing can happen after vomiting, heavy lifting, or childbirth. It is a burst vessel, not a displaced eyeball, and it has nothing to do with whether the eyes are open or closed.

A rarer and more serious case appeared in the medical literature in 2015. A 32-year-old woman suffered a painful fracture underneath her eyeball — a fracture of the orbital floor, the thin bone that forms the bottom of the eye socket. The doctors who reported the case presumed the fracture was the result of nose-blowing, since the patient could not recall any other injury to the area, and noted that nose-blowing can generate significant intranasal pressure, causing a barotrauma that may fracture the walls of the orbit. Note the details: the injury was a bone fracture, not a dislocation; it was attributed to blowing, not sneezing; and it involved pressure transmitted through the sinuses to a thin wall of bone, not pressure pushing the eyeball itself out of the socket.

Then there is the phenomenon of globe luxation, the medical term for an eyeball dislocating forward out of its socket. It is real, and it is frightening to read about — but the documented cases have nothing to do with sneezing. In a 2006 case study from the Netherlands, a man with floppy eyelids reported that his eye had dislodged from its socket several times over three months. Other reports describe luxation during traumatic accidents, on the sports field, or while putting in contact lenses incorrectly. The eye usually does not fall completely out; it dislodges and pops forward, and it can generally be pushed gently back into place. Crucially, luxation almost always involves an underlying condition — shallow eye sockets, loose eyelids, thyroid disease, or trauma. ScienceAlert's review of the literature found only four cases of spontaneous, random luxation documented between 2002 and 2010, and none of them involved a sneeze. The same review notes that people have accidentally popped their eyes by blocking their nose and mouth while trying to exhale forcefully — a manoeuvre that builds pressure in the head in a way a normal sneeze, with its open exit route, does not.

So the honest summary is this: a sneeze can burst a capillary, and in extraordinarily rare circumstances involving unusual anatomy or forceful nose-blowing, pressure in the sinuses can fracture a thin bone. What a sneeze has never been documented doing, in more than a century of medical literature, is popping a healthy eyeball out of a healthy socket — open eyes or closed. The myth takes a real phenomenon (pressure can injure the eye area) and attaches it to the wrong mechanism (the eyeball flying out) and the wrong trigger (open eyelids).

## A Brief Timeline of the Myth

**April 30, 1882.** The New York Times reportedly publishes the story of a woman on a streetcar whose eyeball "burst" during a fit of sneezing. The anecdote becomes the seed of the legend.

**2006.** A case study from the Netherlands describes a man with floppy eyelids whose eye dislodged from its socket several times in three months — a real case of globe luxation, unrelated to sneezing, that later gets cited in discussions of the myth.

**August 15, 2007.** MythBusters airs its Viewers' Choice Special, episode 84. Adam Savage forces his eyelids open with his fingers and sneezes on camera. His eyes stay in place. The myth is declared busted.

**2015.** A case report describes a 32-year-old woman with a fracture underneath her eyeball, presumed to result from nose-blowing barotrauma — a real pressure injury, but a fractured bone, not a dislocated eye.

**2018–2021.** Medical explainers from Texas A&M, the Mayo Clinic, and Healthline lay out the anatomy in plain language: the reflex is real, the danger is not, and the 1882 story has no evidence behind it.

## The Sneeze's Cousins: Sunlight and Sleep

The open-eye sneeze is not the only strange thing about sneezing, and two of its cousins help explain why the reflex behaves the way it does. The first is the photic sneeze reflex, a genetic quirk that makes between 18 and 35 percent of the world's population sneeze when they step into bright light. It is formally known as ACHOO syndrome, a contrived acronym for Autosomal-dominant Compelling Helio-Ophthalmic Outburst, coined in a 1978 paper. The trait is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: if one parent has it, each child has about a 50 percent chance of inheriting it, a pattern established by a study in the 1960s. A survey by the University of Alabama at Birmingham's School of Optometry found that 67 percent of photic sneezers who responded were female and 94 percent were white, and that the trait was significantly correlated with having a deviated nasal septum.

Nobody fully understands the mechanism, but the leading theory is crossed wiring: the optic nerve, which carries the signal of sudden bright light, runs close to the trigeminal nerve, which carries the sneeze signal, and in some people the signals leak across. The sneezes typically come in bursts of one to ten, followed by a refractory period that can last as long as 24 hours. The phenomenon even worried the military: a 1993 paper in the journal Military Medicine raised concerns that light-induced sneezing might endanger fighter pilots, for whom a split second of lost vision could be lethal, although a small follow-up study found that wearing sunglasses eliminated the effect. The photic sneeze reflex is a reminder that the sneeze and the eye are neighbours in the nervous system, wired together in ways that produce odd, harmless cross-talk — exactly the kind of wiring that also bundles an eyelid blink into every sneeze.

The second cousin is sleep. You cannot sneeze while you are fully asleep — or, more precisely, you cannot sneeze during REM sleep, when most of the body's muscles are temporarily paralysed in a state called atonia, and you almost certainly cannot sneeze in deep sleep either, because the part of the brain that notices nasal irritation is itself asleep. Timothy Tramontana, a family medicine practitioner at the Cleveland Clinic, explains that sneezing requires a certain amount of cortical or brainstem stimulation, and that the threshold for that stimulation actually increases at night: you need to wake up in order to sneeze. The Sleep Foundation is more cautious, noting that research is limited and that it remains unclear whether a sneeze can occur during the lightest stages of sleep without at least a brief awakening — but it agrees that sneezing during REM sleep is thought to be unlikely, because the muscles needed are paralysed. If you have ever felt that you sneezed in your sleep, the likeliest explanation is that you were in light sleep, or that the irritation was strong enough to wake you first, and you drifted back to sleep with no memory of it. The same shutdown that suppresses sneezing also suppresses coughing, hiccups, hunger, and thirst — the body, at rest, turns down the reflexes it does not need.

Both cousins point to the same conclusion. Sneezing is not a single action but a programme the brain runs, and the programme includes the eyes: it closes them when you sneeze, it can be triggered by light entering them, and it switches off when you sleep. None of that makes the eyeball-popping story true. It makes the real story richer: a reflex arc that links the nose, the eyes, and the brainstem in a network that is still not fully mapped, and that produces side effects — a blink, a sun-sneeze, a silent night — that people have been turning into folklore for centuries. There is a reason the same bodily function generates so many myths. The sneeze is violent, involuntary, and slightly mysterious, and the eyes are involved in ways that feel significant. The mistake is to fill the mystery with the most dramatic explanation available instead of the anatomical one.

It is also worth clearing up one more piece of folklore that often travels with this myth, since it shows how these stories breed. You may have heard that a sneeze is equivalent to one-eighth of an orgasm. That, too, is false. Sneezes and orgasms are both reflexes involving muscle tension and release, and there is some evidence that both release endorphins, but scientifically speaking they are two very different things — and neither one will make your eyeballs pop out. The persistence of both myths says something about how the sneeze is perceived: as a small, pleasurable, slightly dangerous explosion that the body barely controls. The reality is more mundane and more impressive: a precisely coordinated reflex that clears your airways several times a day without you ever having to think about it.

## The Verdict

The claim that you cannot sneeze with your eyes open — and that your eyeballs will pop out if you try — is **False**.

You can sneeze with your eyes open. An allergist at Texas A&M calls it "absolutely possible," a Mayo Clinic optometrist explains that it is an involuntary reflex rather than a requirement, and MythBusters proved it on television in 2007 when Adam Savage held his eyelids open with his fingers and sneezed on camera with his eyes intact. Your eyes stay in place because six extraocular muscles and a cushion of orbital fat hold each one firmly in its socket, and because the air passages a sneeze uses are not connected to the space behind the eye. There is no route for sneeze pressure to reach the eyeball, and no case in the medical literature of a sneeze dislodging a healthy eye. The eyelids could not hold an eye in even if they had to — they simply do not have the muscle for it.

The legend traces to a single newspaper anecdote from April 30, 1882, about a woman on a streetcar whose eyeball supposedly burst during a sneezing fit. It has no medical detail, no corroboration, and no plausible mechanism, and the experts who have examined it call it a far-fetched tale. The real injuries a sneeze can cause to the eyes are far less cinematic: burst capillaries that leave the eye bloodshot, and in one extraordinary case, a fractured orbital floor bone attributed to forceful nose-blowing. Neither is an eyeball popping out.

So the next time someone warns you to keep your eyes shut when you sneeze, you can tell them the truth: your eyes close because your nervous system bundles the blink into the sneeze, probably to shield them from the spray and to seal the tear duct against backflow — not because they are in danger of escaping. You can force them open if you really want to. You will look ridiculous, you will feel ridiculous, and your eyes will be exactly where you left them. The reflex is real. The danger is not.
