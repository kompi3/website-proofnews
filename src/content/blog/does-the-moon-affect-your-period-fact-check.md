Every month, somewhere on the internet, a woman posts the same observation: her period arrived with the full moon. Again. The comments pile up fast. Hundreds of women reply that the same thing happens to them, that they bleed with the new moon or ovulate under the full one, that their cycles run in step with the sky. Period-tracking apps have noticed too, and some now sell lunar-cycle features that promise to help you "sync" with the moon. Wellness influencers go further, dividing menstruation into the "white moon cycle" — bleeding at the new moon, ovulating at the full — and the "red moon cycle" — bleeding at the full moon — and assigning each one a personality, a power, a meaning.

It is one of the oldest health beliefs in the world, and on the surface it looks like simple arithmetic. The moon takes about 29.5 days to go from one new moon to the next. The average menstrual cycle lasts about 29 days. Two cycles, nearly the same length, running side by side for thousands of years. Surely they must be connected.

But nearly the same length is not the same thing as a connection, and when scientists have actually tested the idea — with diaries kept for decades, with data from millions of cycles, with the largest studies ever run on the question — the answer has turned out to be far more interesting, and far less mystical, than the folklore suggests. There is a real signal in the data. It is just much smaller, much rarer, and much more fragile than the moon-syncing industry claims.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, is simple: a woman's menstrual cycle synchronizes with the phases of the moon. The most specific version says that women tend to start menstruating at the new moon and ovulate at the full moon. A softer version says only that periods "tend" to cluster around the full or new moon. The most commercial version, promoted by fertility apps and wellness brands, says that tracking your cycle against the moon can help you understand your fertility, your energy, and your health — and that living out of step with the moon is a problem to be fixed.

None of this is new. The English word "menstruation" shares a root with the Latin word for month, *mensis*, and with the Greek word for moon, *mene*. The resemblance in length was obvious to every culture that watched the sky and kept track of the body at the same time. The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle believed that menstruation took place during the waning moon. Roman writers connected the moon to women's bodies as a matter of course. Charles Darwin himself theorised that the lunar link came from our species' time as seashore-dwelling creatures who lived in sync with the tides, an idea that modern writers still repeat.

And yet the idea has never gone unchallenged. In 1806, the French physician J. A. Murat reported that the menstrual cycle is not governed by the lunar cycle — more than two centuries ago, a doctor looked at the evidence and said no. The idea resurfaced anyway, again and again, because it feels right. Two monthly rhythms, running in parallel, seem too neat to be a coincidence.

That feeling is the heart of the problem. Human beings are pattern-seeking animals, and a 29.5-day lunar cycle next to a roughly 29-day menstrual cycle is a pattern begging to be seen. But a pattern that feels obvious is not the same as a pattern that survives measurement, and this one has now been measured more carefully than almost any folk belief in medicine.

## Why the Numbers Make the Myth So Believable

Before looking at the studies, it is worth understanding why the claim is so hard to kill — because the numbers really do line up, at first glance.

The moon's synodic month — the time from one new moon to the next — is 29.53 days. The average menstrual cycle is often quoted as 28 days, but large datasets put the real average closer to 28.8 or 29 days. Normal menstrual cycles range from 23 to 35 days. So the average cycle and the lunar month sit within a day of each other, and any individual cycle that happens to last 29 or 30 days will drift in and out of step with the moon over the course of a few months, aligning for a while and then falling out of alignment again, purely by arithmetic.

That drift matters, because it creates exactly the kind of experience that convinces people. If your cycle is 29 days long and the moon's is 29.53, the two will coincide for a few months, then separate, then coincide again. During the months they coincide, the alignment feels uncanny — your period arriving on the full moon, month after month. During the months they do not, you are less likely to notice, or to remember. Psychologists call this confirmation bias: we remember the hits and forget the misses, and a belief built on remembered hits feels like a fact.

There is a second reason the claim feels true, and it is subtler. Many women do experience their cycles changing with the seasons and with their sleep — and moonlight is, after all, light. The idea that the moon could influence the body through light is not absurd on its face. It is a hypothesis worth testing. And scientists have now tested it, in several very different ways, with very different answers depending on how they asked the question.

## The Early Studies: Small Samples, Big Claims

The formal study of lunar-menstrual synchrony began in earnest in the 1980s. Winnifred Cutler and colleagues reported evidence that women whose cycle lengths were close to 29.5 days were more likely to menstruate during the new moon and ovulate during the full moon. A small 1986 study published in *Psychiatry Research* found that around 30 percent of participants menstruated during the new moon. These were the studies that launched a thousand magazine articles, and they shared a weakness: they were small, and their findings did not hold up when other researchers tried to repeat them.

A larger 1987 study by Friedmann found no evidence of lunar-menstrual synchrony at all. The early literature, in other words, was exactly what you would expect from a weak or nonexistent effect studied in small groups: some studies found something, some found nothing, and nobody could say which result to trust.

The related belief in menstrual synchrony between women — the so-called McClintock effect, named after a 1971 study of 135 women at Wellesley College that claimed women living together saw their periods converge — followed the same arc. Four studies failed to replicate the result, a 1992 comparison of all the studies concluded that menstrual synchrony had not been demonstrated at all, and a 2006 study of college-age women over five months found no conclusive evidence for it. Synchrony, whether with the moon or with roommates, turned out to be very hard to prove.

For decades, that was roughly where the science stood: intriguing hints, failed replications, and no resolution. Then two things changed. First, a group of chronobiologists in Germany decided to look at the question the slow way, following individual women for decades instead of sampling hundreds of women for a month. Second, period-tracking apps produced datasets of a size that earlier researchers could not have imagined — millions of cycles, logged day by day.

## The 22 Women Who Kept Diaries for Decades

The most careful small study of the question was published in January 2021 in the journal *Science Advances*. A team led by Charlotte Helfrich-Förster, a chronobiologist at the University of Würzburg in Germany, analysed long-term menstrual records kept by 22 women — in some cases over a period of 32 years, with an average of about 15 years of records per woman. Instead of asking whether all women sync with the moon, the researchers asked whether individual women do, and under what conditions.

The answer was yes — sometimes. Women whose menstrual cycles lasted longer than 27 days were intermittently synchronous with the moon's cycles of light and gravity. Menstruation tended to cluster around the new or full moon, and the alignment was strongest in winter. In the second halves of 1961, 1979, 1997 and 2015, seven out of nine women in the study were synchronous with the change of full moon and new moon. The researchers found that all three lunar cycles — the synodic month of 29.53 days, driven by changing moonlight; the anomalistic month of 27.55 days, driven by the moon's elliptical orbit and its gravitational pull as it moves between its closest and farthest points from Earth; and, to a lesser degree, the tropical month — affected the onset of menstruation.

But the study's most important finding was not the synchrony. It was the disappearance of the synchrony. With age, and with exposure to artificial light at night, the women's cycles shortened and lost their tie to the moon. The nightly moonlight, the researchers concluded, seemed to be the strongest synchroniser — but modern life had largely switched it off. Their hypothesis, stated plainly in the paper, was that in ancient times human reproductive behaviour was synchronous with the moon, but that modern lifestyles have changed reproductive physiology and behaviour.

The authors were careful about the limits of their own work. Twenty-two women is a small sample, however long their diaries ran. And the pattern they found was intermittent: synchrony came and went, even in the women who showed it. The study did not show that the moon controls the menstrual cycle. It showed that, in some women, under some conditions, the two rhythms sometimes fall into step.

## The 7.5-Million-Cycle Answer

If the Würzburg study is the best evidence for a lunar link, the best evidence against one comes from a dataset of an entirely different scale. In 2019, researchers working with the period-tracking app Clue analysed 7.5 million menstrual cycles from 1.5 million users. Their conclusion was blunt: women's menstrual cycles do not sync with the lunar cycle. Period start dates were distributed essentially at random across the lunar month.

A second large check arrived in March 2021, when a study using menstrual cycle onset data from 529 women found no association between cycle onset and lunar phase. The big datasets, in other words, kept saying no — while the small, long-term diaries kept saying sometimes.

How can both be true? The answer lies in what each kind of study can see. A dataset of 7.5 million cycles is superb at detecting a population-wide pattern: if most women synced with the moon, it would show up immediately. It is poor at detecting a pattern that exists only in a minority of women, only some of the time, and only under particular lighting conditions. The diary study was the reverse: superb at following individuals through time, poor at saying anything about the population at large. The two results are not really in conflict. They describe different things — and the thing they agree on is that there is no strong, reliable, population-wide lunar effect in the modern world.

## The 2024 Study: Your Body Clock, Not the Moon

The most recent large study, published in *Science Advances* in April 2024, took the question in a new direction — and found that the real driver of the menstrual rhythm may not be the moon at all, but the body's own internal clock.

Researchers led by Dr Claude Gronfier analysed almost 27,000 menstrual cycles from more than 3,000 European and North American women, tracking the first day of menstruation in each cycle. They found only a weak correlation between menstrual and lunar cycles — and, crucially, the correlation was not even the same on different continents. In Europe, menstrual cycles tended to begin at the waxing crescent; for North American women, they often began at the full moon. If the moon itself were driving the cycle, the pattern should look the same everywhere. The fact that it varied by continent suggested something else was going on: lifestyle factors, especially sleep-wake cycles, were the more likely explanation.

The study's central finding was about the body's internal clock, known as the circadian clock. The data revealed something the researchers called "phase jumps" — moments when a woman's internal clock falls out of sync with her regular cycle length and self-corrects by jumping ahead to the next stable state. As Gronfier told *BBC Science Focus*: "If the cycle lengthens, for any reason, this clock-based process adapts to quickly shorten it." Phase jumps are a sign of a phenomenon called relative coordination, a typical feature of circadian clocks — the same kind of out-of-sync feeling that follows travel across time zones.

The paper's conclusion, as reported by *BBC Science Focus*, was that the circadian clock maintains the rhythm of women's ovaries, and that any lunar correlation is likely driven more by lifestyle than by the moon. The authors did add one caveat: in communities where women are more exposed to moonlight, the influence of the moon on menstrual cycles may be stronger. But for most women, in most of the modern world, the moon is a bystander, and the clock inside the body is doing the work.

This fits with what we know about the circadian system from other areas of health. Light is one of the most powerful regulators of human biology, and researchers have used chronobiological approaches such as light therapy to treat sleep disorders, depression, and some cancers. The 2024 study even suggested that understanding the circadian control of ovulation could open new treatments for ovulation disorders — and perhaps, one day, help improve fertility. Our earlier fact-check on weekend sleep, <a href="/blog/can-you-catch-up-on-sleep-on-weekends-fact-check/">Can You Catch Up on Sleep on Weekends? The 90,903-Person Study Behind the Claim</a>, found the same theme: the body's internal clock is stubborn, and it does not reset just because the calendar says it should.

## The 2025 Follow-Up: The Signal Is Fading

If the 2021 diary study left the question open, the follow-up published in *Science Advances* in 2025 narrowed it further. The same research group, again led by Helfrich-Förster, analysed the menstrual records of 176 women spanning up to 37 years — a much larger group than the original 22, followed for just as long.

The pattern they found was striking in its specificity. Menstrual cycles recorded before 2010 showed significant synchronisation with the moon. Cycles recorded after 2010 mostly lost that connection — except during January, when the gravitational forces between the moon, the sun and the Earth are at their strongest. The researchers examined all three lunar cycles, and found that the synodic month, the cycle of changing moonlight, acted as the strongest influence before widespread light pollution weakened it.

Age mattered too. Women under 35 with cycle lengths close to 29.5 days had the highest likelihood of temporary synchronisation: on average, 23.6 percent of their cycles synced with the full or new moon. Women over 35 showed only 9.5 percent synchronisation. The signal, in other words, is real but small, concentrated in younger women, fading with age, and fading over calendar time as artificial light has spread.

This is the most honest summary of the evidence available today: there may once have been a stronger lunar influence on human reproduction, and a faint echo of it may still be detectable in some women under some conditions — but for the overwhelming majority of women living in the electrically lit world, the moon is not setting the schedule.

## Moonlight, Melatonin, and the Modern Night

Why would artificial light matter so much? The proposed mechanism runs through melatonin, the hormone that signals darkness to the body, and through the circadian system that melatonin helps to set. Moonlight is dim — far dimmer than a streetlamp, a phone screen, or a bedroom light — but in a world without electric lighting, it was the brightest light in the night, and it varied predictably across the month. A body that used moonlight as a monthly time cue would have had something real to respond to.

In the modern world, that cue is drowned out. Artificial light at night suppresses melatonin and shifts circadian timing, which in turn can influence menstrual regularity. Light is a far more powerful regulator of human biology than moonlight alone, and the light most of us live under after sunset is constant rather than cyclical. If the moon ever acted as a monthly metronome for human reproduction, the metronome is now buried under a much louder, steadier hum.

This is also why claims about melatonin supplements and sleep deserve care. Our fact-check <a href="/blog/does-the-sleepy-girl-mocktail-help-you-sleep-fact-check/">Does the Sleepy Girl Mocktail Help You Sleep? What the Trials Actually Show</a> looked at a viral drink built around tart cherry juice and magnesium, and found that the evidence for its sleep claims was far thinner than its fans suggested. Melatonin is a real hormone with real effects on sleep timing — but "real hormone" and "the moon controls your period" are very different claims, and the second does not follow from the first.

## What About Gravity?

There is a second proposed mechanism, and it is the weakest part of the story: gravity. The moon's gravity raises the ocean tides, so it is natural to wonder whether it could tug on the body as well. The 2021 Würzburg paper did find correlations with the moon's gravimetric cycles — the anomalistic month, tied to the moon's changing distance from Earth — and the 2025 follow-up found residual synchrony in January, when gravitational forces peak.

But a correlation with a gravitational cycle is not proof of a gravitational cause, and the gravitational explanation is the most contested part of the research. The moon's gravitational pull on an individual human body is vanishingly small — far weaker than the pull of nearby objects, and far too weak, on its own, to move fluid or tissue in any meaningful way. No plausible biological pathway has been demonstrated by which such a tiny force could reset the timing of ovulation. The more likely explanation for the gravimetric correlations, if they are real at all, is that the gravitational cycle and the light cycle run together — the moon is closest to Earth at roughly the same times that its light is changing — so a study that finds a link to one will often find a link to the other. The light, not the pull, is the mechanism with a plausible pathway through the eyes, the brain, and melatonin.

This matters because the gravity version of the claim is the one most often repeated in wellness circles, where "lunar gravity" is invoked as a force that moves the blood and the womb the way it moves the sea. The sea moves because it is enormous and unconfined. A human body is neither.

## The Business of Moon Syncing

The claim does not live in folklore alone. It is now a product. Period-tracking apps market lunar features; wellness brands sell moon-cycle journals, courses, and coaching; social media is full of charts mapping the four moon phases onto the four phases of the menstrual cycle, with advice about when to rest, when to create, when to socialise, and when to make decisions.

Some of this is harmless fun — a poetic way to think about a monthly rhythm, and a framework some women find genuinely comforting. But some of it makes health claims that the evidence does not support. There is no consistent scientific evidence that syncing your cycle with the moon improves fertility, and no evidence that living "out of step" with the moon harms your health. A woman whose period arrives at the full moon one month and the new moon three months later is not broken, out of balance, or disconnected from nature. She is normal. The average cycle length guarantees that most women will sometimes align with the moon and sometimes not, and the alignment itself means very little.

It is also worth noting how the claim interacts with a second, related myth. Our fact-check <a href="/blog/full-moon-behavior-myth-fact-check/">Does the Full Moon Really Affect Sleep, Behavior, and Crime?</a> examined the broader belief that the full moon changes human behaviour — more births, more crimes, more emergency room visits — and found that the evidence there is similarly thin. The moon is blamed for a remarkable number of human phenomena, and it keeps being acquitted.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The moon does not control your period — not reliably, not universally, and not in the way the popular claim describes. The largest studies, including an analysis of 7.5 million cycles from 1.5 million app users and a 2024 study of almost 27,000 cycles from more than 3,000 women, find no consistent population-wide synchrony between menstrual cycles and lunar phases. What the evidence does support is narrower and stranger: in a minority of women — younger women, with cycles near 29.5 days, living with little artificial light at night — periods can temporarily fall into step with the moon, and that synchrony has been fading for decades as electric light has spread. The resemblance in cycle length is real. The idea that the moon is steering the cycle is not.

## What This Means for You

If your period sometimes arrives with the full moon, enjoy the poetry of it — but do not read a diagnosis into it. Track your own cycle for a few months, and you will see the arithmetic at work: a 29-day cycle and a 29.53-day moon will line up for a while, drift apart, and line up again, with no intervention from the sky. Apps that chart your cycle against the moon are charting a coincidence, and a pretty one, but a coincidence all the same.

What does deserve attention is your own pattern, not the moon's. A cycle that is usually regular and suddenly changes, bleeding that is very heavy or very painful, or periods that stop altogether are all reasons to talk to a doctor — and the doctor will ask about stress, sleep, weight changes, medication, and thyroid function long before asking about the moon. Those are the factors with demonstrated power over the menstrual cycle. The moon, for most women in the modern world, is scenery.

And there is something quietly reassuring in the real story. The menstrual cycle is not governed by a distant rock. It is governed by a clock inside the body — a clock that keeps time through light and darkness, through hormones and the brain, and that corrects itself when it drifts. That clock is yours, not the moon's, and it has been keeping time far longer than any calendar.
