It starts, as so many modern health scares do, with a router blinking in the corner of a bedroom. A parent reads a post warning that the little box on the shelf is bathing the family in radiation all night. A school board fields angry emails demanding the Wi-Fi be switched off during lessons. A viral video claims the invisible signals passing through our walls are quietly causing cancer, and that governments and telecom companies are covering it up. The fear is understandable: you cannot see, smell, or feel Wi-Fi, and "radiation" is a word that most people associate with nuclear accidents and X-ray warning signs.

But the claim that Wi-Fi causes cancer has been investigated more thoroughly than almost any other technology scare of the last thirty years, and the answer that keeps coming back is the same. The World Health Organization, Cancer Research UK, the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency, and the largest review of the evidence ever conducted all reach the same conclusion: at the power levels used by ordinary routers and devices, there is no convincing evidence that Wi-Fi causes cancer or any other disease. This article traces the claim from its origins to the latest science, including the rat study that fuels most of the fear and the international classification that sounds far scarier than it is.

## What Wi-Fi Actually Puts Into the Air

To judge the claim, it helps to know what a Wi-Fi router actually emits. Wi-Fi communicates using radiofrequency electromagnetic fields, usually called RF fields, on the 2.4 gigahertz and 5 gigahertz bands. These are radio waves, the same broad family of energy that carries FM radio, television broadcasts, and mobile phone calls. The power involved is tiny. The Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency notes that a typical Wi-Fi router transmits at around 0.1 watts, roughly the power of a small LED night light, and far less than a mobile phone pressed against your head during a call.

Just as important is the type of energy involved. Radio waves are non-ionizing radiation. That single word, non-ionizing, is the hinge on which the whole debate turns. Ionizing radiation, such as X-rays and gamma rays, carries enough energy to knock electrons off atoms, break chemical bonds, and directly damage DNA inside cells. That damage is a known route to cancer, which is why X-rays are used sparingly and with shielding. Radio waves do not carry enough energy to do any of that. As the American Cancer Society puts it, RF waves "don't have enough energy to cause cancer by directly damaging the DNA (genes) inside cells." Cancer Research UK makes the same point in plainer language: "Radio waves do not have enough energy to damage DNA. So there's no good explanation for how radio waves could cause cancer."

That does not settle the question by itself. A substance or exposure can, in principle, cause cancer without directly breaking DNA, and scientists have spent decades looking for such an indirect mechanism from radio waves. The only biological effect of RF fields that is firmly established is heating: at very high intensities, radio waves warm tissue, the same principle that cooks food in a microwave oven. The World Health Organization notes that the only health effect from RF fields identified in scientific reviews has been an increase in body temperature of more than one degree Celsius, and only at the very high field intensities found in certain industrial facilities such as RF heaters. The exposure levels from base stations and wireless networks, the WHO says, "are so low that the temperature increases are insignificant and do not affect human health."

There is one more physical fact that matters, and it is reassuring: radio wave intensity falls off very rapidly with distance. Your exposure from a router across the room is a small fraction of your exposure from a phone held to your ear, and your exposure from a phone is itself far below the safety limits. Distance is the simplest protection there is, and with Wi-Fi you usually have plenty of it.

## The "Possibly Carcinogenic" Label That Started Much of the Fear

If you have ever read an article warning about Wi-Fi and cancer, it almost certainly mentions that the World Health Organization classified radiofrequency radiation as "possibly carcinogenic to humans." That is true, and it sounds alarming. It deserves a careful explanation, because the label means something much narrower than it appears.

In 2011, the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer, known as IARC, placed RF electromagnetic fields in Group 2B, the "possibly carcinogenic" category. The classification was based on limited evidence from human observational studies, mostly concerning heavy mobile phone use and certain brain tumours, and less than sufficient evidence from animal studies. IARC's categories describe the strength of the evidence that something can cause cancer, not the size of the risk, and Group 2B is the weakest category in which a hazard is considered possible at all. It is a label that says, in effect, "we cannot rule this out, but we have not established it."

The company it keeps makes the point clearly. Group 2B also contains pickled vegetables and aloe vera extract, along with coffee for many years and the carpentry trade. Nobody suggests pickled vegetables are a serious cancer threat, and nobody should read that into the Wi-Fi classification either. The label was also made in 2011, before the largest and most comprehensive reviews of the evidence existed, and it was based mainly on mobile phones rather than on Wi-Fi, which transmits at lower power and from further away from the body.

That classification has never been updated, and it continues to be quoted in alarming social media posts as though it were a fresh verdict. It is not. It is a fifteen-year-old caution label, and the evidence gathered since then has pointed the other way.

## What the Biggest Human Studies Actually Found

The strongest evidence about Wi-Fi and cancer does not come from laboratory experiments or from theory. It comes from studying real people, in large numbers, over long periods. And the most comprehensive study of its kind was published in 2024.

Commissioned by the World Health Organization and published in the journal Environment International, the review was led by researchers from the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency. The team screened more than 5,000 studies and included 63 of them, covering research published between 1994 and 2022, with participants from 22 countries. It examined exposure from mobile phones and cordless phones, from base stations and broadcast antennas, and from occupational exposure, and looked at brain tumours, leukaemia, and other cancers.

The findings were clear. Mobile phone use was not associated with an increased risk of glioma, the most common malignant brain tumour, with a combined relative risk of 1.01, meaning essentially no difference from non-users. Nor was it associated with meningioma (0.92), acoustic neuroma (1.03), pituitary tumours (0.81), salivary gland tumours (0.91), or brain tumours in children and young people (1.06). There was no increase in risk even among people who had used mobile phones for ten years or more. Exposure from fixed transmitters such as base stations was not associated with childhood leukaemia or paediatric brain tumours either. The review's lead author summarised the work by saying that the systematic review was based on a much larger dataset than the one examined by IARC in 2011, and that the team could be more confident in the conclusion that "exposure to radio waves from wireless technology is not a human health hazard."

This was not the first large study to reach this conclusion. The INTERPHONE study, a 13-country case-control study and the largest of its kind, compared more than 5,000 people who had developed brain tumours with a similar group without tumours, and most published analyses found no statistically significant increases in brain or central nervous system cancers related to higher amounts of cell phone use. The Danish cohort study, which followed hundreds of thousands of mobile phone subscribers, and the UK Million Women Study, which tracked more than a million women, both found no association between mobile phone use and cancer, even among people who had been subscribers for thirteen years or more.

There is also a telling piece of population-level evidence. Mobile phone use has exploded over the last twenty-five years, from a rarity to something carried by nearly every adult on the planet, and Wi-Fi has gone from a novelty to a fixture in almost every home, school, and office. If these technologies caused brain cancer at any meaningful rate, cancer registries should have recorded a corresponding rise. They have not. Brain cancer rates have stayed essentially flat while wireless exposure has increased many times over.

None of this proves that a risk of zero exists for every person in every circumstance, and honest scientists do not claim that it does. But it is a large, consistent body of human evidence, gathered across decades and continents, and it points firmly in one direction.

## The Rat Study That Fuels the Fear

If the human evidence is so reassuring, why does the fear persist? A large part of the answer is a single, widely reported animal study from the United States National Toxicology Program, usually called the NTP study, published in 2018.

The NTP exposed rats and mice to radiofrequency radiation for two years, for nine hours a day, and reported clear evidence of heart tumours, specifically malignant schwannomas, in male rats, along with some evidence of tumours of the brain and adrenal gland. The headlines were dramatic, and they still circulate today in posts warning about Wi-Fi. But the details of the study matter enormously, and they are almost always left out.

First, the doses were extreme. The animals were exposed to whole-body radiation at levels of 1.5 to 6 watts per kilogram of body weight, with the heart tumours appearing at levels about four times higher than the maximum exposure a person would receive from a mobile phone pressed directly against the head. A Wi-Fi router across a room delivers a tiny fraction of that. Second, the study used 900 megahertz signals designed to mimic mobile phone transmission, not Wi-Fi. The NTP itself noted that it did not study the frequencies or modulations used by Wi-Fi. Third, the results were inconsistent in ways that undermine a simple interpretation: the tumours appeared in male rats but not in female rats or in mice, and the exposed male rats actually lived longer than the unexposed control animals.

Animal studies at very high doses are a standard way of testing for hazards, and the NTP study was a serious piece of work. But a hazard found at doses far above anything a person encounters, in one sex of one species, using a different signal from the one Wi-Fi uses, is not evidence that ordinary Wi-Fi exposure causes cancer in people. It is a reason to keep studying the question, and scientists have. The human studies described above are part of that continued study, and they have not found the risk the rat study seemed to suggest.

## How Exposure From Wi-Fi Compares

One reason the claim feels plausible is that Wi-Fi is everywhere, all the time. The router never sleeps, the phone is always connected, and the laptop is always searching for a network. Constant exposure sounds like a lot of exposure. But "constant" and "strong" are different things, and the numbers are worth seeing side by side.

| Source | Typical power | Distance from your body | Relative exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wi-Fi router | About 0.1 watts | Usually metres away, across a room | Very low |
| Mobile phone on a call | Up to 1–2 watts, adjusted to the minimum needed | Pressed against the head | Low, and still below the safety limit |
| Mobile phone base station | Higher power, but serving a whole area | Tens to hundreds of metres away | Extremely low |
| Microwave oven (for comparison) | Around 1,000 watts, shielded | About a metre away while running | Very low outside the oven |

The pattern is clear. The device that worries people most, the router, is among the weakest sources of radio waves in the home, and it is usually the furthest away. The device that delivers the most exposure, the phone, is still limited by law: in the United States, the Federal Communications Commission caps the specific absorption rate, the amount of RF energy absorbed by the body, at 1.6 watts per kilogram of tissue, and phones must be tested to stay below it. International guidelines set by the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection impose similar limits. Wi-Fi devices operate far below those limits, and measurements carried out in many countries confirm that typical Wi-Fi exposure is a small fraction of the permitted maximum.

This is why health agencies talk about Wi-Fi with such calm. Ireland's Environmental Protection Agency states that the levels of radiofrequency fields emitted by Wi-Fi devices are very low and well below recommended exposure limits, and that there is no established scientific evidence that exposure at these low levels adversely affects the health of the general population, including children. ARPANSA says the same for Australia: "There is no established scientific evidence that the low exposure to RF EME from Wi-Fi adversely affects the health of children or the general population."

## What About Children, Schools, and Bedrooms?

The most emotionally charged version of the claim concerns children. Their bodies are still developing, their skulls are thinner, and they will accumulate a lifetime of exposure starting from a young age. Some parents have campaigned to remove Wi-Fi from schools, and some keep the router switched off at night. These are understandable precautions from people who love their children, but they are not precautions the evidence supports.

Every major health agency that has examined the question has reached the same conclusion for children as for adults. The WHO's fact sheet on base stations and wireless networks, which remains its standing position, concludes that "there is no convincing scientific evidence that the weak RF signals from base stations and wireless networks cause adverse health effects," and it does not carve out an exception for children. The WHO-commissioned 2024 review specifically examined paediatric brain tumours and childhood leukaemia and found no association with radiofrequency exposure, including from base stations near homes and schools. ARPANSA's statement on Wi-Fi mentions children explicitly and finds no established evidence of harm to them.

There is a related claim worth addressing here, because it often travels with the cancer claim: that some people suffer from "electromagnetic hypersensitivity," experiencing headaches, fatigue, and other symptoms they attribute to Wi-Fi. This is a real experience of real distress, and the people who report it deserve sympathy rather than dismissal. But the WHO's fact sheet on electromagnetic hypersensitivity states plainly that EMF "has not been shown to cause such symptoms." In carefully controlled experiments where volunteers could not tell whether a Wi-Fi signal was on or off, their symptoms did not track the signal. That does not mean the symptoms are imaginary; it means Wi-Fi is not their cause, and switching off the router is unlikely to cure them.

## The Dissent, Honestly Stated

A fair fact-check should not pretend the science is unanimous, and on this subject it is not entirely. A minority of researchers argue that the WHO-commissioned 2024 review was too quick to clear radiofrequency radiation, and they have published a detailed critique of it. In 2025, researchers Lennart Hardell and Mona Nilsson argued that the review overlooked results showing increased tumour risks in the most heavily exposed groups, in the most exposed part of the head, and after the longest time since first exposure, and they called for the review to be retracted. Other researchers have argued that epidemiology alone cannot settle the question and that more attention should be paid to laboratory findings.

This dissent is part of how science works, and it is worth reporting rather than hiding. But it is important to keep it in proportion. Hardell is a long-standing critic of the mainstream consensus on this subject, and his critique has not changed the position of the WHO, Cancer Research UK, the US Food and Drug Administration, or any other major health agency. The mainstream position rests on dozens of large human studies and decades of monitoring, not on a single review, and the dissenters' own evidence consists largely of the same small and inconsistent findings that the larger reviews weighed and found unconvincing. If the evidence shifts, the agencies' positions should shift with it, and the WHO's International EMF Project continues to monitor the literature for exactly that reason. As of today, though, the dissent remains a minority view, and the consensus is firmly on the other side.

There is also an honest caveat that even the mainstream agencies accept. Wireless technology at its current scale is only a few decades old, and no study can observe a lifetime of exposure to a technology that has not existed for a lifetime. Long-term monitoring continues, and it should. But "we should keep watching" is a very different statement from "Wi-Fi causes cancer," and it is not a reason to fear a router in the corner of a room.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: False.**

The claim that Wi-Fi causes cancer is false. Wi-Fi uses weak, non-ionizing radio waves that do not carry enough energy to damage DNA, the mechanism by which known radiation-related cancers begin. The World Health Organization's standing position is that there is no convincing scientific evidence that the weak RF signals from wireless networks cause adverse health effects. The most comprehensive review ever conducted, a WHO-commissioned analysis of 63 studies published between 1994 and 2022, found no association between radiofrequency exposure and brain cancer, including among people who used mobile phones, which transmit at higher power and closer to the body than Wi-Fi, for more than ten years. Cancer Research UK states the conclusion directly: Wi-Fi and Bluetooth do not cause cancer, and there is no evidence to suggest that wireless technology increases cancer risk.

The two pieces of evidence most often cited by those making the claim do not change this. The IARC Group 2B "possibly carcinogenic" classification from 2011 is a caution label based on limited evidence, shared with pickled vegetables and aloe vera extract, and it predates the largest reviews. The National Toxicology Program rat study used whole-body exposures several times higher than the maximum from a phone, at frequencies Wi-Fi does not use, and found tumours only in male rats. Neither finding has been replicated in human populations, and brain cancer rates have remained flat while wireless use has soared.

That is not the same as saying the science is finished. Researchers continue to monitor long-term exposure, and if credible evidence of harm emerges, health agencies should say so. But a claim is judged on the evidence that exists, and the evidence that exists says Wi-Fi at ordinary power levels does not cause cancer. The router can stay on.
