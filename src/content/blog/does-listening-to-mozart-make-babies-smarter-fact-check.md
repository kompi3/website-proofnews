Every new parent has met this advice. Put Mozart on in the nursery, the story goes, and your baby will grow up smarter. Play the sonatas while they sleep, during tummy time, even before they are born, and the music will wire their brain for mathematics, language, and genius. The claim has sold millions of CDs, shaped state budgets, and survived for more than thirty years. It is also, as a claim about babies, false — and the story of how it went from a one-page laboratory report to a global parenting industry is one of the clearest examples ever recorded of how a real scientific finding can be stretched until it means the opposite of what it actually found.

This is not a story about a fraud. Nobody faked the original data. The study at the centre of it was published in one of the world's most respected journals, and its authors were careful, honest scientists. The distortion happened later, in newsrooms, bookshops, boardrooms, and state legislatures, where each retelling dropped a qualifier and added a promise. By the time the claim reached parents, the college students had become babies, the ten-minute boost had become a lifetime of intelligence, and a narrow test of spatial reasoning had become a guarantee about IQ.

## The Claim

The popular version of the Mozart effect comes in several flavours, and they are worth separating, because they are not equally wrong. The strongest version says that playing Mozart to a baby raises their IQ, permanently, and that the earlier you start — ideally before birth — the cleverer the child will become. A softer version says that classical music in general is "brain food" for children, while other kinds of music are not. A third version, aimed at adults, says that listening to Mozart before an exam or a test will make you smarter.

All three versions share the same structure: passive listening to a particular composer's music produces a lasting increase in general intelligence. And all three rest on the same piece of evidence, a study so small that the entire paper fits on less than a single page of a journal. The problem is not that the study was bad. The problem is that the claim it is used to support is not the claim the study tested, and the gap between the two is where the entire myth lives.

## Where the Claim Came From: 36 Students and a Piano Sonata

In October 1993, the journal *Nature* published a short letter titled "Music and spatial task performance." Its authors were Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw, and Katherine Ky, researchers at the Center for the Neurobiology of Learning and Memory at the University of California, Irvine. The experiment was small and tidy. Thirty-six college students each went through three listening conditions on different days: ten minutes of Mozart's Sonata for Two Pianos in D major (K. 448), ten minutes of relaxation instructions, and ten minutes of silence. After each condition, they completed a spatial reasoning task — the kind of puzzle where you mentally fold a piece of paper or rotate an object in your mind — taken from the Stanford-Binet intelligence test.

After the Mozart condition, the students did better. The authors converted the scores into IQ-style numbers and reported that, in the music condition, the students scored eight to nine points above their results after relaxation or silence. That is the entire finding. But it came with limits that were printed on the page from the very beginning, and those limits matter more than the headline number.

First, no baby took part. Every participant was a college student, an adult. Second, nobody's general intelligence was measured. The test covered one narrow slice of cognition — spatial-temporal reasoning, the ability to picture objects moving and changing shape in your mind. Third, the authors stated plainly that the boost was temporary. It did not last beyond the ten to fifteen minutes in which the students were tested. The paper's own final paragraph carried that warning.

Rauscher herself spent the following years trying to put the genie back in the bottle. She stressed repeatedly that her work showed no enhancement of general intelligence, that the effect was limited to spatial-temporal reasoning, and that casual listening was a very different thing from music lessons. "There is no compelling evidence that children who listen to classical music are going to have any improvement in cognitive abilities," she later said. "But learning to play a musical instrument does." That distinction — between hearing music and learning music — turns out to be the most important sentence in this whole story, and it is the one the Mozart effect industry worked hardest to ignore.

## What Happened Next: The Replication Problem

A single study of 36 people is a starting point, not a conclusion. Science works by asking whether other laboratories, with other people, get the same result. For the Mozart effect, that process began almost immediately, and it did not go well for the myth.

In 1999, the journal *Nature* published a meta-analysis by the psychologist Christopher Chabris, which pooled the results of 16 studies. Combining 20 comparisons between Mozart and silence, covering 714 people, he found an average enhancement of 0.09 standard deviations. In plain terms, that works out at about 1.4 IQ points. Even on the spatial tasks where the effect should have been strongest, the figure was 0.14 standard deviations, or roughly 2.1 IQ points. The combined result was not statistically significant. Chabris concluded that any enhancement was small, did not reflect a change in general intelligence, and was consistent with a short-lived change in mood and alertness rather than anything specific to Mozart.

That same year, Kenneth Steele and colleagues ran replications at three sites in the United States and Canada. Across their studies, the sonata had no effect on performance at all. Earlier attempts to replicate the finding in 1994, 1995, and 1997, using other spatial tests, had also failed to show significant score enhancement. The pattern was becoming clear: the original result was fragile, and when independent researchers looked for it, they mostly did not find it.

By 2010, the question had been settled as far as the research literature was concerned. A meta-analysis by Jakob Pietschnig, Martin Voracek, and Anton Formann pooled roughly 40 studies with more than 3,000 participants. It found no specific Mozart effect. Whatever small gains appeared showed up with other stimuli too, and there were signs of publication bias — studies from the original laboratory showed larger effects than independent replications, exactly the pattern you expect when a field is chasing a famous result. A 2023 "multiverse" re-analysis by Oberleiter and Pietschnig, which re-ran the analysis under many different reasonable assumptions, reached the same conclusion: trivial-to-null effects, with the myth kept alive by underpowered studies and citation-driven false credibility.

It is worth pausing on what this means. The meta-analyses did not prove that music can never help anyone think better. They proved something narrower and more useful: there is nothing special about Mozart, nothing lasting about the boost, and nothing in the evidence that supports playing classical music to a baby as a way of raising their intelligence. The small, temporary improvement that sometimes appears after listening to music you enjoy is real, but it is not a Mozart effect. It is a mood effect, and it works just as well with music you actually like.

## How College Students Became Babies

The most remarkable part of the Mozart effect story is not the science. It is the transformation. Somewhere between 1993 and the end of the decade, the participants in the study changed — in the public telling — from college students into infants. Nobody announced the change. It happened one headline at a time.

Researchers Adrian Bangerter and Chip Heath tracked the evolution of the legend in a 2004 study published in the *British Journal of Social Psychology*. They found that from 1997 onwards, more news articles mentioned infants than college students when reporting on the Mozart effect, even though the effect had never been tested on infants. By the year 2000, only 30 percent of media coverage even mentioned that the participants in the original study were adults. The other 70 percent simply left that detail out, and a reader could be forgiven for assuming the study had been done on children.

This is how myths are built. No single report lied. Each one just dropped a qualifier — the sample size, the age of the participants, the fifteen-minute limit, the fact that only one kind of task was tested — until what remained was a simple, marketable story: Mozart makes you smarter. And once the story was about babies rather than undergraduates, it became something parents could act on. You cannot buy a better brain for your child, but you can buy a CD, and the market understood that instantly.

## The Industry: Georgia, Baby Einstein, and a Trademarked Phrase

The commercial version of the Mozart effect did not grow slowly. It exploded. In 1997, the music teacher and author Don Campbell published *The Mozart Effect: Tapping the Power of Music to Heal the Body, Strengthen the Mind, and Unlock the Creative Spirit*. Campbell had trademarked the phrase "Mozart effect" itself, and his book went far beyond anything Rauscher had claimed, recommending specially selected classical music for infants in the expectation that it would benefit their mental development. He would go on to publish a follow-up, *The Mozart Effect for Children*, and a range of related CDs and products.

The same year Campbell's book appeared, a husband-and-wife team in Georgia, William Clark and Julie Aigner Clark, shot a home video in their basement. From that humble beginning they launched a line of CDs and videos with names like "Baby Mozart," promoting the notion that babies listening to classical music — even in utero — could increase their intelligence. The brand, Baby Einstein, was sold to the Walt Disney Company in 2001, by which time sales had reached more than seventeen million dollars.

The idea had by then reached government. In January 1998, Zell Miller, the governor of Georgia, proposed a state budget that included 105,000 dollars a year to provide every child born in Georgia with a tape or CD of classical music. Miller played legislators an excerpt from Beethoven's "Ode to Joy" before the vote, asking them if they felt smarter for having listened to it — "smart enough to vote for this budget item." The only problem, apart from the science, was that "Ode to Joy" was composed by Beethoven, not Mozart. Miller had focused his bill on "spatial, temporal reasoning," clearly influenced by the 1993 study, but he had got the composer wrong. The state legislature of Florida went further, mandating that classical music be played daily in all childcare and early education programmes that received state funding.

It is easy to laugh at this now, but it deserves a moment of sympathy. The politicians and the parents were not stupid. They were responding to a story that had been told to them by journalists, authors, and marketers as though it were settled science. A governor who has been told that music raises intelligence, and who can buy that music for every newborn in his state for a hundred thousand dollars, is not being irrational. He is being misled. The fault lies with the chain of retelling that turned a fifteen-minute effect on a paper-folding task into a public policy.

## The Arousal-Mood Explanation

If Mozart does not make you smarter, why did the students in the original study do better after listening to it? The best answer science has produced is disappointingly ordinary: they were in a better mood, and more alert, than they were after sitting in silence or listening to a relaxation tape designed to lower their blood pressure.

This is the arousal-mood hypothesis, developed most clearly in the work of the psychologist Glenn Schellenberg. His research showed that people got the same small, temporary boost from any music they genuinely enjoyed — and that people given an engaging story to listen to instead of music showed a similar bump. The effect was never about Mozart. It was about being pleasantly stimulated. A favourite pop song, an exciting podcast, or a good story could do the same job, because the mechanism was alertness, not the composer.

That explanation also fits the failure of the replications. If the boost depends on mood and enjoyment, then it will vary wildly from person to person and from laboratory to laboratory. People who love Mozart will perk up; people who find classical music boring will not. A study that happens to recruit Mozart fans will find an effect. A study that does not, will not. That is exactly the pattern the meta-analyses found — and it is the opposite of what you would expect from a genuine, specific effect of one composer's music on the human brain.

None of this means music is useless for thinking. It means the opposite, in a sense. Music is a powerful tool for changing how we feel, and how we feel changes how we think. A piece of music you love can lift your mood, sharpen your attention, and make a boring task easier for a few minutes. That is a real and valuable effect. It is just not an intelligence effect, and it is not permanent, and it is not something you can bank for your baby by leaving a CD playing in the background while they sleep.

## What About Music Lessons?

There is one version of the music-and-intelligence story that survives contact with the evidence — but it is not the version parents were sold. The surviving version is about learning an instrument, not listening to one.

Rauscher and Shaw themselves later published a study in which children who received musical training — actually learning to play the piano — improved in their ability to move shapes around in space. The children were not infants, and they were not passively listening. They were practising, week after week, with a teacher. Schellenberg's research points in the same direction: he has found small IQ advantages associated with children who took music lessons, and he has been notably careful in his writing not to oversell what he found. The effect is small, and part of it may reflect the kind of families who sign their children up for lessons, but it is a real, replicable finding — unlike the passive-listening claim.

Neuroscience research, including work from Northwestern University, adds a further distinction. Passive listening — background Mozart, playlists, CDs left playing in a nursery — produces minimal biological change in a developing brain. Active, engaged musical experience is different: singing with a baby, clapping along to a rhythm, letting a toddler bang on a drum or pluck at a keyboard. Those experiences genuinely support a baby's developing brain, because they involve attention, movement, social interaction, and prediction — all the things a growing brain needs. The benefit comes from the engagement, not from the composer. A baby clapping along to a nursery rhyme is doing more for their brain than a baby passively hearing a Mozart sonata ever will.

## What Music Can Actually Do for Babies

None of this means you should throw away the Mozart CDs. Music is genuinely good for babies — just not in the way the myth promised. Singing to a baby, dancing with them, letting them hear rhythms and melodies, and talking to them while music plays are all valuable parts of early development. Music helps babies learn the rhythms of language, it soothes them, it gives parents and children something joyful to share, and shared joy is one of the most powerful ingredients in early brain development. None of that requires Mozart specifically, and none of it requires believing a false claim about IQ.

The honest advice is simpler, and cheaper, than the myth. If you love Mozart, play Mozart, and enjoy it with your child. If you love something else, play that instead. Sing, clap, dance, and make music together, because the interaction is what matters. Do not buy a product because it promises to raise your baby's intelligence — no recording can do that, and the companies that implied otherwise were selling a story, not a result. And if you are choosing between a passive playlist and an active music class, the evidence says the class is worth far more than the playlist, even though it costs more and demands more of your time. The things that genuinely help a child's brain are almost always the ones that ask something of the child, not the ones that happen to them while they sleep.

The Mozart effect also belongs to a larger family of brain myths that sound scientific because they contain a grain of truth. The same pattern — a real finding, stretched past its limits, then sold back to the public — appears in the claim that we use only a tiny fraction of our brains, in the idea that people are either "left-brained" or "right-brained," and in the story that Einstein failed mathematics at school. In each case, as in this one, the myth survived because it told people something they wanted to believe, and because the correction — that the brain is more complicated, and less easily hacked, than the slogan suggests — is less exciting than the promise.

## The Verdict

Does listening to Mozart make babies smarter? No. The claim is **False**. The study that started it all tested 36 college students, not babies, and found only a small, temporary improvement on one spatial reasoning task that faded within ten to fifteen minutes. The researchers themselves said it had nothing to do with general intelligence. Meta-analyses pooling 16 studies and 714 people in 1999, and roughly 40 studies and more than 3,000 people in 2010, found no reliable evidence that passive listening to Mozart raises intelligence in any age group — and no study has ever shown that playing Mozart to a baby produces lasting intelligence gains. What the evidence does support is something else entirely: that enjoying music can briefly lift mood and alertness, and that actively learning music — lessons, practice, singing together — is associated with small, real cognitive benefits. A Mozart CD in the nursery is a pleasant thing. It is not a shortcut to genius, and the thirty-year industry built on pretending otherwise is the clearest proof of how far a small finding can travel once it leaves the laboratory.
