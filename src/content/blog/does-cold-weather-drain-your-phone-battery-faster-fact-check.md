You know the scene. You step out of a warm house on a freezing morning with your phone at 60 percent, and by the time you reach the bus stop it reads 31 percent. An hour later, in the middle of a call, the screen goes black at 22 percent — a phone that was fine indoors has apparently given up. Was the battery dying, or was something else going on?

Millions of people have lived some version of this moment, and winter after winter the same question circulates online: does cold weather actually drain your phone battery faster, or is it a trick of the display? The answer is that the claim is true — but the reason is stranger and more reassuring than most people assume. Your phone is not burning through its stored energy in the cold. Much of that energy is still inside the battery, temporarily locked away by chemistry that has gone sluggish. The battery meter, meanwhile, is doing its best to guess what is happening inside a cell that no longer behaves the way it does at room temperature.

This is a fact-check of a claim that sits at the intersection of everyday experience and electrochemistry, and the evidence for it is unusually strong. It comes not from anecdotes but from laboratory discharge data, from the official guidance of the world's largest phone maker, and from a 2025 report by the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory on how lithium-ion batteries behave in cold-weather vehicles. The same evidence also draws a sharp line that most winter advice misses: using a cold phone is mostly harmless, while charging a freezing phone can damage it permanently.

## The Claim: Winter Eats Your Battery

The claim, in its most common form, is simple: phones lose charge faster in cold weather. People report percentages falling twice as fast outdoors, phones shutting down with a quarter of their charge apparently remaining, and batteries that seem to recover some of their charge after coming back indoors. Skiers, delivery riders, photographers, and anyone who has waited for a bus in January will recognise the pattern.

There is a second, quieter version of the claim that circulates alongside it: that the cold damages the battery for good. That version is worth separating out, because the evidence treats the two very differently. The fast drain is real and mostly temporary. Permanent damage is real too — but it comes from a specific mistake, not from merely using your phone outside.

To settle the claim, it helps to understand what a phone battery actually is. Every modern smartphone runs on a lithium-ion cell, a device that stores energy as a chemical difference between two electrodes and releases it by shuttling lithium ions through a liquid electrolyte. That shuttling is a chemical reaction, and like almost all chemical reactions, it is temperature-sensitive. Warm it up and the ions move freely. Cool it down and everything slows.

## What Happens Inside the Battery When It Gets Cold

Three things happen inside a lithium-ion cell as the temperature falls, and together they explain nearly every symptom people notice in winter.

First, the electrolyte thickens. The liquid that carries lithium ions between the electrodes becomes more viscous in the cold, so the ions move more slowly. Less ion movement means less current available on demand, which means less power the phone can actually draw — even though the total stored energy has barely changed.

Second, internal resistance climbs. Every real battery resists the flow of its own current to some degree, and cold raises that resistance sharply. When resistance rises, more of the battery's voltage is lost inside the cell itself whenever the phone asks for power. The phone experiences this as a voltage sag: under load, the voltage it receives drops faster and further than it would at room temperature.

Third, the chemical reactions at the surfaces of the electrodes slow down. A 2025 report from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory on lithium-ion batteries in cold-weather vehicles describes exactly this combination — slower ion mobility, higher internal resistance, and lower available power and capacity. A phone battery is far smaller than an electric-vehicle pack, but it relies on the same family of electrochemical processes, so the same physics applies.

None of this means the energy has vanished. It means the battery temporarily cannot deliver it. That distinction is the key to the whole claim.

## The Numbers: How Much Capacity Does the Cold Actually Cost?

Laboratory data puts hard numbers on the effect, and they are dramatic. Battery University, the long-running reference site on rechargeable battery behaviour, publishes discharge data showing that a lithium-ion cell that delivers 100 percent of its rated capacity at 27°C (80°F) will typically deliver only about 50 percent of that capacity at −18°C (0°F). Half the usable capacity, gone — not because the energy was spent, but because the cold chemistry cannot release it.

The same source stresses that the loss is conditional: the reduced capacity "only applies while the cell is in that condition and will recover" once the battery returns to room temperature. Bring the phone back inside, let the cell warm up, and the ions start moving freely again. The percentage on the screen may even climb back up on its own, without any charging at all. No energy has flowed back into the battery from the room; the chemistry has simply regained access to energy that was there all along.

The effect does not require Arctic conditions to appear. It begins to be noticeable below about 10°C (50°F) and becomes obvious near freezing. Around −5°C (23°F), phones under heavy load may slow down or shut off to protect themselves. At −20°C (−4°F), the apparent battery life can drop by roughly half for as long as the battery stays cold. And the popular image of a phone dying at 30 percent in a ski jacket pocket is consistent with all of it: the meter is calibrated for warm conditions, and in the cold its estimate of remaining charge becomes unreliable.

A real-world test illustrates the gap. During the 2014 polar vortex, Global News ran a simple experiment: an iPhone left outside in extreme cold dropped 14 percent of its charge in just 30 minutes, while an identical phone left indoors dropped only 1 percent. The outdoor phone was not doing fourteen times more work. It was the same phone, in the same state, with a battery whose chemistry had been chilled into sluggishness.

## Why Your Phone Shuts Down at 30 Percent

The sudden shutdown is the most alarming winter symptom, and it follows directly from the chemistry. The battery percentage on your screen is not a fuel gauge measuring liquid in a tank. It is an estimate, calculated by software from the battery's voltage, the current being drawn, the temperature, and the battery's history. At room temperature those measurements map neatly onto remaining charge. In the cold, the relationship breaks down.

When a cold phone is asked to do real work — record video, navigate with GPS, stream music over a weak signal — the current draw is high. High current against high internal resistance produces a sharp voltage sag. If the cell voltage falls below the device's protective cutoff, the phone shuts down to prevent unstable operation, even though chemical energy remains in the battery. The system is protecting itself, not failing. Apple says its performance controls take temperature, state of charge, and battery impedance into account when preventing unexpected shutdowns, which is why an older battery with higher impedance is more likely to die suddenly in the cold than a new one.

This also explains why the effect is worse for a phone doing real work outside than for one sitting idle in a pocket. An idle phone draws little current, so the voltage sag is small and the percentage falls slowly. A phone recording video in freezing air draws a heavy, continuous current against a high-resistance cell, and that is where the sudden drops and shutdowns appear. It is the same reason a phone at 40 percent indoors can die minutes after stepping into freezing weather: the energy was still there, but the cold temporarily blocked the chemistry from delivering it.

Low charge makes everything worse. A battery that is already nearly empty has less voltage headroom to absorb a cold-weather sag, so the combination of cold and low charge is the most likely to trigger a shutdown. That is one reason manufacturers' guidance converges on the same practical advice: keep the phone warm, and keep it charged, in winter.

## The One Winter Habit That Causes Permanent Damage

So far, the story has been reassuring: the cold steals usable capacity, but gives it back. There is, however, one winter habit that does lasting harm, and it is the one almost nobody knows about. It is not using a cold phone. It is charging one.

When you charge a lithium-ion cell below freezing, the lithium ions cannot insert themselves smoothly into the anode, the electrode that receives them during charging. Instead, metallic lithium deposits on the anode's surface, in a process called lithium plating. Battery University's charging guidance is blunt about it: "no charging is permitted at freezing temperatures because of the reduced diffusion rates on the anode," and "plating of metallic lithium occurs on the anode during a sub-freezing charge that leads to a permanent degradation in performance and safety." Batteries with lithium plating are also more vulnerable to failure if exposed to vibration or other stressful conditions.

The safe windows are well documented. Battery University puts the best results for charging at 10°C to 30°C (50°F to 86°F), allows fast charging between 5°C and 45°C (41°F to 113°F), and draws the line at zero: below 0°C, charging should stop. Apple takes the same position in plain language, warning that if an iOS or iPadOS device gets too cold, it "might not charge or could stop charging."

Many modern phones protect themselves here. Apple, Samsung, and other manufacturers build in charging restrictions that pause or block charging when the battery itself — not just the surrounding air — reads below a documented low-temperature threshold, resuming automatically once the cell warms. That is a safety behaviour, not a defect. But it is not universal, and it is not a reason to test it. A phone left overnight in a freezing car and plugged in first thing in the morning is exactly the scenario the guidance warns against. The safe move is simple: let a cold phone warm up to room temperature before plugging it in.

## Cold Versus Heat: Which Is the Real Battery Killer?

The comparison with heat puts the cold in perspective. Heat is the genuine long-term enemy of a lithium-ion battery. Apple warns that ambient temperatures above 35°C (95°F) "can permanently damage battery capacity," and its guidance places the ideal comfort zone for its devices at 16°C to 22°C (61°F to 72°F). A phone baked on a car dashboard in summer loses capacity it will never get back, because heat accelerates the chemical degradation of the cell's materials.

Cold, by contrast, is mostly a temporary inconvenience — with the single exception of sub-freezing charging. That asymmetry is why battery experts summarise the seasonal risks in one line: cold weather causes temporary capacity loss, while heat causes permanent capacity loss. It is also why a phone that died in the snow in January will usually be perfectly fine in April, while a phone that spent a summer afternoon on a dashboard may never hold a charge quite the same way again.

This is worth remembering because it changes what winter battery care should look like. The goal is not to protect the battery from the cold in the sense of preventing damage — ordinary cold use does not damage it. The goal is to keep the battery warm enough to deliver the energy it already holds, and to avoid charging it while it is still freezing.

## What the Manufacturers Actually Say

Apple's official battery guidance is the clearest statement from any phone maker, and it confirms the claim directly. iPhones and iPads are designed for use where the ambient temperature is between 0°C and 35°C (32°F to 95°F), with storage between −20°C and 45°C (−4°F to 113°F). Apple says that using a device in very cold conditions "might temporarily shorten battery life" and could cause the device to turn off, adding that "battery life will return to normal when you bring your device back to higher ambient temperatures." It also notes that a device that gets too cold "might not charge or could stop charging."

Samsung publishes the same 0°C to 35°C operating range for its Galaxy phones. The convergence is no coincidence: it reflects the shared chemistry of lithium-ion cells rather than any brand-specific design choice. Every manufacturer of a lithium-ion-powered phone is managing the same temperature-sensitive reaction, and every one of them draws the same lines.

That manufacturer consensus matters for this fact-check because it rules out the most common sceptical explanation — that winter battery drain is a software trick, a planned-obsolescence scheme, or a quirk of one brand. It is not. It is physics that every maker acknowledges, documents, and designs around, and it affects every phone with a lithium-ion battery, from budget handsets to flagships.

## How to Protect Your Phone in Winter

The evidence points to a short list of practical steps, none of which requires buying anything. Keep the phone in an inside pocket, close to your body, rather than in an outer pocket or a bag, so your own warmth keeps the battery near its comfort zone. Avoid leaving it in a parked car in winter, where the battery can fall well below freezing. If you need to use it heavily outdoors — navigation, video, a long call — accept that the percentage will fall faster than usual and plan for it, rather than assuming the battery is failing.

Most importantly, do not charge a freezing phone. If it has spent hours in the cold, let it warm up indoors first. The same applies to wireless chargers and car chargers on a cold morning: the phone's own protections may refuse the charge, and that refusal is doing you a favour. There is also a simpler, less technical reason the winter drain feels so dramatic: phones are used differently in the cold. Screens get brighter against snow glare, cameras work harder, navigation runs longer, and gloves come off for a quick photo that turns into ten. All of that draws current, and current drawn from a cold, high-resistance battery is exactly what produces the steepest percentage drops. The cold is the main cause, but the way we use our phones in winter is a supporting actor.

## Why This Myth Persists — and Why It Is Not a Myth

It is worth asking why a claim with such strong evidence behind it still gets questioned every winter. Part of the answer is that the most visible symptom — the battery percentage bouncing back up after the phone warms — looks like a software glitch. A fuel gauge that goes up without charging feels broken, so people assume the phone is lying to them rather than reporting a real chemical effect. The other part is that the language around batteries is confusing: "drain," "capacity," "charge," and "damage" get used interchangeably, and the claim is true for some of those meanings and false for others.

That confusion is exactly why the verdict here needs its nuance stated plainly. If "drain your battery faster" means the phone's usable charge falls more quickly in the cold, the claim is true, and the evidence is overwhelming. If it means the cold permanently consumes or destroys the battery's stored energy, that version is false — the energy is still there, and it comes back when the battery warms. Both statements are about the same phone on the same freezing day, and both are correct once the words are defined carefully.

The distinction also explains the pattern of anecdotes. The skier whose phone died at 30 percent and worked again indoors is not imagining things, and neither is the commuter whose phone lost 14 percent in half an hour outside. The laboratory data, the manufacturer guidance, and the field reports all point the same way, which is rare for a viral tech claim. Usually, when a claim is this widely repeated, the evidence is mixed. Here it is not.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: True.**

Does cold weather drain your phone battery faster? Yes. Laboratory data from Battery University shows a lithium-ion cell delivering only about 50 percent of its capacity at −18°C compared with full capacity at 27°C. Apple confirms that very cold conditions can temporarily shorten battery life and switch a device off, with performance returning once the device warms. A 2025 National Renewable Energy Laboratory report describes the same mechanism in cold-weather vehicle batteries: slower ion movement, higher internal resistance, and lower available power. And a real-world test during the 2014 polar vortex found an iPhone outdoors losing 14 percent in 30 minutes against 1 percent for the same model indoors.

The crucial nuance is that the drain is mostly temporary. The cold does not consume the stored energy; it temporarily prevents the battery from delivering it, and the capacity returns when the cell warms. The one genuine danger is charging a battery below freezing, which can cause lithium plating — permanent damage that no amount of warming will undo. So keep your phone warm, keep it charged, and never plug in a freezing phone. Winter is not killing your battery. It is just making it sluggish — and sluggish is fixable with a warm pocket and a little patience.
