It is the kind of tip that sounds too elegant to be wrong. No gym membership, no diet plan, no willpower — just drink your water ice cold, and your body will burn calories warming it up. The claim has floated around fitness forums, weight-loss listicles, and social media for two decades, usually attached to an impressive number: drink two litres of cold water a day and burn an extra 100 calories, every day, for free. Over a year, the arithmetic goes, that is several kilograms of fat, melted away by nothing more than a cold glass.

The physics behind it is real. Your body holds itself near 37 degrees Celsius, and a glass of water straight from the fridge arrives at about 4 degrees. Something has to close that gap, and closing it costs energy. The question is how much — and whether the famous study that launched this claim actually measured what people think it measured. When researchers went back and checked, the answer turned out to be far less exciting than the headline, and the story of how a 30 percent metabolic boost shrank to almost nothing is a small masterclass in why one study is never enough.

This is a fact-check of the ice water claim: the physics, the original 2003 experiment, the replications that failed to reproduce it, and the honest accounting of what a cold glass actually costs your body.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, goes like this: drinking ice water burns calories because your body must expend energy heating the water to body temperature, and if you drink enough of it, the effect adds up to real weight loss. Some versions put a number on it — 100 extra calories a day from two litres of cold water, or about seven pounds of fat a year. Others are vaguer, promising that ice water "boosts your metabolism" or "fires up calorie burning," as if a cold drink were a small furnace you could stoke all day.

It is easy to see why the idea spread. It has the shape of science — a mechanism anyone can follow, numbers that feel precise, and a study to cite. It also has the shape of a wish: weight loss without effort is the oldest promise in the wellness industry, and this version asks only that you add ice to something you were going to drink anyway.

But a claim with a real mechanism is not the same as a claim with a meaningful effect. Your body does spend energy warming cold water. The question is whether that energy amounts to anything next to the thousands of calories you burn in a day — and whether the study everyone quotes actually proves what the listicles say it proves.

## The Physics: Real, Tiny, and Capped

Start with the physics, because it sets a hard ceiling that no study can exceed. Heating water takes a known amount of energy: one small calorie raises one gram of water by one degree Celsius, and a food Calorie — the kind printed on nutrition labels — is a thousand of those.

An ordinary glass of water is about 237 grams. Warming it from fridge temperature, around 4 degrees, up to body temperature, 37 degrees, means covering roughly 33 degrees. Multiply 237 grams by 33 degrees and you get about 7,800 small calories, or roughly 8 food Calories. That is the entire energy cost of one glass of ice water, if every bit of the warming is paid for by burning extra fuel. For half a litre, the theoretical cost is about 16 to 17 Calories. For two litres a day — eight full glasses of ice water — the ceiling is roughly 68 Calories, and that assumes the water is near freezing and your body pays the full price.

There is even a catch inside that ceiling. Your body is constantly shedding heat to the room around you. A cold drink can simply reduce how much heat you lose to the air for a while, or let your core temperature dip by a fraction of a degree, rather than triggering any extra burning at all. The physics tells you the maximum possible effect; the physiology decides how much of it actually happens. As one careful review of the evidence put it, the honest estimate for a large glass of ice-cold water is somewhere around 5 to 10 extra calories — and you would need to drink an unrealistic amount of ice water to burn even a fraction of what a short walk accomplishes.

To put 8 calories in perspective: it is, in the words of nutrition researcher Rachele Pojednic of Simmons College and the Harvard Extension School, "equivalent to one plain M&M." A single bite of toast wipes out several glasses' worth. The mechanism is genuine. The magnitude is not.

## The 2003 Study That Started It All

The claim's authority comes from a single experiment. In 2003, a German research team led by Michael Boschmann published a study in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism. They had 14 healthy adults drink 500 millilitres of water and measured what happened to their metabolic rate. The result was startling: metabolic rate rose by about 30 percent, kicked in within ten minutes, peaked around thirty to forty minutes later, and stayed elevated for over an hour. The researchers estimated the total extra energy burned at roughly 100 kilojoules, about 24 calories, and calculated that drinking two litres of water a day could burn an extra 100 calories daily.

Crucially, only about 40 percent of that thermogenic effect was attributed to warming the water from room temperature up to body temperature. The rest, the researchers argued, came from somewhere else — the sympathetic nervous system, the body's fight-or-flight wiring. When they blocked beta-adrenoreceptors, the response was blunted, which suggested the water itself was triggering a nervous-system response, not just a heating bill.

It was a striking finding, and it travelled fast. A 30 percent metabolic boost from drinking water is the kind of number that writes its own headlines. The problem was that it was also the kind of number that made other researchers suspicious. As Pojednic later recalled, "lots of researchers saw this study and said, 'Wait, there have been other studies looking at the thermogenesis of food, and those studies didn't show anything or just a tiny amount [of calorie burn].'" The results did not fit with what physiology said should happen. So other teams tried to reproduce them.

## The Replications That Found Almost Nothing

Three years later, a team led by Brown, Dulloo, and Montani published a direct reassessment in the same journal. They tested the mechanism specifically, comparing distilled water against saline, and found that energy expenditure did not significantly increase after drinking either one at room temperature. When they cooled the water to 3 degrees Celsius, they did observe a small bump — about a 4.5 percent rise in energy expenditure over sixty minutes — but that was well below the theoretical energy cost of warming the water to body temperature. Their conclusion was blunt: the results cast doubt on water as a thermogenic agent.

Later work landed in a similar place. Charrière and colleagues gave 27 adults 500 millilitres of room-temperature water and measured resting energy expenditure rising by less than 3 percent above baseline — a change so small it was not significantly different from sham drinking, going through the motions of drinking without actually swallowing water. If pretending to drink produces nearly the same result as drinking, the water is not doing much.

The one population where the effect looked larger was children with excess weight. Dubnov-Raz and colleagues gave 21 overweight children 10 millilitres per kilogram of body weight of 4-degree water and measured resting energy expenditure rising by as much as 25 percent above baseline, peaking near an hour after drinking. It is a genuinely interesting result, but it is a small study, in children, whose metabolisms and body composition differ from adults — and it has not become the basis for a general weight-loss recommendation.

Taken together, the replication record tells a consistent story: the original 30 percent figure likely overstated the effect, possibly because of methodological issues the later teams tried to correct. The careful summary from the reviewers is that cold water does cost the body some energy to warm up, but the real-world caloric cost is tiny. MyFitnessPal's review of the evidence reached the same conclusion: the more scientifically sound research found the thermogenic effect of drinking cold water is only about 4 to 7 calories per glass. Dietitian Lauren Harris-Pincus, speaking to SELF, put it plainly: "It's very minimal, not enough to make a significant change in anybody's weight."

## What the Studies Actually Found

| Study | Who and what | What they measured |
|---|---|---|
| Boschmann et al., 2003 (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism) | 14 healthy adults drank 500 mL of water | Metabolic rate up ~30% for 60–90 minutes; ~24 calories total; ~40% of the effect attributed to warming the water |
| Brown, Dulloo & Montani, 2006 (same journal) | Direct reassessment; distilled water vs saline, room temperature and 3°C | No significant rise at room temperature; 3°C water gave ~4.5% rise over 60 minutes, below the theoretical warming cost |
| Charrière et al. | 27 adults drank 500 mL of room-temperature water | Resting energy expenditure rose less than 3% above baseline, not significantly different from sham drinking |
| Dubnov-Raz et al. | 21 overweight children drank 10 mL/kg of 4°C water | Resting energy expenditure rose up to 25% above baseline, peaking near an hour |
| Physics ceiling | 237 g glass warmed from 4°C to 37°C | ~8 food Calories per glass; ~16–17 per 500 mL; ~68 per 2 litres a day at most |
| Expert summary (Pojednic; Harris-Pincus) | Review of the evidence | About 4–7 calories per glass — "equivalent to one plain M&M"; "not enough to make a significant change in anybody's weight" |

The table makes the pattern visible. One early study found a large effect. Every careful attempt to repeat it found a small one, or none at all. And the physics sets a ceiling that the large effect was already straining against: the 2003 study's 24 calories from half a litre of room-temperature water exceeded what warming the water alone could explain, which is why the researchers invoked a nervous-system mechanism — the very mechanism the later studies could not reproduce.

## The Benefits That Are Real

None of this means drinking water is useless for weight management. It means the temperature is not the active ingredient. The genuine benefits of water have nothing to do with ice.

A European Journal of Nutrition study found that drinking 500 millilitres of water at 2 degrees Celsius suppressed stomach contractions and reduced how much food participants ate afterward, compared with the same volume of warm or hot water. Very cold water, in other words, may help you eat a little less at the next meal — not by burning calories, but by quieting the stomach. And a randomized controlled trial with adults who had obesity found that people who drank 500 millilitres of water 30 minutes before their main meals lost about 1.3 kilograms more over 12 weeks than a control group. The mechanism there is satiety: water before a meal leaves less room for food.

That is the same logic behind our earlier check of <a href="/blog/does-drinking-water-before-meals-help-you-lose-weight-fact-check/">does drinking water before meals help you lose weight</a>, which found a modest extra loss of about one to two kilograms over twelve weeks in two small trials — a real effect, but only as a helper alongside eating less, not a standalone fix. Water helps weight loss mainly by replacing something else. Swap a sugary drink for water, at any temperature, and you save the calories that drink contained. That saving is hundreds of calories a day, not eight. As one metabolic health specialist quoted in the reporting put it, "the real benefit comes from replacing sugary drinks with water — regardless of temperature."

There is also the question of how much water you need in the first place. Our check of <a href="/blog/eight-glasses-water-rule-fact-check/">Do You Really Need 8 Glasses of Water a Day?</a> found that scientists cannot find where the famous "8x8" rule came from, and that kidney experts recommend drinking to thirst rather than chasing a number. The ice-water claim quietly depends on that number: to burn even 60 to 70 extra calories, you would need to drink eight full glasses of ice water every day, on top of whatever else you drink. Most people do not, and the payoff for doing so is a rounding error.

## Cold Water vs. Hot Water: Does Temperature Matter at All?

If ice water burns a few calories and hot water burns none, is cold at least better than hot? Strictly speaking, yes — by a handful of calories a day. Hot water arrives at or above body temperature, so there is no warming cost at all. Cold water has a slight metabolic edge, but the effect is far smaller than most people expect, and the difference between hot and cold probably amounts to fewer calories than a single bite of toast.

There are also reasons to prefer water at whatever temperature you will actually drink. Some people find ice water refreshing and drink more of it; others find it uncomfortable, especially with sensitive teeth, and drink less. A few people report that very cold water triggers headaches or, in rare cases, worsens migraines. None of that changes the calorie arithmetic, but it does change the only thing that matters for hydration: whether you drink enough, consistently, day after day.

The temperature debate is also a reminder of how wellness claims borrow the language of science while dropping its sense of scale. "Boosts metabolism by 30 percent" sounds like a transformation. "Burns about as many calories as one plain M&M per glass" sounds like what it is. Both describe the same body of research. Only one of them survives contact with the replications.

It is worth noting that the ice-water claim often travels with other morning-ritual claims, and those do not fare much better. Our check of <a href="/blog/does-lemon-water-detox-your-body-fact-check/">does lemon water detox your body</a> found that no study shows lemon water speeds up the liver or kidneys — the organs already handle that job around the clock. The pattern is the same in each case: a real habit, a real ingredient, and a mechanism that sounds scientific, attached to an effect the evidence does not support.

## What Actually Works Instead

If the goal is weight loss, the ice-water arithmetic points to a short list of things that genuinely move the numbers — and none of them involves a thermometer.

First, replace caloric drinks with water. A can of soda carries around 140 calories; a sweetened coffee drink can carry twice that. Swapping one of those for water, at any temperature, saves more in a day than a month of ice water ever could. This is the single largest, easiest, and best-evidenced water-related weight-loss strategy there is.

Second, drink water before meals if it helps you eat less. The trial evidence — 500 millilitres about half an hour before eating, associated with roughly an extra kilogram of loss over twelve weeks — is modest, but it is real, and it works through satiety rather than thermogenesis. It is also free, and it has no side effects beyond an extra trip to the bathroom.

Third, keep perspective on scale. A short walk burns more than a day of ice water. Cooking one meal at home instead of ordering takeaway saves more than a week of it. The habits that change weight are the ones that move hundreds of calories a day, sustained over months — not the ones that move single digits.

Fourth, drink water at the temperature you enjoy. If ice water makes you drink more, drink ice water. If room-temperature water is what you reach for, drink that. Hydration is the part of this story that actually matters for health, and the best water is the water you will drink.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Drinking ice water does burn calories — the physics is real, and your body really does spend energy warming a cold drink to 37 degrees. But the amount is about 4 to 8 calories per glass, roughly one plain M&M, and the famous 2003 study that launched the claim reported a 30 percent metabolic boost that later, more careful experiments could not reproduce. Reassessments found increases of 4.5 percent or less, and in one careful study the rise was indistinguishable from sham drinking. The claim is true in the trivial sense and false as a weight-loss strategy, which is exactly what "misleading" means.

The story of the ice-water claim is really the story of a single study. A small experiment with 14 people produced a dramatic number, the number travelled the world, and the quieter work of checking it — the replications, the reassessments, the physics — never caught up in the public imagination. It is a useful reminder that a mechanism is not a magnitude, and that "scientists found" is the beginning of a question, not the end of one.

So drink your water cold if you like it cold. Enjoy the chill, enjoy the ritual, enjoy the glass sweating on the table. Just do not count it as exercise. Your body is not a furnace you can stoke with ice. It is a thermostat, and it is very good at its job — which, in this case, means spending as little as possible to deal with a cold drink.
