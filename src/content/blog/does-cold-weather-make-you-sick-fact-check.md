Every winter, the same warning echoes from parents and grandparents around the world: put on a coat, wear a hat, don't go out with wet hair — or you'll catch a cold. It is one of the oldest pieces of health advice in circulation, repeated so often that most of us never stop to ask whether it is actually true. The timing certainly looks convincing. Colds and flu surge every winter, emergency rooms fill up, and whole offices seem to fall ill in the same week the temperature drops. But is the cold itself making us sick, or is something else going on?

The answer turns out to be more interesting than a simple yes or no. Cold weather cannot give you a cold. No amount of standing in the snow, forgetting your scarf, or walking home with damp hair can conjure a virus out of thin air. And yet the folk wisdom is not entirely wrong either. Over the last twenty years, researchers have found that cold air changes the battlefield inside your nose in ways that genuinely help viruses win — and winter changes human behaviour in ways that hand those viruses more opportunities. The claim, as it is usually stated, is misleading. The truth is subtler, and it matters, because understanding what actually makes you sick in winter changes what you should do about it.

## The Claim: Cold Air Gives You a Cold

The belief is ancient and nearly universal. In many languages, the common cold is literally named after temperature: the English word "cold" doubles as the name of the illness, the German word is "Erkältung" (a chilling), and similar links exist in French, Spanish, Russian, and Chinese. The logic feels like common sense. You go out underdressed, you shiver, and a day or two later your throat is sore and your nose is running. Cause and effect seem obvious.

The claim has also been remarkably persistent in the face of modern medicine. Ask a room full of adults whether going outside without a coat can give you a cold, and a large share will still say yes. The idea survives because it is half-observed: winter really is cold season. In the United States, flu activity usually peaks between December and February, and the average adult catches two to three colds a year, mostly in the colder months. Children catch even more. If the calendar lines up that neatly, why wouldn't the cold be the cause?

But correlation is not causation, and this is one of the classic cases where the two part ways. To settle the question, scientists had to do something the folk wisdom never did: separate the cold from the virus, and test each one on its own.

## What Actually Causes a Cold

A cold is an infection, not a reaction to temperature. According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 200 different respiratory viruses can cause a cold. Rhinoviruses are the most frequent culprits in the United States, but the list also includes common human coronaviruses, parainfluenza viruses, adenoviruses, enteroviruses, and human metapneumovirus. Influenza viruses, the virus that causes COVID-19, and respiratory syncytial virus can all produce cold-like symptoms, but strictly speaking they do not cause "a cold" — they cause their own illnesses.

That single fact decides the central question. A virus has to enter your body for a cold to happen. Cold air contains no viruses. A drop in temperature cannot manufacture a rhinovirus, and no chill, however severe, can create an infection out of nothing. Without exposure to a virus, there is no cold, however cold it gets outside. The illnesses that cold itself directly causes are different ones: hypothermia and frostbite, which have clear temperature thresholds and nothing to do with a runny nose.

So if the cold doesn't cause colds, why does the pattern look so convincing? The answer comes in three parts, and each one has been tested in the laboratory and in human volunteers.

## The Experiment That Tested the Folk Wisdom

The most direct test of the old saying came from the Common Cold Centre at Cardiff University in Wales. In a trial published in the journal *Family Practice* in 2005, researchers Johnson and Eccles randomised 180 healthy volunteers into two groups. One group had their feet chilled in cold water; the other went through a control procedure. The volunteers then scored their cold symptoms twice a day for four to five days.

The result was genuinely surprising, and it is still quoted today because it refuses to fit neatly into either side of the argument. Chilling produced no immediate change in symptoms — nobody started sneezing on cue. But over the following days, 13 of the 90 chilled volunteers reported a cold, compared with only 5 of the 90 controls. The difference was statistically significant, and the chilled group's average symptom scores were higher too: 5.16 against 2.89. The authors concluded that acute chilling of the feet causes cold symptoms in around 10 percent of people chilled.

Crucially, the study did not show that cold water infected anyone. The researchers' interpretation was that chilling constricts blood vessels in the nose, dampening local defences and allowing a virus that was already present — a dormant or incubating infection — to tip over into a symptomatic cold. The cold didn't plant the virus. It may have woken one up.

An earlier review from the same centre, published in 2002, had summed up a series of trials in which volunteers had cold viruses deliberately put into their noses and were then exposed to cold. Those trials, the review concluded, "failed to demonstrate any effect of cold exposure on susceptibility to infection with common cold viruses." Put the two findings together and the picture is consistent: cold exposure does not make a healthy, virus-free person catch a cold, but chilling the body may help an infection that is already underway to break through.

That distinction — between causing an infection and helping one along — is the hinge this whole claim turns on. And it is exactly where the newest research comes in.

## Why Your Nose Is the Weak Link

Your nose is the front door for the common cold, and it is also the coldest part of your airway. That turns out to matter a great deal, because the common cold virus is picky about temperature.

Scientists have known since the 1960s that most rhinovirus strains replicate better at the cool temperatures of the nasal cavity — around 33 to 35 degrees Celsius (91 to 95 degrees Fahrenheit) — than at the body's core temperature of 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 degrees Fahrenheit). For decades, nobody knew why. The virus itself seemed to simply prefer the cold. Then a team at Yale University found the real explanation, and it was not about the virus at all. It was about us.

In a study published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in 2015, Ellen Foxman, Akiko Iwasaki, and their colleagues infected mouse airway cells with rhinovirus and incubated them at 33 degrees and at 37 degrees. The cells kept at the cooler, nose-like temperature mounted a strikingly weaker antiviral defence. They produced less interferon — the signalling molecules that rally the immune response — and switched on fewer antiviral defence genes. When the researchers disabled key parts of that defence system, the temperature advantage disappeared: cells with genetic deficiencies in the RIG-I-like receptor pathway or the type I interferon receptor supported much higher levels of viral replication even at 37 degrees.

"We found that the innate immune response to the rhinovirus is impaired at the lower body temperature compared to the core body temperature," Iwasaki said at the time. In plain terms, the cold doesn't make the virus stronger. It makes your nose's defences weaker, by cooling the very tissue where the virus lands first. The researchers were careful about the limits of their work — it was done in mouse cells in a dish, not in people walking around in winter — but the mechanism was real, measurable, and large.

So the folk wisdom contains a grain of truth after all, just not the one people think. Going out in the cold doesn't give you a virus. But breathing cold air cools the inside of your nose by several degrees, and a cooler nose is a worse defender.

## The 2022 Discovery: Your Nose's Tiny Defenders

If the Yale study explained why a cool nose is vulnerable, a study published seven years later found a second, entirely different defence that cold air undermines — and this one was demonstrated in human tissue.

In December 2022, researchers from Mass Eye and Ear, Harvard Medical School, and Northeastern University published a study in the *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology* that identified a previously unknown immune response inside the nose. When nasal cells detect a virus, they release billions of tiny fluid-filled sacs called extracellular vesicles — or EVs — into the mucus. These vesicles swarm invading viruses, bind to them, and deliver antiviral molecules, including a microRNA called miR-17, that suppress viral replication. In laboratory tests, these vesicles cut the replication of three respiratory viruses — a coronavirus and two rhinoviruses — by roughly 38 to 73 percent.

Then the researchers tested what cold does to this system. They took healthy volunteers from a room-temperature environment and exposed them to 4.4 degrees Celsius (about 40 degrees Fahrenheit) for just 15 minutes. The temperature inside their noses fell by about 5 degrees Celsius. When the same temperature drop was applied to nasal tissue samples, the results were dramatic: the quantity of extracellular vesicles secreted by the nasal cells fell by nearly 42 percent, and the antiviral proteins carried by the remaining vesicles were impaired too.

"That little bit of coldness in the tip of the nose was enough to take nearly 42 percent of the extracellular vesicles out of the fight," said Benjamin Bleier, the study's senior author and director of otolaryngology translational research at Mass Eye and Ear. It was the first biological mechanism that could explain, in concrete numbers, why respiratory viruses like the common cold, flu, and COVID-19 spike in colder seasons.

The authors were careful to note the limits: the work was done on cells and tissue samples, not in a trial measuring who actually caught a cold after going outside. It proves that cold weakens a specific nasal defence. It does not prove that a cold walk guarantees a cold. But combined with the Yale findings, the picture is now much clearer than it was a decade ago. Your nose has at least two temperature-sensitive defence systems, and cold air blunts both of them.

## Why Winter Is Still Cold Season

The nose is only part of the story. Winter changes the world around the virus as well, and a 2020 review in the *Annual Review of Virology* by Moriyama, Hugentobler, and Iwasaki grouped the explanations into two families: changes in the environment, and changes in human behaviour. Both favour the virus.

First, the environment. Many respiratory viruses survive longer and remain infectious for longer in cold, dry air. In a classic series of experiments with guinea pigs, influenza transmission was far more efficient under cold, dry conditions than under warm, humid ones. Dry winter air also dries out the mucous membranes lining the nose and airways, and dry mucus moves more slowly, which means trapped viruses are cleared less efficiently. The air itself becomes a better delivery system and a worse cleaning system at the same time.

Second, behaviour. When it gets cold, people go inside — into homes, offices, classrooms, buses, and trains — and stay there, close together, with the windows shut. That is precisely the environment in which respiratory viruses spread best: shared air, poor ventilation, and plenty of hands touching the same doorknobs, keyboards, and handrails. The virus doesn't need the cold to travel. It needs people crowded together, and winter delivers that.

Third, timing. Respiratory viruses have their own seasons, and the cold season is theirs. Flu activity in the United States usually peaks between December and February. Rhinoviruses circulate year-round but surge in autumn and spring. Add the school calendar — children, who catch more colds than adults, packed into classrooms and then bringing viruses home — and the seasonal pattern becomes almost inevitable.

None of this makes cold weather the cause of your cold. But it does make winter the season in which every link in the chain — virus survival, virus spread, and your nose's ability to fight back — tilts in the virus's favour. The folk wisdom saw the pattern correctly and named the wrong culprit.

## What Cold Weather Can Actually Do to You

It is worth being precise about what cold can and cannot do, because the confusion cuts both ways. Cold weather cannot give you a viral infection. But it can genuinely harm you in other ways, and some of those harms look, at first glance, like being "sick from the cold."

Hypothermia and frostbite are the direct injuries of cold: the body's core temperature falling, or skin and tissue freezing. Both have clear temperature and exposure thresholds, and both are medical emergencies, not colds. Cold air can also trigger bronchospasm in people with asthma, and epidemiological studies show that cold conditions can worsen symptoms and increase functional impairment in people with chronic respiratory conditions. That is a real effect — cold air irritating already-sensitive airways — but it is not an infection, and it is not contagious.

## The Studies, Side by Side

The research on this question spans more than twenty years, from human volunteer trials to mouse cells to human nasal tissue. Laid out together, the studies tell a consistent story — and they also show exactly where the evidence stops.

| Study | Year | What was tested | What it found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cardiff review (Eccles) | 2002 | Trials exposing virus-inoculated volunteers to cold | No effect of cold exposure on susceptibility to infection |
| Johnson & Eccles, *Family Practice* | 2005 | 180 volunteers, feet chilled in cold water vs control | 13 of 90 chilled vs 5 of 90 controls reported a cold within 4–5 days; symptom scores 5.16 vs 2.89 |
| Foxman et al., Yale, *PNAS* | 2015 | Mouse airway cells infected with rhinovirus at 33°C vs 37°C | Weaker interferon response at 33°C; rhinovirus replicated better at nasal temperature |
| Huang et al., Mass Eye and Ear / Harvard, *JACI* | 2022 | Human nasal cells and tissue; 15 min at 4.4°C | Nasal temperature fell ~5°C; extracellular vesicle release fell nearly 42%; antiviral function impaired |
| Lowen et al., guinea pig transmission | 2007 | Influenza spread at different temperatures and humidities | Transmission far more efficient in cold, dry air |
| Moriyama et al., *Annual Review of Virology* | 2020 | Review of seasonality mechanisms | Winter favours viruses through environment and human behaviour combined |

The pattern across the table is the verdict in miniature. The human trial that tested the folk claim most directly found no effect on catching a cold from a virus you were given. The trial that chilled people's feet found a small, real effect on symptoms — but only, the authors argued, by letting an infection already present break through. And the laboratory studies found real, measurable ways that cold weakens the nose's defences. Cold is a risk amplifier, not a cause.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Cold weather does not make you sick. Colds are caused by viruses — more than 200 of them, most often rhinoviruses — and no temperature, however low, can create a virus where none exists. That part of the claim is simply false, and it has been tested directly in volunteers who were given cold viruses and then exposed to cold: the cold made no difference to whether they became infected.

But the claim is not pure fiction, which is why it survives. Chilling the body can help an infection you already carry tip into symptoms. Breathing cold air cools the inside of your nose by several degrees, and two separate defence systems in the nose — the interferon response and the extracellular vesicle swarm — both work worse when cold. Winter air also keeps viruses infectious for longer, dries out the mucus that traps them, and drives people indoors into crowded, poorly ventilated rooms where viruses spread easily. So winter genuinely is cold season. It just isn't the cold that does it.

The practical difference matters. If cold itself caused colds, then a warm coat would be a cure. It isn't — though it is still a good idea, since staying warm keeps your nose's defences at full strength and protects you from hypothermia and frostbite. What actually protects you from a cold is boring and unglamorous: washing your hands, not touching your face, keeping rooms ventilated, getting enough sleep, and getting your flu vaccine. Your grandmother was right to worry about winter. She was just wrong about which part of winter to blame.

## What Actually Protects You in Winter

If a coat alone cannot keep a cold away, what can? The measures that work are the ones that break the chain between a virus and your nose.

Hand hygiene comes first, because rhinoviruses survive on surfaces for hours and reach your nose and eyes on your own fingers. Ventilation matters more than most people realise: opening a window, even briefly, dilutes the shared air in a crowded room. Sleep and general health keep your immune system at its best, and the seasonal flu vaccine protects against influenza — the winter virus that causes the most serious illness — even though it does nothing against the common cold.

None of this is as satisfying as a single villain. "Put on a coat or you'll catch a cold" is a tidy story with a visible cause and a visible fix. The real story is messier: an invisible virus, a nose that runs a few degrees cooler than the rest of you, defences that weaken in the cold, dry air that preserves the virus, and a room full of people breathing the same air. But the messy story has one great advantage over the tidy one. It is true — and knowing it means you can aim your precautions at the thing that is actually making you sick.

So this winter, wear the coat. Just don't expect it to do the job of handwashing.
