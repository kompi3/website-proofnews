Every family seems to have a version of the warning. A child laces their fingers together, pulls, and produces a satisfying volley of pops — and an adult in the room immediately delivers the verdict: stop that, or you will get arthritis. The warning is delivered with total confidence, as if it were as settled as gravity. It has been repeated in kitchens, classrooms, and doctors' waiting rooms for generations, and it still circulates online today in listicles of "bad habits that ruin your joints."

It is also, according to the best available evidence, wrong.

This is not a case where the science is murky or the studies point in different directions. On the specific question of whether habitual knuckle cracking causes arthritis, the research has been remarkably consistent for decades: it does not. The evidence includes one of the most patient self-experiments in the history of medicine, a man who cracked the knuckles of one hand and only one hand for more than sixty years, and larger studies that compared hundreds of habitual crackers with people who never crack at all. The crackers' joints looked no worse. In one study, they even looked marginally better — though not by enough to mean anything.

That does not make knuckle cracking a health food. Forceful, aggressive cracking can injure a joint, and pain or swelling that accompanies cracking deserves medical attention. But the claim most people actually believe — that ordinary, painless knuckle cracking gives you arthritis — is false. Here is how we know, and why the myth refuses to die.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, goes like this: cracking your knuckles damages your joints, wears down the cartilage, and eventually leads to arthritis — the painful, stiff, swollen joints that afflict millions of older adults. Some versions add that crackers will end up with enlarged, deformed knuckles, weakened grip, or hands that look decades older than they should.

The logic feels intuitive, and that is a large part of why the myth survives. The sound itself is alarming. A knuckle crack is sharp, sudden, and percussive; it sounds like something breaking. If you did not know what was happening inside the joint, you could be forgiven for assuming that something had snapped, ground, or torn. Add to that a second coincidence: arthritis becomes more common with age, and people who cracked their knuckles as teenagers eventually grow old. When their hands start to ache in their sixties, it is natural — but wrong — to connect the two.

Doctors hear the question constantly. As one hand surgeon put it, "The idea that cracking your knuckles leads to arthritis is one of the most common misconceptions we hear," adding that "Multiple long-term studies have found no clear link between habitual knuckle cracking and the development of osteoarthritis in the hands or fingers." That sentence, from Dr. Kalpit Shah, an orthopedic hand surgeon at Scripps Clinic, summarizes the medical consensus in two lines. The rest of this article is the evidence behind it.

## The Man Who Cracked One Hand for Sixty Years

The most famous piece of evidence against the myth began as an act of childhood defiance. Donald Unger, a California physician, grew up hearing the same warning every child hears: crack your knuckles and you will get arthritis. His mother, aunts, and other adults in his life repeated it so often that the young Unger decided to test it — not with a laboratory, but with his own body.

Starting in his youth, Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice a day, every day, while deliberately never cracking the knuckles of his right hand. He kept this up for more than sixty years. By his own count, that amounted to tens of thousands of cracks on one side of his body and none on the other — a perfectly controlled experiment, with the two hands sharing the same genes, the same diet, the same environment, and the same owner. The only difference was the cracking.

When he finally compared the two hands, the result was anticlimactic in the best possible way: there was no difference. Neither hand showed signs of arthritis. There was no meaningful difference in pain, swelling, mobility, or grip between the hand he had cracked for six decades and the hand he had left alone. He published the results in the journal Arthritis and Rheumatism in 1998, and in 2009 the experiment earned him the Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine — the award that honors research that first makes people laugh, and then makes them think.

Unger's experiment is beloved because it is so clean and so funny. It is also, on its own, weak evidence in one important respect: it involved exactly one person. A single-subject study cannot rule out the possibility that Unger was simply lucky, or unusually resilient, or that cracking harms most people but not him. If his experiment were the only evidence available, the verdict on this claim would have to be "unproven" rather than "false." Fortunately, it is very far from the only evidence.

## What Actually Makes the Sound

Before looking at the larger studies, it helps to understand what a knuckle crack actually is — because the mechanism itself explains why the arthritis claim never made much biological sense.

Each finger joint is a synovial joint: two bones whose ends are cushioned by cartilage, enclosed in a capsule filled with synovial fluid. That fluid lubricates the joint, and it contains dissolved gases, mainly nitrogen, with smaller amounts of oxygen and carbon dioxide. When you pull, bend, or press a knuckle, you stretch the joint capsule slightly and the surfaces of the joint separate by a fraction of a millimeter. The pressure inside the joint drops suddenly, and the dissolved gases rush out of solution to form a bubble in the fluid. That rapid bubble formation — a process called cavitation, or more precisely tribonucleation — produces the pop.

Crucially, nothing is grinding, snapping, or breaking. The bones are not rubbing together. The cartilage is not being scraped away. The sound is a pressure event in fluid, not a structural failure in tissue. That is why the pop cannot simply be repeated on demand: after a crack, the gases need roughly twenty minutes to dissolve back into the synovial fluid before the conditions for another crack can be recreated. This refractory period is familiar to every habitual cracker, who knows that a freshly cracked knuckle stubbornly refuses to pop again right away.

For decades, scientists argued about a finer detail: whether the sound comes from the bubble forming or from the bubble collapsing. A real-time MRI study published in 2015, which filmed a knuckle cracking inside a scanner, supported formation — the bubble appearing rapidly as the joint separated. Later mathematical modelling suggested that the bubble's partial collapse could also generate sound. Either way, the two candidate mechanisms agree on the point that matters for this claim: the sound is made by gas in fluid, not by damage to the joint. There is no plausible mechanism by which a gas bubble in synovial fluid would inflame a joint, erode cartilage, or trigger the disease process of osteoarthritis.

That mechanistic point matters because it sets a high bar for anyone claiming a link. If cracking caused arthritis, it would have to do so through some route other than the crack itself — and no such route has ever been demonstrated.

## What the Bigger Studies Found

The strongest population evidence comes from a study published in 2011 in the Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine. Researchers examined 215 adults between the ages of 50 and 89, took X-rays of their hands, and compared people with radiographic hand osteoarthritis against people without it. They also measured each person's lifetime exposure to knuckle cracking in "crack-years" — daily cracking frequency multiplied by the number of years they had been doing it — so that someone who had cracked four times a day for forty years counted differently from someone who had cracked occasionally for a decade.

The result was unambiguous. Osteoarthritis was present in 18.1 percent of habitual knuckle crackers, compared with 21.5 percent of non-crackers — a difference that was not statistically significant, and which, if anything, leaned the wrong way for the myth. There was no association between cracking and osteoarthritis for any finger joint, at any frequency, or at any duration. When the researchers controlled for age, sex, family history of hand osteoarthritis, and history of heavy manual labor, the two strongest predictors of hand arthritis were family history and age. Knuckle cracking was not a significant factor at all.

An earlier study, published in 1990, had examined 300 patients aged 45 and older — 74 habitual knuckle crackers and 226 non-crackers — and reached the same conclusion on arthritis: no increased rate of hand arthritis in either group. That study did report two differences that have been quoted ever since by defenders of the myth: the habitual crackers were more likely to have hand swelling and lower grip strength. But those findings came with an important caveat that is often dropped when the study is quoted. The crackers in that group also differed in other ways, including occupational factors such as manual labor, which could easily explain both the swelling and the weaker grip. And a later study using ultrasound and physical examination found no measurable differences in grip strength, swelling, or joint looseness between crackers and non-crackers. The 1990 findings on swelling and grip have never been cleanly replicated, while the finding of no arthritis link has been replicated repeatedly.

Harvard Health Publishing has summarized the state of the evidence in the same terms, noting that several studies have found no increased risk of arthritis among habitual knuckle crackers. Clinical guidance reflects the same conclusion: routine, painless knuckle cracking has not been demonstrated to cause osteoarthritis, and doctors do not list it among the risk factors for the disease.

It is worth pausing on what those risk factors actually are, because the contrast is instructive. Osteoarthritis of the hand is driven by age, by genetics and family history, by previous injuries that damaged cartilage, by heavy manual labor over many years, and by inflammatory and autoimmune conditions. Rheumatoid arthritis, a different disease, is driven by the immune system attacking the joints. None of these causes has anything to do with whether a person pulls on their fingers. When a lifelong knuckle cracker develops arthritis in old age, the cause is almost certainly one of these factors — most often simply age and genes — and the cracking is a bystander that happened to be present for the ride.

## The Caveats: Where Cracking Can Actually Hurt

A verdict of "false" on the arthritis claim is not a verdict that knuckle cracking is entirely without consequence, and honesty requires saying so. The evidence clears ordinary, painless cracking of causing arthritis. It does not clear every possible way of cracking a joint, and it does not clear cracking that hurts.

The real risk comes from force. Yanking, twisting, or wrenching a finger aggressively — pulling it sideways, bending it far beyond its normal range, or forcing it with sudden violence — can injure the soft tissues around the joint. Case reports and clinical experience describe ligament sprains and tendon injuries from forceful manipulation, and in extreme cases even dislocation. These are rare, and they result from the force, not from the pop itself: the same wrenching motion would injure the joint whether or not it happened to produce a cracking sound. The distinction matters. Gentle cracking, the kind most people do absentmindedly while reading or thinking, has no such documented injury pattern.

There is also a second, subtler caveat. Some studies have suggested that people who crack very frequently may have slightly looser joints than those who do not, though this looseness has not been shown to translate into arthritis or functional problems. And the older, unreplicated findings on swelling and grip strength mean that the broadest possible claim — that knuckle cracking has absolutely no effect on anything, ever — is less firmly established than the narrow claim about arthritis. The honest formulation is this: cracking does not cause arthritis, and ordinary painless cracking has no reliably documented harms, but forceful cracking is a different activity with real, if rare, risks.

That distinction is also the practical advice. If cracking your knuckles feels good and does not hurt, there is no medical reason to stop. If it hurts, swells, catches, or locks, stop and pay attention — not because the cracking caused arthritis, but because pain and swelling are signs that something else may be going on in the joint, and that something deserves a doctor's attention.

## Why the Myth Refuses to Die

If the evidence is this one-sided, why does the warning survive? Partly because of the intuitive sound argument described above: a pop sounds like damage. Partly because of the age coincidence: crackers grow old, old hands ache, and the human brain is wired to connect events that happen in sequence. And partly because the warning is passed down as folk wisdom, from parent to child, with the authority of love and concern behind it. A mother who tells her child to stop cracking is not lying; she is repeating something she was told by someone she trusted, who was told by someone they trusted. Myths transmitted this way are unusually durable because questioning them feels like questioning the people who raised you.

There is also a feedback loop in popular media. Articles that promise "bad habits that destroy your joints" need entries, and knuckle cracking is a vivid, familiar one. The correction — a quiet study of 215 people and a man who cracked one hand for sixty years — is less clickable than the warning. Even well-meaning health content sometimes hedges, writing that cracking "may" cause problems or that the evidence is "mixed," when on the arthritis question specifically, it is not mixed at all. Hedging feels responsible; in this case, it misleads.

Unger's own story may have contributed to the myth's persistence in an ironic way. Because his experiment is so famous and so funny, it is easy to treat it as a joke rather than as evidence — and easy for skeptics to dismiss it as a sample of one. The less famous population studies, which are the real backbone of the verdict, rarely get mentioned in the same breath. But they exist, they agree, and they are the reason this article can say what it says.

## When to See a Doctor

None of this means that every sound a joint makes is harmless, and readers should know the difference between an ordinary crack and a warning sign. Painless cracking — a clean pop from pulling or bending a finger, with no aftereffects — is the pattern the research has cleared. Cracking that comes with pain, swelling, stiffness that does not ease with movement, reduced grip strength, a catching or locking sensation, visible deformity, or numbness is a different matter. Those symptoms can point to osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, tendinitis, or a joint injury — conditions that are not caused by cracking but that may be revealed by it, or that may simply coincide with it.

The rule is simple: the sound is not the problem, but pain is. If cracking hurts, stop doing it forcefully and talk to a clinician. If it does not hurt, you may carry on with a clear conscience — and, if you like, you may mention Donald Unger to the next person who warns you.

## The Verdict

**False.** Cracking your knuckles does not cause arthritis.

The evidence is unusually tidy for a health myth. A man cracked only his left hand for more than sixty years and found no difference between his hands, publishing the result in 1998 and winning the 2009 Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine. A 2011 study of 215 adults aged 50 to 89, measuring lifetime cracking exposure in crack-years and confirming joint health with X-rays, found osteoarthritis in 18.1 percent of habitual crackers versus 21.5 percent of non-crackers — no association at any frequency or duration. An earlier study of 300 adults reached the same conclusion. The sound itself is a gas bubble forming in synovial fluid, not bones grinding or cartilage wearing away, so there is no plausible mechanism for the claimed harm. The real drivers of hand arthritis are age, family history, prior injury, and inflammation — none of which have anything to do with pulling on your fingers.

Two qualifications belong in the same breath as the verdict. First, forceful cracking — yanking or twisting a finger violently — can sprain a ligament or injure a tendon, so gentle cracking and violent wrenching are not the same activity. Second, pain, swelling, or locking that accompanies cracking is a reason to see a doctor, because those symptoms can signal a genuine joint condition. Neither qualification rescues the myth. The claim that ordinary knuckle cracking gives you arthritis is false, and it has been false for as long as anyone has bothered to test it.

So the next time someone in the room tells a child to stop cracking their knuckles or face a future of arthritis, you can give them the good news, and the name behind it. Donald Unger spent sixty years — and tens of thousands of cracks — proving his mother wrong. He did it one hand at a time, and the science has spent the decades since confirming that he was right. Your knuckles are not keeping score. Crack away — gently — and let the myth finally rest.
