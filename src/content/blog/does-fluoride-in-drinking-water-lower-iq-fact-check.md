Scroll through social media in 2025 or 2026 and you will find the claim everywhere: fluoride in tap water is lowering children's IQs, the government has known for years, and two US states have already banned it. The posts usually come with a screenshot of a scientific paper, a quote from a federal judge, and a confident conclusion — that the stuff in your drinking water is making kids less intelligent, and that the only sensible response is to stop fluoridating water altogether.

It is the kind of claim that works because it is built around real events. In August 2024, the US National Toxicology Program really did publish a major review concluding that higher fluoride exposure is associated with lower IQ in children. In January 2025, a meta-analysis in *JAMA Pediatrics* really did pool 74 studies and find the same pattern. In September 2024, a federal judge really did rule that fluoridated water poses an "unreasonable risk of reduced IQ in children." And in 2025, Utah and Florida really did become the first states to ban the addition of fluoride to public water supplies.

Every one of those facts is true. The conclusion the viral posts draw from them is not. The question is not whether fluoride and IQ have ever been linked in a study — they have. The question is at what dose, in whom, and whether the evidence says anything at all about the 0.7 milligrams per liter that actually flows from a US tap. When you read the studies instead of the screenshots, the story changes shape: a genuine scientific signal at high exposures, a conspicuous gap in the evidence at the level used in American water systems, and a public debate that has run far ahead of what the data can support.

This is a claim where both sides have something real to point to — and where the most honest verdict is that the viral version of it is misleading.

## The Claim

The claim, in the form it takes on TikTok, X, and Facebook, goes something like this: "Fluoride in drinking water lowers IQ in children. The government's own scientists proved it in 2024, a federal court confirmed it, and that's why states are banning it." Some versions go further, calling fluoridation "forced medication" or describing fluoride as a neurotoxin being added to the water supply. The implication is direct and personal: the water coming out of your kitchen tap is damaging your child's brain.

The claim matters because it is not a fringe internet rumor anymore. It has moved policy. Utah banned fluoridation statewide in 2025. Florida followed the same year. More than 80 communities have voted to end fluoridation since the court ruling in September 2024. The US Department of Health and Human Services announced in April 2025 that it was reconvening the Community Preventive Services Task Force to make a new recommendation on fluoride, and the Environmental Protection Agency launched its own review of the science. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., a longtime critic of fluoridation, has pushed the issue to the center of the Make America Healthy Again agenda.

That is a lot of official motion for a claim that deserves a careful look. So let us look at what the research actually found — starting with the two documents the viral posts cite most.

## What the Federal Review Actually Found

The National Toxicology Program, part of the National Institutes of Health, began its review of fluoride and brain development in 2016. After eight years of work, two rounds of review by a National Academies committee, and peer review by five external experts, it published its final monograph on August 21, 2024. The conclusion, stated with what the program calls "moderate confidence," was that higher levels of fluoride exposure — such as drinking water containing more than 1.5 milligrams of fluoride per liter — are associated with lower IQ in children.

That sentence has been quoted thousands of times. The sentences around it have been quoted far less. The same review said there were insufficient data to determine whether the low fluoride level of 0.7 mg/L currently recommended for US community water supplies has a negative effect on children's IQ. It found no evidence that fluoride exposure had adverse effects on adult cognition. And it was designed to evaluate total fluoride exposure from all sources — water, food, beverages, toothpaste swallowed by small children — not the health effects of fluoridated drinking water alone.

The distinction matters because the studies behind the conclusion mostly did not come from American towns with fluoridated water. Of the 74 studies pooled in the companion meta-analysis, 45 were conducted in China, with others in India, Iran, Pakistan, Mexico, and Canada. Many of the study populations lived in areas where fluoride occurs naturally in groundwater at levels well above the World Health Organization's guideline value of 1.5 mg/L — sometimes several times higher. The NTP itself noted that its determination about lower IQs was based primarily on epidemiology studies in countries where some pregnant women, infants, and children received total fluoride exposure amounts higher than 1.5 mg/L of drinking water.

An association, the NTP was careful to add, indicates a connection between fluoride and lower IQ. It does not prove cause and effect. Children in high-fluoride regions may differ from children elsewhere in ways the studies could not fully account for — nutrition, iodine deficiency, exposure to other contaminants, parental education, and the quality of the IQ tests themselves. Of the 74 studies in the meta-analysis, 52 were rated at high risk of bias and only 22 at low risk of bias. That does not make the findings worthless, but it does mean they should be handled with the caution the authors themselves applied.

## The 2025 Meta-Analysis: The Numbers Behind the Headlines

The second document, published in *JAMA Pediatrics* on January 6, 2025, was written by the same team of scientists at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, led by Kyla Taylor. It quantitatively pooled the 74 epidemiological studies the NTP had reviewed, covering more than 20,000 children. Its headline results were striking. Across 59 studies that measured fluoride exposure at the group level, higher exposure was associated with lower IQ scores, with a pooled standardized mean difference of −0.45. Across 13 studies that measured fluoride in individual children's urine, each additional 1 mg/L of urinary fluoride was associated with an IQ score 1.63 points lower. Among the studies judged to be at low risk of bias, the figure was 1.14 points lower per 1 mg/L.

Those numbers are real, and they survived the toughest test the authors could apply: the inverse association held when the analysis was restricted to high-quality studies only, and it was consistent across subgroups defined by sex, age, country, the type of IQ test, and the timing of exposure. In December 2025, the same authors published a detailed response to critics in which they reported that the inverse association persisted among high-quality studies even when exposure was restricted to levels below 1.5 mg/L, measured in both urine and drinking water.

But the dose-response detail is where the viral version of the story starts to come apart. When the researchers looked at fluoride measured in drinking water specifically, the association held when exposed groups were restricted to levels below 4 mg/L and below 2 mg/L — but it was null below 1.5 mg/L. In other words, in the drinking-water studies, the signal disappeared at exactly the range that includes the US recommended level of 0.7 mg/L. The exception was the smaller group of low-risk-of-bias studies, where an inverse association persisted below 1.5 mg/L — a finding the authors themselves described as based on limited data, and one that other reviewers have questioned.

A separate 2023 meta-analysis of 33 studies, published in *Environmental Research*, reached a similar conclusion from a different angle: it estimated IQ falling by about 3 points per 1 mg/L as water fluoride rose above 1 mg/L — but the link was strongest in studies at high risk of bias, and the one study judged at low risk of bias found no adverse effect. The authors could not rule out confounding.

None of this means fluoride is proven safe at every dose. It means the evidence has a shape, and the shape is dose-dependent: a consistent signal at higher exposures, and uncertainty — not proof of safety, but not proof of harm either — at the level in American tap water. That is a very different statement from "fluoride in drinking water lowers IQ."

## The Dose Makes the Difference

The single most important fact in this whole debate is also the simplest: fluoride is not one exposure. It is a range of exposures, and where a water supply sits on that range changes everything. The numbers below are the ones regulators, researchers, and courts keep coming back to.

| Fluoride level | What it is | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| 0.7 mg/L | US Public Health Service recommended level for community fluoridation since 2015 | Insufficient data to determine an effect on children's IQ, according to the NTP |
| 1.5 mg/L | World Health Organization guideline value for fluoride in drinking water | Above this level, the NTP found with moderate confidence an association with lower IQ in children |
| 2.0 mg/L | EPA secondary standard, aimed at preventing dental fluorosis (tooth discoloration) | A 2023 CDC analysis found only 0.01% of measurements in fluoridating US systems exceeded this level in 2016–2021 |
| 4.0 mg/L | EPA enforceable maximum contaminant level for fluoride in public drinking water | Set to prevent skeletal fluorosis; well above the level at which IQ associations were studied |

That table is the whole controversy in miniature. The US target of 0.7 mg/L sits at less than half the WHO guideline value and less than a fifth of the EPA's legal maximum. The NTP's finding begins above 1.5 mg/L. The gap between 0.7 and 1.5 is not a rounding error — it is a doubling of the dose, and in toxicology, dose is often the difference between a nutrient and a poison. Fluoride itself is a good example: the same ion that strengthens tooth enamel at low concentrations causes dental fluorosis — faint white marks on children's teeth — at higher ones, and skeletal fluorosis at higher ones still.

There is a second dose problem that rarely makes it into the viral posts. The NTP reviewed total fluoride exposure, not water alone. A child in a fluoridated town also gets fluoride from toothpaste, food, and beverages — and in the high-fluoride regions where most of the studies were done, from naturally fluoridated groundwater that nobody added anything to. Attributing a study's result to the tap alone is a leap the researchers did not make.

None of this settles the question of whether 0.7 mg/L is safe. "Insufficient data" is not the same as "proven safe," and honest scientists on both sides say so. The American Dental Association's position is that the best available evidence does not establish a causal relationship between water fluoridated at recommended levels and lowered IQ — a careful sentence that reflects the state of the evidence rather than claiming certainty. Critics of fluoridation argue, with some justification, that the absence of good low-dose studies is itself a reason for caution, especially for pregnant women and infants. Both positions are defensible. What is not defensible is presenting the high-dose finding as though it were a finding about American tap water.

## What the Court Cases Did — and Didn't — Decide

If the science is dose-dependent and uncertain at low levels, why did a federal judge rule that fluoridated water poses an "unreasonable risk"? The answer is that courts and scientists answer different questions.

In September 2024, Senior Judge Edward Chen of the US District Court for the Northern District of California ruled in *Food & Water Watch v. EPA* that fluoride added to drinking water at the recommended 0.7 mg/L concentration poses an "unreasonable risk of reduced IQ in children" under the Toxic Substances Control Act, and ordered the EPA to begin rulemaking. The case had been running since 2016, when advocacy groups petitioned the EPA to regulate fluoridation. Judge Chen paused the proceedings to await the NTP review, then leaned heavily on it in his ruling.

The ruling was immediately described in headlines as a finding that fluoridation is dangerous. It was, in legal terms, something narrower: a finding that the evidence met the Toxic Substances Control Act's threshold for the EPA to take regulatory action — a precautionary legal standard, not a scientific determination that 0.7 mg/L causes IQ loss. The judge did not conduct his own study, and the NTP monograph his ruling relied on was the same document that said there were insufficient data to judge the 0.7 mg/L level.

Then, on May 21, 2026, the story flipped again. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated Judge Chen's ruling, finding that he had abused his discretion by putting the case on hold to wait for a report that neither side had entered into the trial record. The appeals court sent the case back to the district court to rule again on a narrower evidentiary record — one that does not include the studies that emerged between the two trials or the final NTP monograph itself. Crucially, the appeals court did not address the science behind Chen's finding, and the EPA did not challenge the factual merits of his unreasonable-risk determination. The underlying legal question — whether the EPA must regulate fluoride under the toxic substances law — is open again rather than settled in either direction. In November 2026, the advocacy groups that brought the case asked the appeals court to reconsider its decision.

So the legal scoreboard reads: a district court found an unreasonable risk; an appeals court threw that ruling out on procedural grounds without touching the science; the case goes back for another round. Anyone who cites the court case as proof that fluoride lowers IQ is citing a ruling that no longer stands, based on a report the appeals court said should not have been in the record. Anyone who cites the appeals court as proof that fluoride is safe is making the opposite error — the court expressly did not decide the science.

## Why Two States Banned It Anyway

The science is uncertain at 0.7 mg/L. The court case is unresolved. So why did Utah and Florida ban fluoridation in 2025 — and why have more than 80 communities followed?

Utah moved first. Governor Spencer Cox signed House Bill 81 on March 27, 2025, and it took effect on May 7, 2025, making Utah the first state to ban the addition of fluoride to public water systems outright. The law says no person may add fluoride to water in a public water system, and no local government may pass an ordinance permitting it. Florida followed with a provision in Senate Bill 700, the state's farm bill; Governor Ron DeSantis signed it on May 15, 2025, and it took effect on July 1, 2025. DeSantis described fluoridation as "forced medication" and framed the ban as a matter of individual choice.

The politics are inseparable from the science here, and honest reporting should say so. The bans were driven in large part by the Make America Healthy Again movement and by Secretary Kennedy, who has challenged fluoridation for years and has long argued that the oral health benefits of fluoride are mainly topical — from toothpaste applied directly to teeth — rather than from swallowing it in water. That argument has support from independent researchers who note that fluoride's main proven mechanism is topical. But the bans were also political decisions made ahead of the evidence: neither state waited for the EPA review or the reconvened federal task force to finish its work.

It is worth noting what the bans do not prove. A legislature banning a substance is not a scientific finding. Lawmakers ban things for political, philosophical, and precautionary reasons all the time — including things the scientific consensus considers safe. Utah's ban tells you something real about American politics in 2025. It tells you nothing new about whether 0.7 mg/L of fluoride affects a child's IQ.

Meanwhile, the federal machinery is still turning. In April 2025, the Department of Health and Human Services said it would reconvene the Community Preventive Services Task Force to study fluoride and make a new recommendation, and EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin launched an accelerated review of the science under the Safe Drinking Water Act. The EPA released a preliminary assessment plan for public comment in January 2026. As of late 2026, the EPA's 4.0 mg/L limit has not changed, and the 0.7 mg/L recommendation still stands — though Connecticut, notably, passed a 2025 law locking the 0.7 mg/L standard into state statute regardless of what federal guidance does next. The country is, in effect, running a natural experiment in two directions at once.

## The Other Side of the Ledger

There is a reason fluoridation survived for eighty years, and any honest fact-check has to weigh it. Community water fluoridation began in Grand Rapids, Michigan, in 1945, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention later named it one of the ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century. The CDC's position is that fluoridation at the recommended level reduces tooth decay by about 25 percent in children and adults. The American Dental Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics continue to support it, and after the 2024 NTP report the ADA said the monograph did not provide new or conclusive evidence that should change current fluoridation practice.

That benefit, however, is also smaller than it used to be — and the same era of evidence review that produced the IQ findings has quietly shrunk it. A 2024 update of the Cochrane review on water fluoridation, which pooled 157 studies, found that starting fluoridation today may lead to a slightly greater reduction in tooth decay in children, but with smaller effects than in the studies from before 1975, and rated that evidence as low certainty. The reason is straightforward: when fluoridation began, almost nobody used fluoride toothpaste. Today almost everybody does, so the extra benefit of fluoride in the water is harder to detect.

This is the genuinely difficult trade-off at the heart of the debate, and it is the part the viral posts almost never mention. The cavity benefit is real but smaller than the textbooks suggest. The IQ risk is real at higher doses and unproven at the dose in the tap. A policymaker weighing those two facts could reasonably conclude that fluoridation is still worth it, or reasonably conclude that it is not — but neither conclusion follows from pretending one side of the ledger does not exist. The same lesson applies to other foods and chemicals that social media has declared toxic on thin evidence, as we found when we asked <a href="/blog/seed-oils-toxic-claim/">Are Seed Oils Toxic? What the Science Actually Says About the Viral Claim</a>. The NTP review, notably, did not assess fluoride's dental benefits at all. It was a hazard review, not a risk-benefit analysis. Treating it as the final word on whether fluoridation should continue is like judging a drug by reading only the side-effects leaflet.

It is also worth remembering how fluoride got into this debate in the first place. Fluoride is not a synthetic chemical invented for water treatment. It occurs naturally in rocks, soil, and groundwater, and many communities drink water with natural fluoride levels above 1.5 mg/L without anyone adding a thing. The question was never whether fluoride is "natural" or "artificial." The question is how much, and in whom — and the evidence answers that question differently at different doses. The same pattern shows up in other viral chemical scares we have checked: the dose and the context, not the mere presence of a substance, decide whether there is a real risk, as we found when we asked <a href="/blog/does-aspartame-cause-cancer/">Does Aspartame Cause Cancer? What the WHO's 'Possibly Carcinogenic' Label Really Means</a>.

## A Short History of Fluoride and IQ

The debate did not start in 2024. It has been building for decades, and the timeline explains why it exploded when it did.

**1945** — Grand Rapids, Michigan, becomes the first city in the world to add fluoride to its public water supply, launching a public health experiment that would spread across the United States.

**1999** — The CDC names community water fluoridation one of the ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century.

**2006** — A National Research Council report reviews fluoride's health effects and flags the need for more research on neurodevelopment — the first official signal that the IQ question deserved serious study.

**2015** — The US Public Health Service lowers its recommended fluoridation level from a range of 0.7–1.2 mg/L to a single target of 0.7 mg/L, aiming to maximize dental benefit while minimizing fluorosis.

**2016** — The National Toxicology Program begins its systematic review of fluoride and neurodevelopment; separately, advocacy groups petition the EPA to regulate fluoridation under the Toxic Substances Control Act, beginning seven years of litigation.

**August 2024** — The NTP publishes its final monograph: moderate confidence that higher fluoride exposure is associated with lower IQ in children; insufficient data at 0.7 mg/L.

**September 2024** — Judge Edward Chen rules that fluoridation at 0.7 mg/L poses an "unreasonable risk of reduced IQ in children" and orders the EPA to begin rulemaking.

**January 2025** — The *JAMA Pediatrics* meta-analysis of 74 studies quantifies the association: 1.63 fewer IQ points per 1 mg/L increase in urinary fluoride.

**March–July 2025** — Utah bans fluoridation (HB 81, effective May 7); Florida follows (SB 700, effective July 1). The Department of Health and Human Services announces a new review of fluoride guidance, and the EPA launches its own accelerated review.

**December 2025** — The NTP study authors publish a detailed defense of their findings, reporting that the inverse association persisted among high-quality studies even below 1.5 mg/L.

**May 2026** — The Ninth Circuit vacates the 2024 court ruling on procedural grounds, sending the case back to the district court. The EPA's fluoride limit remains unchanged.

**November 2026** — Advocacy groups ask the Ninth Circuit to reconsider its decision. More than 80 communities have now voted to end fluoridation since the original 2024 ruling.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The viral claim takes a real scientific finding and applies it to the wrong dose.

Higher fluoride exposure is genuinely associated with lower IQ in children — that is the conclusion of a federal review conducted with moderate confidence and a peer-reviewed meta-analysis of 74 studies, and it should not be dismissed. But the association was found at exposures above 1.5 mg/L, mostly in regions where fluoride occurs naturally in groundwater at high levels. The same review found insufficient data to determine whether the 0.7 mg/L level used in US community water supplies affects children's IQ at all. The meta-analysis found no association below 1.5 mg/L in its drinking-water studies, except in a small subgroup of low-bias studies. The court ruling the posts cite has been vacated. And the state bans they point to were political decisions, not scientific findings.

"Fluoride lowers IQ" is therefore true in the specific, high-dose sense the researchers described, and false as a blanket statement about American tap water. A claim that is true at one dose and unproven at another, presented as though it were true at every dose, is the definition of misleading.

That verdict cuts both ways. It is also misleading to say fluoride has been "proven safe" at 0.7 mg/L — it has not. The evidence at that level is thin, the low-dose studies the field needs have not been done at scale, and the precautionary argument for protecting pregnant women and young children deserves to be heard on its own merits. What the evidence does not support is certainty in either direction — and certainty is exactly what the viral posts are selling.

## What You Can Actually Do

If you are a parent reading this and wondering what to do on Monday morning, the evidence points to a few practical, unglamorous steps rather than a grand gesture.

First, find out what is actually in your water. In the United States, your water utility publishes an annual Consumer Confidence Report, and many utilities list fluoride levels online. Most fluoridated systems run close to 0.7 mg/L; if yours runs higher, or if your water comes from a private well in a high-fluoride area, that is worth knowing. The EPA's limit is 4.0 mg/L, but the research signal begins at 1.5 — so the number that matters for this debate is not whether your water is "legal" but where it sits relative to 1.5. The same "check before you drink" logic applies to other untreated water sources, as we found when we asked <a href="/blog/is-rainwater-safe-to-drink/">Is Rainwater Safe to Drink? No — Not Straight From the Sky or the Barrel</a>.

Second, remember that water is not the only source. Fluoride toothpaste is the main proven benefit for teeth, and it is also a source of exposure — especially for small children who swallow toothpaste instead of spitting it out. Pediatric dentists recommend a smear of toothpaste for children under three and a pea-sized amount for ages three to six, precisely to keep the dose low while keeping the topical benefit.

Third, if you are pregnant or caring for an infant, talk to your pediatrician or dentist rather than taking guidance from a viral post. They can weigh your local water level, your child's fluoride exposure from all sources, and the state of the evidence — including the fact that the federal guidance is currently under review and may change.

And finally, treat any post that tells you the science is settled — in either direction — with skepticism. The fluoride debate is a case study in how a real finding gets flattened into a slogan. The finding deserves attention. The slogan does not.
