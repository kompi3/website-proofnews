Every kitchen has a version of the same small ritual. You open the fridge, pick up a carton of milk or a tub of yoghurt, and your eyes go straight to the stamped date. One day past it, and the item is treated as if it has crossed some invisible line — safe at 11:59 p.m., dangerous at midnight. Into the bin it goes, often without a sniff, a glance, or a second thought.

That ritual feels like common sense. It is also, for most foods, wrong — or at least badly misleading. The date printed on a package is rarely the moment food becomes unsafe. In the United States, it is usually not a safety deadline at all. It is a manufacturer's estimate of when the food will taste its best, and in most cases nobody in government required it to be there in the first place. The confusion is understandable, because the labels themselves are confusing: a jumble of "sell by," "best by," "use by," and "best if used by" that sound like warnings but mostly are not. And the confusion is expensive. It is one of the largest single drivers of household food waste, sending billions of pounds of perfectly edible food to landfill every year.

None of this means you should eat anything, of any age, with total confidence. Some dates genuinely matter, some foods genuinely become dangerous without showing any sign, and one category — infant formula — carries a date that should never be ignored. The truth is more useful than either extreme: the date is a clue, not a verdict. Learning to read it properly can save money, cut waste, and still keep you safe.

## The date on the package is not what most people think it is

The first surprise is how little the law actually says about food dates. In the United States, the Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) states plainly that, except for infant formula, product dating is not required by federal regulations. Manufacturers apply dates voluntarily. The FSIS guidance explains that manufacturers provide dating "to help consumers and retailers decide when food is of best quality," and adds, in terms that leave little room for doubt: "Except for infant formula, dates are not an indicator of the product's safety and are not required by Federal law."

That single fact reframes the whole debate. If the date were a safety deadline, you would expect it to be set by regulators, tested against pathogen growth, and enforced uniformly. Instead, it is set by the company selling the food, and companies have every incentive to be conservative. A date that is too generous risks complaints about stale-tasting products and damage to the brand. A date that is too strict costs the company nothing — the consumer throws the food away and buys another one. The system, in other words, is tilted toward throwing food out early.

Dr. Linda Harris, chair of the Food Science and Technology Department at UC Davis, put it simply when explaining the system to reporters: "The date is meant to signal quality. It's not a safety issue." That distinction — quality versus safety — is the key to the entire subject, and it is the distinction most shoppers were never taught.

Quality is about taste, texture, colour, and aroma. Crackers go stale. Cereal loses its crunch. Spices fade. Coffee goes flat. None of those changes will make you ill; they just make the food less enjoyable. Safety is about something else entirely: whether harmful bacteria have grown to dangerous levels, or whether toxins have formed. And here is the awkward part: the two things do not reliably move together. Food can taste perfectly fine and still be unsafe, and food can taste stale and still be perfectly safe.

## Decoding the labels: sell by, best by, use by

Part of the problem is that the labels do not even mean the same thing from one product to the next. The FSIS defines the main phrases as follows, and it is worth learning them because they answer most everyday questions:

**"Best if Used By" or "Best Before"** indicates when a product will be at its best flavour or quality. The FSIS is explicit: "It is not a purchase or safety date." This is the most common label, and the FSIS actually recommends it to manufacturers, precisely because research shows it communicates to shoppers that the product may still be eaten after the date if it shows no signs of spoilage.

**"Sell-By"** tells the store how long to display the product for sale, for inventory management. It is not a safety date, and it is not really addressed to you at all. It is a stock-rotation instruction that happens to be printed where shoppers can see it. Food bought on or even after its sell-by date can still be perfectly good for days, depending on the product and how it has been stored.

**"Use-By"** is the last date recommended for the use of the product while at peak quality. In the United States, the FSIS stresses that it is not a safety date — with the single exception of infant formula, where it is federally required and genuinely matters, because the date guarantees that the nutrients a baby depends on are still present in the labelled amounts. Never buy or use infant formula past its use-by date.

**"Freeze-By"** indicates when a product should be frozen to maintain peak quality. It is not a purchase or safety date either.

Notice the pattern: in the American system, every one of these phrases is, at heart, about quality. That is why food-safety experts keep saying that foods handled properly and showing no signs of spoilage should still be wholesome beyond their labelled dates. The FSIS says exactly that in its guidance.

The picture is different in the United Kingdom and the European Union, and the difference matters because so much food advice online mixes the two systems without saying so. Food Standards Scotland draws a sharp line: a use-by date is about food safety, and food should not be eaten after it; a best-before date is about quality, and food remains safe after it, though flavour, colour, or texture may fade. In Britain, use-by dates appear on the high-risk perishables — raw meat and fish, cooked sliced meats and deli items, pâté, soft cheeses, and some dairy products — and the official instruction is explicit. So if you are reading British guidance, "use by" genuinely is a safety line. If you are reading American guidance, it generally is not. Confusing the two systems is one reason this topic generates so much contradictory advice online.

## Why the sniff test works — and where it fails

Ask most people how they decide whether food is still good, and they will say they smell it. For many foods, that is a reasonable instinct. Spoilage bacteria — the microbes that make food smell sour, slimy, or "off" — announce themselves loudly. If bread smells musty, if milk smells sour, if leftovers smell wrong, your nose is doing its job.

But the sniff test has a hard limit, and it is an important one. The bacteria that cause food poisoning are not the same as the bacteria that cause spoilage, and the dangerous ones are often invisible and odourless. Listeria, for example, can multiply slowly at refrigerator temperatures on foods such as deli meats and soft cheeses without changing how the food looks or smells at all. Salmonella on raw poultry gives no warning either. That is why food-safety agencies warn never to rely on the sniff test for high-risk perishable foods, and never to taste food to test whether it is safe. Tasting is not a test; it is a gamble, and a small taste of food carrying a dangerous dose of bacteria is not meaningfully safer than a full serving.

The practical rule that follows is simple, if slightly unsatisfying: your senses are a good guide to quality, and a poor guide to safety. Use them to judge whether food is still appetising. Use the food's identity, its storage history, and its date label — in that order — to judge whether it is still safe.

## What the evidence says, food by food

General principles only get you so far in a real kitchen, so here is how the guidance shakes out for the foods people ask about most.

**Eggs.** Eggs are one of the most forgiving foods in the fridge. The USDA guidance cited by food-safety writers says refrigerated shell eggs can be used three to five weeks after the date of purchase, even if the sell-by date has passed. Keep them in their original carton in the main body of the refrigerator, not in the door, where temperatures swing. If you are unsure about an individual egg, the float test is a freshness check, not a safety check: an egg that floats has a larger air cell and is older, but it is not automatically unsafe, and an egg that sinks is not automatically safe if it has been stored badly. Crack eggs into a separate bowl, and discard any that smell off or look unusual.

**Milk and dairy.** Milk is where the date feels most authoritative, and it is also where storage matters most. University extension guidance widely quoted by food writers suggests milk can last about five to seven days past its printed date if the refrigerator is held at around 36 to 38 degrees Fahrenheit (roughly 2 to 3 degrees Celsius) and the carton has been kept cold throughout. But milk that has sat on the counter, or in a warm car, or in a fridge door that is opened constantly, may turn well before its date. Sour smell, curdling, or a bloated carton are all throw-it-out signals. Hard cheeses are more forgiving: the USDA says you can salvage a hard cheese by cutting off at least one inch around and below a mould spot. Soft cheeses — brie, feta, ricotta, cream cheese — are a different story, because mould and bacteria penetrate them more easily; discard soft cheese that shows mould.

**Canned food.** Properly stored canned food is one of the great survivors of the pantry. Food-safety writers summarising USDA guidance note that high-acid canned foods such as tomatoes, citrus juice, and pineapple maintain their best quality for 12 to 18 months, while low-acid canned foods such as vegetables, meat, poultry, and beans hold their best quality for two to five years — and remain safe well past those windows as long as the can is intact. The exceptions are physical, not calendrical: never eat from a can that is bulging, leaking, deeply dented along the seam, rusted through, or that spurts liquid when opened. Those are signs that the seal has failed or that gas-producing bacteria have been at work, and no date can override them.

**Dry goods: pasta, rice, flour, beans, spices.** Stored dry, sealed, and free of pests, these foods remain safe for years. White rice, dried pasta, dried beans, crackers, cereal, and flour may go stale or lose flavour, but they do not become dangerous simply because a date passed. Whole-grain flours and ground spices lose flavour faster because their oils go rancid — unpleasant, but again a quality issue rather than a poisoning risk. Keep them sealed, cool, and dry, and check for pantry moths or weevils before cooking, not because the insects are dangerous, but because nobody wants them in dinner.

**Frozen food.** Food kept at a constant 0 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 18 degrees Celsius) stays safe indefinitely, according to the USDA. Freezing stops bacterial growth completely; it does not kill bacteria, but it pauses them. That is why a chicken breast frozen for a year is safe to eat once thawed and cooked properly, even though its texture may have suffered from freezer burn. Quality windows are shorter — ground meat keeps its best quality for about three to four months in the freezer, raw chicken pieces for about nine months, according to the FoodSafety.gov cold storage chart — but those are taste windows, not safety deadlines. The danger with frozen food comes after thawing, not before: once thawed, the clock starts again, and thawed food should be cooked promptly and never refrozen raw.

**Bread and baked goods.** Bread that has dried out is still safe to eat; staleness is a texture problem, solved by toasting. Mould is the real signal. Unlike hard cheese, bread is porous, so mould roots can spread invisibly through the loaf — the standard advice is to discard mouldy bread rather than picking around the spot.

**Leftovers and cooked food.** Leftovers play by different rules from packaged food, because their clock starts when they are cooked, not when they are bought. The standard guidance is three to four days in the refrigerator for cooked meat, poultry, and most leftovers, and that window does not reset just because the original ingredient was fresh. This is also where storage history matters most: food left in the so-called danger zone — roughly between 40 and 140 degrees Fahrenheit — for more than two hours should be discarded, regardless of any date.

## The foods where the date genuinely matters

So far, this article has been reassuring, and deliberately so — because the reassurance is what the evidence supports for most foods. But a fact-check that only reassured would itself be misleading. There are cases where caution is not just prudent but required.

First, infant formula, as already noted: the one product whose date is a federally required safety date in the United States. Do not use formula past its use-by date, and do not buy it if the date has passed.

Second, in the UK and EU system, use-by dates on high-risk chilled foods. If you live under that system, or you are following British guidance, treat the use-by date on raw meat, ready-to-eat deli meats, pâté, and soft cheese as a safety deadline. The food may look and smell fine after it, and it may still be dangerous. When in doubt, throw it out — the official advice is unambiguous on this point.

Third, any food whose storage history is unknown or broken. A date assumes proper storage. Food stored in a hot garage, a fridge that lost power, or a car boot on a summer afternoon has effectively had its date cancelled. As Melissa Vaccaro, a senior food safety program specialist at the National Environmental Health Association, has warned, if food "was stored in a hot garage, the freezer lost power or the fridge door was left open frequently, the labelled date may no longer be reliable." The date is a promise made on the assumption of a cold chain; break the chain, and the promise is void.

Fourth, anyone at higher risk. Pregnant people, young children, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems face more severe consequences from foodborne illness, and for them the cautious option is worth more. Listeria is the classic example: it can cause severe illness in pregnancy and in immunocompromised people, and it is exactly the bacterium that grows quietly in the fridge without warning signs. If you are in a high-risk group, or cooking for someone who is, there is no shame in being stricter than this article's general guidance.

Fifth, anything that shows spoilage, whatever the date says. The date never overrules your senses in the other direction. A carton of milk three days before its date that smells sour is bad. A can that is bulging a year before its best-quality window is dangerous. The date is a guide to when quality fades; spoilage is evidence that it already has, and possibly that safety has gone with it.

## The cost of getting this wrong

The stakes here are not abstract. Date-label confusion is estimated to account for around a fifth of household food waste in the United States, with some estimates putting the value of food discarded by an average family of four at more than a thousand dollars a year. One survey cited by food writers found that only about a quarter of consumers knew what a "use-by" date meant, that roughly half would not eat food beyond its "sell-by" date, and that around three in ten believed "use-by" marked the last date the food was edible at all. Those misunderstandings add up to roughly three billion pounds of edible food discarded annually in the United States, according to estimates repeated in recent food-waste reporting.

That waste has a second cost beyond the household budget. Food in landfill decomposes and produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas, which means that a label designed to protect quality ends up harming the climate when it is misread as a safety warning. None of this is an argument for eating risky food. It is an argument for directing caution where it belongs — at high-risk foods, broken storage, and genuine spoilage — instead of spreading it evenly across everything in the pantry, including the crackers.

There is also a fairness angle worth noting. Households with tight budgets feel this confusion hardest, because they are the least able to absorb throwing away good food, and the most likely to face the choice between wasting food and risking illness. Clearer labels would help everyone, but they would help those households most. The food industry has moved slowly toward standardisation — the Grocery Manufacturers Association has long pushed for just two phrases, "best if used by" for most products and "use by" for very perishable ones — but in the United States there is still no single federal standard, and manufacturers remain free to choose their own wording. Until that changes, the burden of decoding the label falls on the shopper.

## How to decide, in practice

If the research in this article could be distilled into a routine for standing in front of an open fridge, it would look like this.

Start with the label, not the calendar. Ask what kind of date it is. A "best if used by" or "best before" date is a quality promise; food past it deserves a look and a sniff, not an automatic trip to the bin. A "sell-by" date is a message to the shop, and the food usually has days of good life left at home. A "use-by" date deserves more respect, especially under the UK system or on high-risk chilled foods, and on infant formula it is absolute.

Then ask about the food itself. Is it a dry, canned, or frozen staple? It is almost certainly fine well past its date if it looks and smells normal and its packaging is intact. Is it a high-risk perishable — raw meat, deli slices, soft cheese, ready meals? Then be stricter, keep it cold, and respect its date, particularly a use-by date.

Then ask about storage. Has it been kept cold, sealed, and dry? If the cold chain broke, or you genuinely do not know, the date cannot help you, and caution is the right call.

Then use your senses — for quality. Off smell, slime, unexpected mould, bloating, or a broken seal are all reasons to discard, whatever the date says. But remember their limit: the absence of a bad smell is not proof of safety for high-risk foods, so do not let a clean sniff overrule a use-by date on deli meat or a broken cold chain.

Finally, know who is eating. If the meal is for a pregnant person, a small child, an older adult, or someone with a weakened immune system, choose the cautious option. Caution costs a few dollars; food poisoning in a vulnerable person can cost far more.

## The Verdict

So, is it safe to eat food after the expiration date? The claim that food becomes unsafe the moment its date passes is **Misleading**. For most foods, the date is a quality estimate set voluntarily by the manufacturer, not a safety deadline set by regulators — in the United States, the USDA says so explicitly, with infant formula as the sole exception. Dry goods, canned foods, frozen foods, and many dairy products and eggs remain safe well past their printed dates when stored properly and showing no signs of spoilage. At the same time, the reassuring version of the story has real limits: under the UK system, use-by dates on high-risk chilled foods are genuine safety deadlines; listeria and other dangerous bacteria can grow without any smell or visible sign; broken storage voids the date's promise; and infant formula past its date should never be used. The date is a useful clue, but it is not the danger, and it is not the guarantee either. Read the label, know the food, check the storage, and when the food itself tells you something is wrong, believe it over any date — in either direction.

## The quiet skill nobody teaches

There is something quietly satisfying about learning to read a date label properly. It is a small, unglamorous skill — no gadget, no app, no subscription required — but it sits at the intersection of money, health, and waste, three things every household cares about. Most of us were taught a single rule: trust the date, throw it out. The evidence suggests a better rule: trust the date as a hint about quality, trust the food itself about spoilage, trust the cold chain about safety, and reserve real fear for the few cases where it is earned.

The next time you stand in front of the fridge with a carton in your hand, pause before the bin. Read the words next to the date, not just the numbers. Ask what the food is, how it has been stored, and who is going to eat it. More often than you expect, the answer will be that the food is fine — a little less crisp, a little less bright, but fine. And the money you save, and the waste you avoid, will be the quiet reward for a decision made with evidence instead of habit.
