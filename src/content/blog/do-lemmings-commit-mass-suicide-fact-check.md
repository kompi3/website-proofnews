Everyone knows what a lemming is. Not the animal itself — almost nobody has seen one outside a zoo — but the metaphor. A lemming is a mindless follower. When politicians want to accuse the other side of marching in lockstep toward disaster, they call them lemmings. When a crowd makes a bad decision together, someone says the lemmings are at it again. The word itself is a little insult with a built-in nature documentary: small, furry rodents that, when times get tough, throw themselves off cliffs in a mass suicide pact.

There is just one problem. None of it is true. Lemmings do not commit mass suicide. They never have. The entire idea — cliff, plunge, splash, silence — was staged by a Disney film crew in 1958 with a snow-covered turntable, a river in Alberta, and a few dozen terrified rodents who had been shipped hundreds of miles from home. The movie went on to win the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. The truth, meanwhile, spent decades trying to catch up with the footage.

This is the story of how one of the most successful hoaxes in wildlife filmmaking rewired a perfectly innocent Arctic rodent's reputation — and what lemmings actually do when nobody is pushing them off a cliff.

## The Claim

The version of the story that lives in our heads goes like this: every few years, when lemming populations explode beyond what the Arctic tundra can support, the rodents go mad. Driven by some combination of overcrowding and despair, they form enormous columns and march across the landscape until they reach the sea — or a cliff above it — and hurl themselves over the edge in an act of collective, voluntary suicide. The sea fills with drowning lemmings. The population is "reset." Nature's darkest form of population control, played out on cue.

It is a vivid story, a moral story, and, in the form most of us know it, a fabricated story. The claim has three load-bearing pillars: that lemmings migrate in coordinated hordes, that they deliberately choose death, and that this behavior is a natural phenomenon. Each one collapses under inspection.

Before we tear it down, though, it is worth noting that the suicide myth did not spring from nowhere. It grew out of something real — the genuine, genuinely baffling population cycles of Arctic lemmings — and was then fertilized by centuries of folklore and, finally, manufactured into "documentary fact" by Hollywood.

## Meet the Actual Lemming

Real lemmings are small, stout-bodied rodents of the Arctic and subarctic tundra — close relatives of voles and hamsters, built for a life at the top of the world. They live across the northern reaches of North America, Scandinavia, and Russia, in a landscape of snow, moss, and bitter cold that would kill most mammals. Lemmings do not hibernate. They stay active all winter, living in extensive burrow systems beneath the snowpack, where the insulation of the snow keeps their tunnels a relatively cozy place to raise young. Their diet is unglamorous: mosses, grasses, and sedges, eaten around the clock.

They are solitary and famously bad-tempered for such small animals — aggressive toward each other, especially when crowded, which matters more than you might think. And they are prolific breeders. Under good conditions — plentiful food, mild weather, few predators — lemming populations can explode by 100 or even 1,000 times their original size, according to research reported by ScienceDaily on the animals' population dynamics. Then they crash. The boom-and-bust cycle typically runs on a rhythm of about three to four years.

For more than half a century, this cycle was one of ecology's great mysteries. A major study published in the journal Science, led by researcher Olivier Gilg of the University of Helsinki, found that the four-year cycle of collared lemmings in Greenland is driven largely by predators — the stoat, the Arctic fox, the snowy owl, and the long-tailed skua — rather than by food shortages or any lemming death wish. The stoat, a short-tailed weasel that can hunt lemmings beneath the snowpack, plays an especially important role, with its delayed numerical response driving the cyclic rise and fall. Field monitoring on Bylot Island in the Canadian Arctic has documented the same pattern: three to four years between population peaks, with predators and snow conditions shaping the cycle.

In other words, when lemming numbers collapse, it is mostly because things eat them — not because they volunteer.

## The Grain of Truth: Migrations, Swimming, and Accidental Drownings

So where did the suicide story come from? From a real behavior, observed from a distance and misunderstood up close.

When a lemming population peaks and the tundra can no longer comfortably support the crowd, the animals disperse. This is not an organized migration like wildebeest crossing the Serengeti; scientists describe it as a mass dispersal — large numbers of hungry, stressed lemmings moving outward in all directions in search of new territory and food. During these chaotic movements, the animals push through whatever lies in their path: rivers, lakes, rocky ground. Their drive to keep moving can carry them across bodies of water up to 200 meters wide.

Here is the important part: lemmings are strong swimmers, with dense, water-resistant fur. Most crossings succeed. But some do not. Animals misjudge the width of a lake, get caught in a current, or — in the crush of a dense crowd — are shoved off a ledge or cliff edge by the sheer pressure of the bodies behind them. These deaths are accidental, the byproduct of a desperate dispersal, not a decision.

It is easy to see how a 19th-century traveler, arriving at a riverbank littered with drowned rodents after one of these episodes, might conclude the animals had thrown themselves in on purpose. The lemming's population swings were so dramatic — valleys suddenly teeming with rodents, then suddenly empty — that people reached for dramatic explanations. And they had been reaching for dramatic explanations about lemmings for centuries before that.

## Centuries of Strange Folklore

The suicide myth is actually a relatively modern chapter in a much older book of lemming weirdness. As Wikipedia's lemming entry documents, misconceptions about these animals go back centuries. In 1532, the Bavarian geographer Jacob Ziegler proposed that lemmings fell out of the sky during stormy weather and died when the spring grass grew — spontaneous generation, delivered by thundercloud. The Danish naturalist Ole Worm pushed back, accepting the sky-fall observation but arguing the animals had been carried by wind rather than created by it; his dissections showed lemmings were anatomically ordinary rodents, kin to voles and hamsters, and Carl Linnaeus later confirmed their natural origin.

In the August 1877 issue of Popular Science Monthly, the myth took another step toward its modern form: lemmings were described as apparently swimming out into the Atlantic Ocean, with some speculating they were searching for the lost continent of Lemuria. The ingredients were all there — mass movement, water, death, a hint of the uncanny — decades before anyone had filmed a single lemming.

## White Wilderness: How Disney Manufactured a Mass Suicide

Then came 1958, and the myth got its defining visual. Disney's True-Life Adventure series released "White Wilderness," a nature documentary about the subarctic North. One sequence showed lemmings — dozens of them — streaming across the snow in a vast migrating column, reaching a cliff, and tumbling over the edge into the sea below, where the narrator suggested they would swim on until they drowned. It was riveting television. It won the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature. And it was, as investigations would later establish, entirely staged.

Here is what actually happened behind the cameras, pieced together from later reporting by outlets including Mental Floss, Gizmodo, the Nature Conservancy's blog, and a 1983 investigation by CBC producer Brian Vallee:

First, the film was shot in Alberta, Canada — a landlocked province nowhere near lemming habitat. Lemmings are not native to the area, so the production imported a few dozen animals for the shoot. Second, the species used does not migrate in the dramatic fashion shown. To fake the great migration, the crew placed the lemmings on a snow-covered turntable — essentially a lazy Susan — so that the same small group of animals running in circles would appear on film as an endless streaming column. Finally, the crew herded the animals to the bank of a nearby river and pushed or threw them in, filming the resulting plunge as a mass suicide into the "Arctic Sea." The survivors that made it to the far bank had been chucked into the water by people, not driven there by instinct.

It was, by today's standards, straightforward animal cruelty — and, as Gizmodo's coverage notes, it was of a piece with 1950s Hollywood, an era when studios routinely killed animals for footage. The difference here is that the victims were small enough, and the fiction entertaining enough, that the film was celebrated rather than condemned. It took a quarter-century for the staging to be fully exposed to the public.

## The Science That Replaced the Story

While the myth was busy conquering pop culture, field biologists were quietly solving the actual mystery of lemming cycles — and the answer turned out to be more elegant than any suicide pact.

In the high-Arctic tundra of Greenland, researchers spent years studying the collared lemming, the single rodent prey species in what scientists describe as one of the world's simplest vertebrate predator-prey communities. A study published in the journal Science found that the lemming's famous four-year boom-and-bust cycle is driven not by the rodents' own behavior, but by a guild of four predators: the stoat, the Arctic fox, the snowy owl, and the long-tailed skua. Each predator plays a different role at a different phase of the cycle. The snowy owl, for instance, breeds only when lemming densities are high enough at snowmelt; the Arctic fox preys steadily across densities; and the stoat — the most specialized of the four, a short-tailed weasel capable of hunting lemmings in their burrows beneath the winter snow — delivers the delayed killing blow that crashes the population and keeps it low for years afterward. According to the study's modeling, neither food nor space shortages were needed to explain the cycle; predation alone did the work.

Long-term monitoring at Bylot Island in the Canadian Arctic has painted the same picture from a different angle. Researchers there have run a snap-trapping census of brown and collared lemmings since 1994, documenting regular peaks every three to four years. When lemmings are abundant, the whole tundra community surges — foxes, owls, skuas, even non-predators like snow geese and shorebirds reproduce better. When the predators finish their work, the lemmings vanish again, not into the sea but into stomachs.

This is the real lemming story: not a rodent with a death wish, but a keystone prey species whose extraordinary fertility periodically feeds an entire Arctic food web. It is a far better story than the Disney version — and it has the advantage of being true.

## Why the Lie Outlived the Film

Debunked or not, "White Wilderness" had done its work. The image of lemmings pouring off a cliff was too powerful, too narratively perfect, to be dislodged by facts. The myth metastasized into the culture: "to call someone a lemming" became shorthand for a blind follower, as the Nature Conservancy's writeup notes. Politicians deployed it against opponents — Mental Floss records its use in a 2008 U.S. Senate campaign ad. It surfaced in a Blink-182 song. Cartoons, commercials, and video games ran with it. The 1991 puzzle game "Lemmings," in which players rescue hordes of marching rodents from walking off cliffs, cemented the image for a generation of gamers who never saw the documentary.

Each retelling sanded off another edge of the truth. By the time the myth reached the average person, the qualifiers were gone: not "some lemmings occasionally drown during dispersal" but "lemmings commit mass suicide." Not "a staged film" but "nature." The metaphor did the rest of the work — once "lemming" meant "mindless conformist," nobody had much incentive to check whether the underlying animal behavior was real. Questioning it would have spoiled a perfectly good insult.

Meanwhile, the actual science quietly moved on. Researchers mapped the predator-driven cycles. Field studies confirmed that dispersal deaths are accidents of geography and crowding. No biologist studying wild lemmings has ever documented a deliberate mass suicide — because there is no such behavior to document. The animal that the myth describes, a rodent with a collective death drive, does not exist.

## What Lemmings Really Do When Populations Explode

It is worth dwelling for a moment on the real drama, because it is more interesting than the fake one. A lemming population boom is an ecological event. When conditions align — good snow cover for insulated burrows, abundant sedges and mosses, a lull in predation — the solitary, short-tempered rodents breed at astonishing speed. Densities spike. The tundra, as researchers on Bylot Island describe it, comes alive: snowy owls arrive to breed, Arctic foxes den more successfully, skuas and jaegers feast. The entire predator community surges on the back of one small rodent's fertility.

Then the predators catch up — especially the stoat, hunting beneath the snow through the winter — and the population collapses. It is a brutal, elegant cycle, driven by teeth and hunger rather than despair. During the boom's peak, the excess animals scatter in every direction, swimming rivers and crossing lakes, some drowning by misadventure. It is not suicide. It is a traffic jam with casualties.

## The Verdict

**False.** Lemmings do not commit mass suicide. They do not march off cliffs on purpose, and they do not drown themselves by choice. What actually happens: populations boom and bust on roughly three-to-four-year cycles driven largely by predators; during peak years, crowded lemmings disperse in search of food and space; during these chaotic movements some individuals drown crossing water or are crushed off ledges by the press of the crowd — accidents, not suicides. The iconic footage that "proved" the myth — Disney's 1958 "White Wilderness" — was staged in Alberta with imported lemmings, a snow-covered turntable, and a crew that physically pushed the animals into a river. A 1983 CBC investigation by producer Brian Vallee exposed the fabrication, and biologists have never documented deliberate mass suicide in any wild lemming population.

The myth survives because it is useful, not because it is true. "Lemming" is a wonderful insult — compact, visual, faintly scientific-sounding. But the animal behind the insult deserves better. Real lemmings are tough, fertile, ill-tempered survivors of one of Earth's harshest environments, and their only collective death wish exists in a film reel from 1958. Every time someone calls a crowd of people lemmings, the irony is perfect and completely backwards: the humans are repeating a fiction without checking, which is exactly the behavior the insult is supposed to describe.

The lemmings, for their part, were never following anyone off a cliff. They were pushed. And the ones doing the pushing were holding cameras.
