Ten minutes a day. A few cheerful games on your phone. A sharper memory, faster thinking, a brain that stays young. That is the promise sold by brain-training apps, a category led for more than a decade by Lumosity and now crowded with rivals with names like Peak, Elevate, CogniFit, and BrainHQ. The apps are beautifully designed, the testimonials are glowing, and the pitch is almost irresistible: your brain is a muscle, and these games are its gym.

But a muscle is not a brain, and a game is not a workout. Over fifteen years, researchers have run the largest experiments ever attempted on this question, regulators have fined the industry's biggest name, and about seventy of the world's leading cognitive scientists have issued a public warning. The evidence they produced is remarkably consistent — and it is not what the advertisements say.

This is not a story about apps that do nothing. Brain training does something real, and that is exactly what makes the claim so slippery. The question is not whether you get better at the games. You do. The question is whether getting better at the games makes you better at anything else. On that question, the biggest trials, the largest reviews, and the longest follow-up study ever run all point the same way.

## The Promise on the Box

To understand why the claim matters, it helps to hear it in the industry's own words. Lumosity, the most famous brain-training company, once told customers that its games could improve performance at work and school, delay age-related cognitive decline, protect against dementia and Alzheimer's disease, and reduce cognitive impairment associated with conditions including stroke, traumatic brain injury, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Its advertising ran on CNN, Fox News, and National Public Radio, and its games were marketed to a generation of baby boomers worried about their memories.

The promise was not subtle, and it was enormously lucrative. Lumosity charged about $15 a month, or up to $300 for lifetime access, and at its peak it claimed tens of millions of users. Rival apps made similar claims in softer language: train your memory, sharpen your focus, keep your brain young. The pitch was always the same shape — a small daily habit, a measurable score that climbs, and a better brain on the other side.

The scientific problem with that pitch is a single word: transfer. Psychologists distinguish between two kinds. Near transfer means getting better at tasks very close to the one you practiced — train on one memory game and you improve on a slightly different memory game. Far transfer means getting better at something quite different, such as general reasoning, school results, or everyday functioning. Brain-training apps are sold on far transfer. The research mostly finds near transfer. That gap, between what you practice and what you are promised, is the whole story. It is the same gap that keeps other brain myths alive — we have already checked the claim that <a href="/blog/do-we-only-use-10-percent-of-our-brain-fact-check/">we only use 10 percent of our brains</a>, and found a slogan that outlived the science behind it.

## The 11,430-Person Experiment

The decisive test came in 2010, and its scale has never been matched. Adrian Owen and colleagues at the Medical Research Council's Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit in Cambridge ran a six-week online experiment with viewers of the BBC science show *Bang Goes the Theory*. A total of 52,617 people registered. Of those, 11,430 completed both rounds of testing and at least two full training sessions — a sample so large that it could detect even small effects.

Participants were randomly assigned to train on reasoning, memory, planning, or visuospatial tasks, and they were tested on a whole battery of cognitive tests before and after the six weeks. The results, published in *Nature*, were clean and unambiguous. Everyone improved on the tasks they trained. Almost nobody improved on anything else — not even on untrained tasks in the same cognitive domain. The control group, which did not train, improved by similar amounts on some measures, suggesting that part of what looked like progress was simply practice with the tests themselves.

The Owen trial is the single most important study in this field because of what it rules out. If brain games produced general cognitive benefits, a trial of 11,430 people training for six weeks should have found them. It found that the games make you better at the games. That is a real effect, and it is also the whole effect, as far as this experiment could measure.

## What the Reviews Found

Single studies can mislead, so the field's verdict rests on reviews that pool dozens of them. The most influential came in 2016, when Daniel Simons and six colleagues reviewed more than 130 studies — including the studies the brain-game companies themselves cited as evidence — and published their conclusions in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Their finding was that people improve on the trained tasks, improve less on closely related tasks, and show very little evidence of improvement on distant tasks or in everyday life.

The same year, Monica Melby-Lervåg and colleagues published a meta-analysis of 87 publications on working-memory training, the most common ingredient in brain-training apps. They found short-term gains in working memory itself, but no convincing far transfer when the training was compared with an active control group — a group that did something equally engaging but different, which is the only fair comparison, because it controls for the placebo effect of expecting to improve.

In 2019, Giovanni Sala and Fernand Gobet pooled the pooled evidence in a second-order meta-analysis, a study of meta-analyses covering chess, music, video games, and working-memory training. Their conclusion was stark: once placebo effects and publication bias were controlled, the far-transfer effect and its true variance both came out at zero. In other words, when you account for the fact that people who expect to get smarter tend to perform slightly better, and that positive results are more likely to be published than null ones, the general benefit of brain training disappears.

This is why the claim is misleading rather than simply false. The near-transfer effect is real. Practicing a memory game does improve your memory for that kind of task, and the improvement is not trivial. But the advertised benefit — a generally sharper mind, better performance at work and school, protection against decline — is the far-transfer claim, and that is the part the evidence does not support.

## The Honest Exception: The ACTIVE Trial

There is one study that complicates the picture, and any honest fact-check has to give it its due. The ACTIVE trial — Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly — is the longest and most careful study of cognitive training ever run. It began in the late 1990s and randomized 2,832 adults aged 65 to 94, with an average age of 74, to ten sessions of memory, reasoning, or speed-of-processing training, or to no training. The training was structured and supervised, delivered in person by a human trainer over about five weeks, with progressive difficulty and booster sessions later. It was not an app.

The results, reported by Ball and colleagues in *JAMA* in 2002 and followed up for a decade by Rebok and colleagues in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* in 2014, were the best news the field has ever produced — and also a lesson in how narrow good news can be. At two years, the researchers detected no effect on everyday functioning. At ten years, 73.6 percent of the reasoning group still performed above their starting level on the trained ability, against 61.7 percent of controls, and 70.7 percent against 48.8 percent for the speed group. Memory training showed no difference at all. Participants in the training groups reported less difficulty with everyday tasks than controls — but the standard performance-based tests of function conducted by the researchers showed no difference between the groups.

Then, in February 2026, researchers linked ACTIVE participants to Medicare records from 1999 to 2019 and published the follow-up. Among the 2,021 participants enrolled in traditional Medicare at the start, those who did speed training *and* completed at least one booster session were about 25 percent less likely to be diagnosed with dementia than the control group. Speed training without boosters showed no difference, and neither did memory or reasoning training.

That is a genuinely interesting result, and it is the strongest positive evidence in the entire field. It is also, for the purposes of this fact-check, almost entirely beside the point. ACTIVE trained one specific visual speed task, in supervised sessions, in older adults, with human coaching and booster sessions. It did not test a commercial product. It is not evidence that Lumosity works, or that any app works, and the researchers themselves have been careful to say so. The most generous reading is that a specific kind of structured, supervised speed training, with boosters, may carry a long-term benefit for older adults. The least generous reading is that it comes from a single trial and needs confirming in others. Either way, it is not a license to sell ten minutes of phone games as brain insurance.

## The $2 Million Fine

If the science was ambiguous, the regulatory record is not. On January 5, 2016, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission announced that Lumos Labs, the company behind Lumosity, would pay $2 million to settle charges of deceptive advertising. A $50 million judgment was entered and suspended because of the company's financial condition, on the condition that the payment was made. The case, *Federal Trade Commission v. Lumos Labs Inc.*, was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California.

The FTC's charge was that Lumosity had claimed, without adequate evidence, that its games would improve performance on everyday tasks, in school, at work, and in athletics; delay age-related cognitive decline and protect against mild cognitive impairment, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease; and reduce cognitive impairment associated with health conditions including stroke, traumatic brain injury, post-traumatic stress disorder, ADHD, the side effects of chemotherapy, and Turner syndrome — and that scientific studies proved these benefits.

The complaint also alleged that Lumosity had failed to disclose that some of its glowing customer testimonials were solicited through contests offering prizes such as an iPad, a lifetime membership, and a round-trip flight to San Francisco. Under the settlement, Lumos Labs had to notify subscribers who had signed up for auto-renewed subscriptions between January 1, 2009 and December 31, 2014, and give them an easy way to cancel.

Jessica Rich, then the director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, put the agency's view bluntly: "Lumosity preyed on consumers' fears about age-related cognitive decline, suggesting their games could stave off memory loss, dementia, and even Alzheimer's disease. But Lumosity simply did not have the science to back up its ads."

A settlement is not a finding of fact — Lumos Labs neither admitted nor denied the allegations — and the settlement covered Lumosity's advertising up to 2016, not its current marketing. But the order also bars the company from making future claims about the real-world, age-related, and health benefits of its training without competent and reliable scientific evidence. It remains the clearest official statement ever made about the gap between what brain-training ads promise and what brain-training studies show. It was also, at the time, the first government settlement with a maker of apps intended to boost brain health.

## The Scientists' Warning — and the Dissent

Two years before the FTC acted, the scientific community had already spoken. On October 20, 2014, the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Berlin Max Planck Institute for Human Development published a consensus statement signed by about seventy of the world's leading cognitive psychologists and neuroscientists. Its verdict was unsparing: "To date, there is little evidence that playing brain games improves underlying broad cognitive abilities, or that it enables one to better navigate a complex realm of everyday life. No studies have demonstrated that playing brain games cures or prevents Alzheimer's disease or other forms of dementia."

The signatories went further, objecting directly to the industry's central claim: "We object to the claim that brain games offer consumers a scientifically grounded avenue to reduce or reverse cognitive decline when there is no compelling scientific evidence to date that they do."

That statement deserves to be quoted in full because it is the closest thing this field has to a verdict from the people who study the aging mind for a living. But honesty also requires reporting the dissent. A group of cognitive-training researchers published an open letter in response, agreeing that many companies had not subjected their exercises to peer-reviewed trials and that claims promoting brain games are frequently exaggerated and often misleading — but objecting to the word "consensus" and arguing that a substantial body of evidence shows certain training regimens can improve cognitive function, including in ways that generalize to everyday life. A 2014 review of 52 brain-training studies in *PLOS Medicine* by Amit Lampit and colleagues found a similarly mixed picture: some benefits for specific trained skills in older adults, little support for the idea that playing the games alone on a computer sharpens the mind in general.

That disagreement is real, and it is worth stating plainly. The field is not unanimous. What is true is that the skeptics have the largest trials and the most comprehensive reviews on their side, while the optimists have smaller studies, specific training regimens, and supervised laboratory conditions — none of which look much like a ten-minute phone game. The same pattern — a real laboratory finding stretched into a sweeping promise — is behind the <a href="/blog/does-listening-to-mozart-make-babies-smarter-fact-check/">Mozart effect, the claim that listening to Mozart makes babies smarter</a>, which we have also fact-checked.

## The Placebo Problem

There is one more wrinkle that makes the brain-training literature hard to read, and it cuts against the apps. In 2016, researchers at George Mason University ran a small experiment with fifty people. Everyone did the same one-hour training session. Only the twenty-five who had been recruited by a flyer promising brain training improved on two intelligence tests afterward. The training was identical. The expectation was different.

That is the placebo effect, and it is the reason serious trials use active controls — a comparison group that does something equally engaging, like crosswords, and expects to improve just as much. When brain training is compared with a passive control group that does nothing, it often looks effective. When it is compared with an active control, the advantage usually shrinks or vanishes. The Melby-Lervåg meta-analysis and the Sala and Gobet review both found exactly that pattern, which is why they concluded that far transfer is not convincingly demonstrated.

It is also the reason to be cautious about the industry's favorite study. In 2015, Hardy and colleagues randomized 4,715 participants to Lumosity training or to crossword puzzles as an active control, and reported a positive effect of d = 0.255 on a cognitive battery. That is a real, properly controlled result, and it is the strongest evidence the company has ever produced. It is also a study whose author list includes a Lumosity co-founder, and an effect size of 0.255 — a small effect, on a test battery, after weeks of training — is a long way from the advertised promise of a sharper mind at work and protection against dementia. Small effects on laboratory tests are not the same as meaningful changes in daily life, and one company-linked study does not outweigh the pooled evidence of more than a hundred others.

## The Prescription Exception

There is one recent development that genuinely changes the picture — but only for a narrow group of people. In December 2025, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration cleared LumosityRx, a prescription app for adults aged 22 to 55 with ADHD. It uses thirteen games and was tested in a sham-controlled trial of more than five hundred people, where it improved scores on a computerized attention test.

That clearance matters, and it is worth understanding precisely why. LumosityRx is a regulated medical device, prescribed for a diagnosed condition, tested against a sham control, and cleared for a specific, measured outcome — performance on an attention test — in a specific population. That is the opposite of the consumer pitch: a general promise, made to healthy adults, of a generally sharper brain. A prescription device that helps some adults with ADHD focus on a test does not validate the claim that brain games make healthy people smarter. If anything, it proves the point the critics have been making all along: when the claim gets specific and the testing gets rigorous, the benefit gets narrow.

## What Actually Works

If ten minutes of phone games will not sharpen your mind, what will? The Stanford consensus statement's own recommendation, based largely on correlational findings, was that people lead physically active, intellectually challenging, and socially engaged lives. That advice sounds boring next to a gamified app, but it has something the apps do not: evidence across whole populations.

Regular aerobic exercise is the strongest single candidate. Johns Hopkins Medicine notes that thirty minutes of cardio on most days consistently improves memory, attention, processing speed, and executive function across age groups — the very abilities the apps claim to train. Sleep, too, does more for next-day cognition than any game, and the same is true of learning genuinely hard things: a language, an instrument, a complex skill with real-world feedback. The irony is that the activities the apps displace — going for a walk, calling a friend, practicing something difficult — are closer to what the science recommends than the apps themselves.

None of that means you should delete the apps. They are entertaining, they are harmless for most people, and getting better at a memory game is a legitimate small pleasure — the same way doing a crossword is. The problem was never the games. It was the promise stapled to them: that a high score means a better brain, and that a subscription is an investment in your future self. Treat the games as games, and the evidence stops being a disappointment.

## The Verdict

So, do brain training apps work? It depends entirely on what you mean by "work." If you mean the games make you better at the games, yes — reliably, measurably, and sometimes durably. If you mean they make you smarter in general, improve your performance at work or school, or protect you against cognitive decline and dementia, the largest experiment ever run found no such effect, the largest reviews found no convincing far transfer, and the only long-term trial that found anything found it for one specific supervised speed exercise in older adults, with booster sessions, in a laboratory — not in an app.

The claim as advertised is therefore **Misleading**. It takes a real effect — near transfer, the improvement on trained tasks — and presents it as a general one. It borrows the authority of the ACTIVE trial, which tested supervised training in older adults, and applies it to a product the trial never tested. And in the case of Lumosity, it once made those claims so aggressively that the Federal Trade Commission charged the company with deceptive advertising and collected two million dollars.

The apps are not a scam and they are not useless. They are a modest, enjoyable way to practice specific skills — sold, for years, as something much bigger than that. The science has now said, in the largest trial, in the pooled reviews, and in a regulator's settlement, what that something bigger is: a game, not a treatment.

## Why This Myth Persists

Brain-training apps survive their own evidence for reasons that have little to do with science. The score goes up, and a rising score feels like progress — the app shows you a graph, and the graph never lies about the game, even when it says nothing about your brain. Brain myths are durable in general: we have previously found that the claim that <a href="/blog/does-alcohol-kill-brain-cells-fact-check/">alcohol kills brain cells</a> is also more complicated than its slogan suggests, and it survives for the same reason — a simple story that feels true. The games are also genuinely absorbing, and absorption feels like effort, and effort feels like improvement. Add a testimonial from a satisfied customer, a celebrity endorsement, and the anxiety of an aging population, and the pitch sells itself.

The claim also borrows credibility from a neighboring truth. Cognitive training *can* work, in specific forms, for specific people, under supervised conditions — the ACTIVE trial proved that, narrowly. The apps take that narrow, supervised, laboratory result and stretch it across a product the trial never tested, for a population the trial never studied, at a dose the trial never used. That is the structure of the misleading claim: a real finding, carried one step further than the evidence allows, and then another, until the promise on the box bears no resemblance to the study in the footnote.

If you enjoy the games, play them. Just don't confuse the score with your brain. The best evidence we have says that a walk, a good night's sleep, and something genuinely hard to learn will do more for your mind than ten minutes of tapping at a screen — and none of them will send you a push notification to remind you.
