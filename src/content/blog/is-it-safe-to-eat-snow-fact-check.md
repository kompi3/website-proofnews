Every winter, the same scene plays out in backyards across the Northern Hemisphere. The first real snowfall arrives, a child tips their head back and catches flakes on their tongue, and an adult nearby says the line everyone has heard a hundred times: don't eat the yellow snow. The joke is older than most of the people telling it. The question underneath it, though, is a serious one, and it gets asked millions of times every winter: is the white stuff actually safe to eat?

Snow looks like the purest substance on Earth. It falls from the sky, it is blindingly white, and it melts into plain water. It feels like nature's own filtered drinking water, delivered free of charge. That appearance is exactly why the claim that snow is clean and safe to eat has survived for so long. It is also why the claim is misleading. Snow is not pure frozen water. From the moment a flake forms high in a cloud to the moment it lands on the ground, it is collecting things — dust, soot, exhaust chemicals, bacteria, and, in the modern world, microscopic plastic and so-called forever chemicals. A single mouthful of clean, fresh snow is unlikely to hurt a healthy adult. But "snow is safe to eat" as a general statement is wrong, and in some situations — large quantities, city snow, snow eaten for survival — it is actively bad advice.

## The Claim: Snow Is Pure, So Eating It Is Safe

The idea that snow is safe to eat rests on a simple and appealing piece of logic. Snow is water that evaporated from the Earth's surface, rose into the sky, froze, and fell back down. Evaporation is nature's distillation process, so the water in a snowflake should be cleaner than the water in a tap. Add the fact that snow is cold — and cold kills germs, or so the folk wisdom goes — and you have a food that seems self-purifying.

The tradition of eating snow is genuinely old. Appalachian families have made "snow cream" for generations, churning fresh snow with milk, sugar, and vanilla into a rough ice cream. Children have always caught flakes on their tongues. Survival manuals have long listed snow as an emergency water source. And the most famous warning about snow-eating comes from pop culture rather than public health: Frank Zappa's 1974 song "Don't Eat the Yellow Snow" turned a piece of playground common sense into a catchphrase that has outlived the song itself. The joke works because everyone understands the rule it implies — yellow snow is contaminated — and the unspoken corollary that white snow is fine.

That corollary is the part that needs checking. Is white snow actually clean? The short answer from hydrologists, doctors, and environmental scientists is: sometimes, a little, in the right place. The longer answer is that snow is one of the most effective collectors of airborne pollution that nature has ever produced, and the modern atmosphere gives it plenty to collect.

## How a Snowflake Is Born Dirty

A snowflake does not start out as pure water. As meteorologist Mary Scarzello Fairbanks explained to Prevention in 2021, snow forms when water droplets freeze around some dust or debris in the air. Every flake needs a nucleus — a tiny particle — to crystallise around. That nucleus can be a speck of desert dust, a grain of pollen, a particle of soot, or a fragment of sea salt carried inland on the wind. From its very first second of existence, a snowflake is water wrapped around something that was floating in the air.

Then the flake falls, and the collecting continues. On its way down it sweeps through the lower atmosphere, gathering whatever is hanging there: dirt, soot, and even bacterial particles. Depending on the region, snow can also contain sulfates, nitrates, formaldehyde, mercury, or pesticides. If it is windy, snow can mix with dirt kicked up from the soil before it settles. By the time a flake reaches your tongue, it has travelled through kilometres of air and picked up a souvenir from every layer.

Scientists call this process wet deposition, and it is one of the ways the atmosphere cleans itself. Rain and snow scrub pollutants out of the air as they fall, then deposit them on the ground along with the water. That is good news for the air you breathe after a snowfall — the air really is cleaner. It is less good news for the snow itself, which has done the scrubbing. The same problem applies to rain: we previously examined whether <a href="/blog/is-rainwater-safe-to-drink-fact-check/">rainwater is safe to drink</a>, and found that it, too, picks up contaminants on its way down. Cleveland Clinic physician assistant Max Huddleston puts it plainly: as snow forms, it collects bits of dust or pollen, and as it falls to Earth, it gathers small chemical contaminants. The snow that looks whitest is often the snow that has just finished doing the atmosphere's housekeeping.

Once snow lands, the collecting does not stop. Snow on the ground absorbs whatever is around it: exhaust particles from a nearby road, salt spread on a sidewalk, fertiliser from a lawn, droppings from a passing dog. Bacteria such as E. coli can live in spots where animal waste has landed, and snow packed around those spots can pick up the microbes. The longer snow sits, the more it accumulates, and melting snow is more likely to carry pollutants and runoff from soil than fresh powder is.

## What the Studies Actually Found

This is not just theory. Researchers have spent the last decade measuring exactly what ends up in snow, and the results are consistent: even snow that looks pristine is carrying a chemical and biological load. The table below summarises four key findings.

| Year | Study or source | What researchers found in snow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2015 | Study in *Environmental Science: Processes & Impacts* | A Canadian team simulated snowfall in the presence of common traffic pollutants and found that concentrations of BTEX chemicals — benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene — rose significantly in the snow after just one hour. |
| 2018 | Study in Northern Ireland | Scientists found a variety of bacterial strains in freshly fallen snow, including bacteria capable of causing infections in humans. |
| 2019 | Alfred Wegener Institute | Researchers found microplastics in snow even in remote locations in the Arctic and the Alps — places far from any obvious source of plastic pollution. |
| 2020 | Study in *Environmental Science & Technology* | Even snow in the most pristine regions of the world contained traces of pesticides and other pollutants, showing that no snowfall is entirely free of human-made chemicals. |

The 2015 Canadian experiment is the most striking of the four, because it tested the exact scenario people imagine when they eat snow: fresh flakes, falling now, in a place with traffic nearby. The researchers did not wait for snow to sit on the ground for days and soak up grime. They simulated a snowfall in polluted air and measured what the flakes picked up on the way down. Within a single hour, the concentrations of benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene — the BTEX compounds found in gasoline exhaust — had increased significantly in the snow. The flakes were trapping the chemicals in their ice structure and dissolving them in tiny pockets of liquid water inside the snowpack. Fresh snow, in other words, is not a blank slate. It is a sponge.

The 2019 microplastics finding extends the problem to the most remote places on the planet. The Alfred Wegener Institute team found tiny plastic fragments in snow samples from the Arctic and the Alps, carried there by wind and deposited with the snowfall. Microplastics are now so widespread in the atmosphere that there is effectively no snow on Earth that is guaranteed free of them. The amounts in a single mouthful are tiny, and nobody has shown that eating a handful of snow delivers a harmful dose of plastic. But the finding destroys the premise that snow is pure. It is not pure in the Alps. It is not pure in the Arctic. It is certainly not pure beside a highway.

The 2018 Northern Ireland study adds a biological dimension. Researchers found a variety of bacterial strains in freshly fallen snow, including strains that can cause infections in humans. One organism repeatedly detected in snow is *Pseudomonas syringae*, an environmental bacterium that, if ingested, can trigger digestive problems — diarrhoea, vomiting, and stomach upset. Most microbes do not survive cold temperatures well, but cold does not sterilise snow, and bacteria sheltered inside a snowpack can persist. Eating snow can be particularly risky for people with weakened immune systems, such as young children or older people, because a dose of bacteria that a healthy adult shrugs off can make them ill.

## The First Flakes Are the Dirtiest

If you are going to eat snow, timing matters more than most people realise — and the timing is counterintuitive. The instinct is to rush out at the start of a snowfall and scoop the freshest snow before anyone else touches it. The evidence says the opposite. The first snow of a storm is the dirtiest snow of the storm.

The reason is the scrubbing effect. When snow starts falling, the air is at its dirtiest: it holds all the pollution that has accumulated since the last rain or snowfall. The first flakes sweep that pollution out of the sky, so they arrive carrying the heaviest load. As the storm continues, the air gets cleaner with every passing hour, and the later snow falls through progressively cleaner air. Climate researcher John Pomeroy explained the pattern to NPR in 2022, advising people to wait until a considerable amount of snow has fallen before sampling any of it. "The longer the snow falls, the lower the pollution levels in the air, and thus in the snow," he said.

Cleveland Clinic's Huddleston makes the same point from the medical side. The first layer of snow, and the first few hours of a snowfall, mix with everything already on the ground — salt, fertiliser, and whatever else is lying there — and the falling snow is busy purifying the air. "The first snowfall in the first few hours contains more toxins than snow that would fall a couple of hours later," he says. So the bowl of snow cream made from the first scoop of the season's first storm is, ironically, made from the most contaminated snow available. If you wait a few hours into a storm, choose snow from an undisturbed spot well away from roads, and take only the top layer, you are eating the cleanest snow the storm has to offer.

There is a second timing rule, too. Snow that has been sitting on the ground and starting to melt is worse than fresh snow, because melting concentrates pollutants and lets runoff from soil and surfaces seep in. The top, fluffy layer of white snow is the freshest and potentially cleanest layer. The deeper, older layers, and any snow that has been ploughed into a pile, should be left alone.

## City Snow and Country Snow Are Not the Same Snow

Where you eat snow matters as much as when. A snow hydrologist who studies this for a living, Steven Fassnacht of Colorado State University, was asked whether any snow is safe to eat, given atmospheric contaminants that now include forever chemicals from manufacturing, heavy metals from car emissions, and microplastics from virtually everything. His answer was honest and unsatisfying to anyone who wants a yes or no: "Most of the time you're probably OK, but you want to really be aware of where you are eating this snow."

The main contaminants Fassnacht sees in snow in his region are nitrogen- and sulfur-based compounds — nitrates, nitrites, sulfates, and sulfites — plus phosphorus-based equivalents, all of which come from emissions. They come out of the tailpipe and out of the smokestack. If you are downwind of a major industrial source, the likelihood of those compounds being in your snowpack is a lot higher. The same goes for dust and black carbon, which darken the snowpack, lower its reflectivity — what scientists call albedo — and speed up melting. In cities, the contaminant load is higher across the board. As Huddleston notes, the amount of contaminants a big city gives off is far greater than what a smaller town produces, thanks to the number of cars, trucks, buses, buildings, and factories.

Snow near streets and driveways is the worst of all. Vehicle exhaust releases tiny particles and chemicals that drift into nearby drifts, and studies of urban snow show it can absorb fuel components such as benzene and toluene from car exhaust, along with soot. Snow beside a road also picks up the chemicals spread to melt it. Road salt — often magnesium chloride or calcium chloride — dissolves into the snow and is not always visible. Eating snow containing road salt can cause vomiting and diarrhoea, and the salt is only one of the roadside contaminants; oil, petrol, and grit from traffic end up there too. Snow that has been shovelled, ploughed, or walked on should never be eaten, because it has had every chance to pick up oil or fuel from cars and whatever was on people's shoes.

Then there is the question of colour. The rule about yellow snow is real, but yellow is only one warning sign. Grey, brown, pink, orange, or blue snow should all be avoided. Pink snow — sometimes called watermelon snow — is coloured by an algae called *Chlamydomonas nivalis* that thrives in snow. The algae itself is not directly harmful to humans if ingested, but it tends to appear in melting snow, which is more likely to carry other dirt and contaminants. Yellow snow usually means urine, but as Huddleston points out, you also do not know what someone may have dumped onto snow to discolour it. The only snow that should ever be considered for eating is pristine white snow, and even that comes with conditions.

The forever chemicals deserve a mention of their own. Fassnacht confirms that microplastics and PFAS — the per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances known as forever chemicals because they barely break down in the environment — can be found in snow, depending on how they get there. These are relatively new areas of research, and scientists are still working out how much of these substances snow carries and what the health significance is. Nobody is claiming that a mouthful of snow delivers a dangerous dose of PFAS. But the presence of industrial chemicals that did not exist a century ago in a substance people still think of as nature's purest water shows how far the claim "snow is pure" has drifted from reality.

That brings us to the question of dose, which is where the honest answer to "is it safe to eat snow" actually lives. The contaminants found in snow are also present in the air we breathe, and as one analysis notes, there does not seem to be a surge of reports of illnesses related to snow consumption. Assuming you do not consume a huge quantity of it, it is unlikely that the contaminants in the snow will be present in large enough amounts to cause problems. A small bite of clean snow is, in the words of several experts, probably fine. The claim fails not because a single snowflake is poisonous, but because it presents snow as clean and safe without qualification — and the qualifications are doing most of the work.

## The Germs Hiding in the Drift

Chemicals are only half the story. Snow is also a habitat, and some of its residents can make you sick. The most common route is animal waste. Droppings and urine end up on or under the snow surface, and bacteria such as E. coli can live in those spots. Snow packed around them picks up the microbes, and eating contaminated snow can lead to stomach bugs, food poisoning, or an E. coli infection, with symptoms including severe stomach cramps, fever, vomiting, and diarrhoea. This is why experts warn against eating snow from areas where pets may have been, and against eating any snow with animal footprints, bird feathers, or other signs of disturbance on or near it.

The *Pseudomonas syringae* finding shows that contamination is not only a matter of what lands on the snow after it falls. Bacteria can be part of the snowfall itself, carried up into the clouds and frozen into the flakes. The 2018 Northern Ireland study that found infection-causing bacterial strains in freshly fallen snow is a reminder that "fresh" and "clean" are not the same thing when it comes to snow. Fresh snow has simply had less time to collect ground-level contamination; it has already collected whatever was in the air.

People with kidney or liver disease, serious heart problems, or chronic lung conditions may react more strongly to impurities in snow, and children and older people are more vulnerable to the bacteria it can carry. That matters because children are the people most likely to eat snow in the first place. A healthy adult who eats a spoonful of clean snow is taking a very small risk. A small child who eats handfuls of snow from a city park is taking a larger one, and the snow they are eating is likely to be the dirtiest kind: snow near paths, near roads, in a place where dogs walk and cars park.

## Why Eating Snow Can Leave You Thirstier and Colder

There is a second, separate reason the "snow is safe to eat" claim is misleading, and it has nothing to do with pollution. Even perfectly clean snow is a bad way to hydrate, and in the wrong circumstances it is dangerous. The problem is temperature.

Snow is cold, and it contains a lot of air. Your body has to spend energy to melt it into a drinkable form, using your own body heat to do the work. As Huddleston explains, any hydration you get from eating or drinking snow is not as great as the energy you are using to melt it. Over time, if you are consuming a high quantity of snow, it can dehydrate you in theory — you are losing more water and heat than you are gaining. A heaped bowl of snow also melts down to a surprisingly small puddle, because snow is mostly air, so the amount of water you actually get for all that effort is small. And snow contains no electrolytes or minerals, so in larger amounts it can even dilute the body's mineral and electrolyte balance.

The cooling effect is the serious part. Eating a handful of unmelted snow chills the mouth, throat, and stomach, which can lower body temperature little by little. For a child playing in the backyard who sneaks a few flakes, that is not a concern. But for someone depending on snow as a source of hydration — a hiker who is lost, a driver stranded in a storm — it is a genuine hazard. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention warns against eating snow for hydration when stranded, because the cooling effect works against you when you are already cold. Consuming large amounts of snow can decrease your overall body temperature and increase the risk of hypothermia. Prolonged contact with cold snow can even cause frostbite on the lips or mouth. The same counterintuitive lesson applies to other water that looks drinkable but is not: we have also checked whether <a href="/blog/can-you-drink-seawater-fact-check/">you can drink seawater</a>, and found that it, too, makes you thirstier rather than less thirsty.

The CDC's advice for emergencies points to the correct way to use snow as water: collect it and boil it. Boiling kills the germs, and melting it over a fire or stove means your body does not have to pay the heat cost. Melted and boiled, snow is a perfectly good emergency water source. Eaten raw, by the handful, in a survival situation, it is a liability. That distinction is exactly the kind of nuance the simple claim "snow is safe to eat" leaves out.

## What the Claim Gets Right — and Where It Goes Wrong

To be fair to the claim, it is not entirely wrong, and understanding what it gets right is part of giving a fair verdict. Snow really is water that evaporated from the Earth's surface and froze in the sky, and the evaporation step really does leave most dissolved salts and heavy contaminants behind. A snowflake that forms high in a clean atmosphere and falls on a remote mountainside is about as close to pure natural water as you will find. People have eaten snow for generations without obvious harm, and doctors do not report waves of snow-eating illness every winter. The experts quoted in this article — the Cleveland Clinic, a Colorado State hydrologist, a Canadian climate researcher — all agree that a small amount of clean snow is probably fine for a healthy person. That is the true core inside the claim.

Where the claim goes wrong is in the leap from that narrow truth to a general rule. It treats snow as pure when snow is, by the physics of how it forms, a collector. It ignores where the snow fell, when in the storm it fell, how long it has been sitting, and how much of it you eat. It ignores the difference between a spoonful of clean snow in a mountain meadow and a handful of grey slush beside a salted road. And it ignores the thermal cost of eating snow, which makes it a poor and potentially dangerous source of hydration in exactly the situation where people most often rely on it. A claim that is true only when you add four or five qualifications — clean location, later in the storm, top layer only, small amounts only, healthy adults only — is not a claim that can be stated without them. That is the definition of misleading. And if the question of what counts as clean drinking water interests you, our earlier check of whether <a href="/blog/is-bottled-water-safer-than-tap-water-fact-check/">bottled water is safer than tap water</a> found a similar pattern: the reassuring label and the reality do not always match.

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Snow is pure frozen water. | Snow forms around dust and collects soot, exhaust chemicals, bacteria, microplastics, and forever chemicals as it falls. |
| White snow is clean snow. | Even pristine-looking snow in the Arctic and the Alps contains microplastics and traces of pesticides. |
| Fresh snow is the safest snow. | The first hours of a snowfall carry the most pollution, because the first flakes scrub the dirtiest air. |
| Eating snow hydrates you. | Melting snow costs your body heat; large amounts can dehydrate you and lower your core temperature. |
| Snow is safe in an emergency. | The CDC warns against eating snow when stranded and advises melting and boiling it instead. |

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Is it safe to eat snow? A small bite of clean, fresh, white snow, taken hours into a snowfall in a quiet area away from roads, is usually low risk for a healthy adult — and the experts agree on that. But snow is not pure frozen water, and "safe to eat" is not a property of snow in general. Snowflakes form around airborne particles and collect sulfates, nitrates, exhaust chemicals, bacteria, microplastics, and forever chemicals on their way down. A Canadian experiment showed traffic pollutants building up significantly in snow within a single hour. The first snow of a storm carries the heaviest load, city snow carries more than country snow, and snow near roads can contain salt, oil, and fuel residues. Eating large amounts can dehydrate you and chill your core temperature, which is why the CDC warns against eating snow for hydration in survival situations. The claim takes a narrow truth — a little clean snow rarely hurts — and presents it as a general rule. That is what makes it misleading rather than true.

If you still want the winter ritual, here is the safer way to do it. Wait until the snow has been falling for a few hours, so the air has been scrubbed and the later flakes are cleaner. Choose an undisturbed spot well away from roads, driveways, and paths, somewhere you know animals have not been. Take only the top layer of fresh, pristine white snow — never snow that is yellow, grey, brown, pink, or blue, and never snow that has been shovelled, ploughed, or walked on. Keep it to a small taste, not a meal. And if you are cold, lost, or relying on snow for water, do not eat it at all: melt it and boil it first. The snow will still be there for catching on your tongue. Just know what you are catching.
