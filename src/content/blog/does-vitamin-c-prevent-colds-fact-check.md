It is the most familiar ritual of cold season. You feel the first tickle in your throat, and someone — a parent, a partner, a pharmacist, the internet — tells you to load up on vitamin C. Orange juice by the litre. Fizzy tablets dissolving in a glass. A chewable the size of a coin, taken three times a day until the sniffles surrender. The advice is so common that it barely feels like advice at all. It feels like common sense, the kind of thing everyone has always known.

But has anyone actually checked? The claim that vitamin C prevents colds has been tested more thoroughly than almost any other idea in the history of nutrition. Dozens of placebo-controlled trials, run over six decades, involving tens of thousands of people, have been gathered together and weighed in the most rigorous review science knows how to produce. And the answer those trials give is not the one on the supplement bottle. For most people, taking vitamin C every day does not stop colds from arriving. It does something smaller, stranger, and more interesting — and in one very specific group of people, it does something genuinely remarkable. The story of how a Nobel Prize winner turned a vitamin into a cold cure, and how the evidence quietly disagreed with him for fifty years, is a case study in why a claim can be believed by millions and still be wrong.

## The Claim: A Glass of Orange Juice Against the Common Cold

The claim comes in several strengths, and it matters which one you mean. The strongest version says that taking large doses of vitamin C will keep you from catching colds at all — that the vitamin is a shield, and the shielded do not get sick. A softer version says that vitamin C will make a cold shorter or milder once it arrives. A third version, the one most people actually practise, says that reaching for a mega-dose at the first sneeze will stop the cold in its tracks. These are three different claims, and the evidence treats them very differently. Two of them fail. One of them partly survives. The confusion between them is the whole reason the myth has lasted so long.

Walk into any pharmacy in winter and you will see the claim in its commercial form. Effervescent tablets promise "immune support." Bottles of ascorbic acid advertise doses of 1,000 milligrams — more than ten times the daily requirement for an adult — with pictures of oranges bursting with juice. The marketing never quite says "prevents colds" in those words, because regulators in most countries would not allow it. It says "supports your immune system" instead, and lets the customer fill in the rest. The customer does. Surveys of supplement use consistently find vitamin C among the most commonly taken supplements in the world, and the common cold is the reason most people give for taking it. The idea is not a fringe belief. It is the mainstream view of a vitamin, held by people who would never describe themselves as believers in alternative medicine.

That is what makes the claim worth checking with care. It is not obviously absurd. Vitamin C is a real nutrient with real jobs in the body. It helps make collagen, the protein that holds skin and blood vessels together. It helps wounds heal. It supports the normal function of the immune system, which is why a severe deficiency causes scurvy, the disease that rotted the gums and loosened the teeth of sailors on long voyages before anyone understood what was missing from their diet. The step from "this vitamin helps your immune system work" to "more of this vitamin will stop you catching colds" feels like a short, logical step. It is not. It is a leap, and the trials were designed to find out whether the landing holds.

## Where the Idea Came From: A Nobel Laureate and a Bestseller

The modern vitamin C craze has a single, traceable origin, and it is not a laboratory finding. It is a book, written by one of the most famous scientists of the twentieth century.

Linus Pauling was, by any measure, a giant. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954 for his work on the nature of the chemical bond, and the Nobel Peace Prize in 1962 for his campaigning against nuclear weapons testing. He remains the only person to have won two unshared Nobel Prizes. When Pauling spoke, the world listened, and in 1970 he spoke about vitamin C. His book, *Vitamin C and the Common Cold*, became an instant bestseller. In it, he argued that large daily doses of the vitamin — far larger than anyone had previously suggested — would substantially reduce both the number of colds a person caught and their severity.

Pauling had come to the idea through the biochemist Irwin Stone, who had been experimenting with vitamin C as a food preservative and became convinced that mega-doses were the secret to health and longevity. Stone told Pauling that high doses had helped him and his wife recover rapidly after a car crash. Pauling, who was in his late sixties and increasingly interested in health, adopted the regimen himself and became its most famous advocate. In his book he recommended taking at least 2.3 grams of vitamin C a day — the equivalent, by one contemporary calculation, of eating more than thirty oranges. The recommended daily amount for adults is 75 to 90 milligrams in the United States, and 40 milligrams in the United Kingdom. Pauling was proposing roughly twenty-five to fifty times the normal intake, every day, indefinitely.

The medical establishment was sceptical from the start, and the scepticism had a specific shape. Pauling's argument rested largely on his reading of early, small studies and on anecdote, including his own experience. Critics pointed out that he was not a physician, that he had not run the kind of large, controlled trial that could settle the question, and that he was promoting his conclusions in a popular book rather than through the slow machinery of peer review. But the public did not care about the machinery. A double Nobel laureate had said that a cheap, safe, natural substance could defeat the common cold, the most universal of human miseries. The book sold, the supplement industry boomed, and the idea entered the culture so deeply that half a century later, most people cannot remember a time before it.

Pauling never backed down. He expanded his claims over the years, arguing in later books that vitamin C could help prevent cardiovascular disease, combat ageing, and even treat cancer. He worked with the Scottish surgeon Ewan Cameron on vitamin C as a cancer treatment, and when rigorous trials at the Mayo Clinic, led by the oncologist Charles Moertel in the 1970s and 1980s, found no evidence that vitamin C helped cancer patients, Pauling argued that the trials had methodological flaws. He continued to advocate for mega-doses until his death in 1994, at the age of 93, of prostate cancer. His defenders note that he lived a long life. His critics note that a long life is not a clinical trial.

The question Pauling raised was a fair one, and it deserved a proper answer. It took decades to get one, because the answer required not one trial but all of them, gathered together and weighed honestly — including the trials that found nothing.

## The Definitive Test: 29 Trials, 11,306 People

The strongest answer to the vitamin C question comes from a Cochrane systematic review by Harri Hemilä of the University of Helsinki and Elizabeth Chalker, published in 2013. A Cochrane review is the closest thing medicine has to a final word: it searches the literature exhaustively, applies strict pre-set rules about which studies count, and pools the results of every qualifying randomised, placebo-controlled trial rather than cherry-picking the ones with the most exciting results. For a question as old and as studied as vitamin C and colds, it is the only kind of evidence that can cut through fifty years of argument.

Hemilä and Chalker gathered 72 placebo-controlled trials carried out since 1966, and excluded any trial that used less than 0.2 grams of vitamin C a day or had no placebo comparison. Twenty-nine of those trials, involving 11,306 participants, addressed the central question: does taking vitamin C every day reduce the number of colds you catch? The answer, pooled across all of them, was a relative risk of 0.97 in the general community trials, which covered 10,708 people. That means the vitamin C group caught colds at 97 percent of the rate of the placebo group — a 3 percent reduction that sits inside the margin of error, with a 95 percent confidence interval running from 0.94 to 1.00, compatible with a benefit as large as 6 percent or with no benefit at all. In plain language: regular vitamin C made no meaningful difference to how often ordinary, healthy people caught colds.

The reviewers stated their conclusion plainly. The failure of vitamin C supplementation to reduce the incidence of colds in the general population, they wrote, indicates that routine vitamin C supplementation is not justified. Hemilä himself has put it even more bluntly in interviews: "There is strong evidence that normal people cannot prevent colds with vitamin C." That is not the verdict of a single study that might be wrong. It is the verdict of twenty-nine studies, involving more than eleven thousand people, that agree with each other.

It is worth pausing on the scale of that evidence, because scale is what separates this fact-check from a dispute about one paper. Eleven thousand people is not a laboratory curiosity. It is a small town, followed through cold seasons, half of them swallowing vitamin C and half swallowing an identical-looking dummy pill, neither group knowing which they had. If vitamin C prevented colds in any way that mattered to daily life — if it cut your chances by a fifth, or a tenth, or even a twentieth — a study of that size would have found it. It found nothing of the kind. The effect, if it exists at all in the general population, is so small that it disappears into statistical noise.

That finding has been confirmed from every direction since. The United Kingdom's National Health Service, on its common cold page, states that there is little evidence that supplements such as vitamin C, echinacea or garlic prevent colds or help people get better more quickly. The United States National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements says that for most people, vitamin C supplements do not reduce the risk of getting a cold. These are not contrarian positions. They are the consensus position of the world's major public health bodies, and they rest on the same pile of trials.

## The One Exception: Marathoners, Skiers and Soldiers

If the story ended there, this would be a simple fact-check with a simple verdict. But the Cochrane review contains one of the most interesting exceptions in all of nutrition science, and it is the reason this article's verdict is "Misleading" rather than a flat "False."

Across five trials involving 598 people, regular vitamin C cut the risk of catching a cold roughly in half. The pooled relative risk was 0.48, with a confidence interval of 0.35 to 0.64 — a result far too large, and far too consistent, to be a statistical accident. But the people in those five trials were not ordinary people living ordinary lives. They were marathon runners, competitive skiers, and soldiers on subarctic exercises — people exposed to short bursts of extreme physical stress, often in bitter cold.

That detail matters enormously, and it is where the popular version of the claim goes wrong. The exception does not rescue the claim for the rest of us. It describes a specific physiological situation: intense, sustained exertion that temporarily suppresses immune function, combined with cold exposure, in people whose bodies are under unusual strain. In that narrow window, vitamin C appears to genuinely help. Outside it, in the daily life of office workers, schoolchildren, and retirees — the people who actually buy the supplements — the same vitamin does nothing measurable to stop colds arriving.

The exception has held up in later research. In 2019, a trial of 1,444 South Korean army recruits in basic training randomised the young soldiers to vitamin C or placebo for thirty days, and the vitamin C group developed fewer colds. The pattern is consistent: the harder the body is being pushed, the more vitamin C seems to matter. One plausible explanation is that extreme exercise burns through the body's vitamin C stores faster, and that people under that kind of stress may be running low in a way that ordinary people are not. Another is that the immune suppression caused by exhausting exercise creates a gap that the vitamin helps to close. Researchers still debate the mechanism. The result itself, though, has now been seen in six separate trials, and it is real.

This is also why the claim is misleading rather than simply false. If you are training for a marathon in winter, or heading out on a multi-day ski expedition, or shipping off to military training in a cold climate, the evidence says vitamin C might genuinely halve your chance of catching a cold. The supplement bottle in your kitchen does not know that. It markets the same promise to everyone, and for almost everyone who buys it, the promise does not hold.

## Shorter, Not Fewer: What Vitamin C Actually Does to a Cold

There is a second finding in the Cochrane review, and it is the one that explains why so many people are convinced, from personal experience, that vitamin C works. Regular supplementation does make colds slightly shorter. Pooling 31 comparisons covering 9,745 cold episodes, the review found that colds were about 8 percent shorter in adults and about 14 percent shorter in children who had been taking vitamin C regularly. In children taking 1 to 2 grams a day, the reduction reached 18 percent. A separate meta-analysis published in 2023 found that symptom severity was about 15 percent lower at doses of 1 gram a day or more, mostly on the worst days of the cold rather than the mild ones.

Eight percent sounds unimpressive until you translate it into days. On a seven-day cold, 8 percent is a little over half a day. On a ten-day cold, it is most of a day. That is not nothing. Half a day less of a sore throat and a blocked nose is a real benefit, and it is a benefit that a person can genuinely feel. If you take vitamin C all winter and your colds consistently end half a day sooner than your partner's, you will conclude — reasonably, from your own experience — that the vitamin is doing something. You will be right. You will simply be wrong about what it is doing. It is not stopping colds. It is trimming them, slightly, at the edges, and only if you were already taking it before the cold began.

That last condition is crucial, and it is the part of the evidence that almost nobody who buys vitamin C knows about. The shortening effect belongs to regular supplementation — taking the vitamin every day, before any cold arrives, as a background habit. It does not belong to the emergency dose. The trials that tested starting vitamin C only after symptoms appeared found no consistent benefit, and that finding is as solid as the rest of the review.

It is also worth being honest about how small the benefit is. An 8 percent reduction in duration is the kind of effect that vanishes entirely if you measure it in a single person rather than in thousands. Your colds vary in length from year to year for dozens of reasons — which virus you caught, how much sleep you had, the weather, pure chance. A half-day difference is invisible against that background noise. That is why personal experience is such an unreliable guide here, and why it took a review of eleven thousand people to see the effect at all. The vitamin does something. It just does far less than the bottle implies.

## Too Late Once You Are Sneezing: Starting After Symptoms

The most common way people actually use vitamin C is also the way the evidence supports least. The ritual is familiar: you wake up with a scratchy throat, you reach for the fizzy tablets, you double the dose, and you wait for the cold to retreat. The Cochrane review looked at exactly this pattern. Across seven comparisons covering 3,249 cold episodes, researchers tested vitamin C started only after cold symptoms had appeared, sometimes at doses of up to 4 grams a day. They found no consistent effect on how long the cold lasted or how bad it got.

This is the finding that most directly contradicts the marketing. The image on the packet — the person at the first sign of a sniffle, reaching for the tube — depicts the one use of vitamin C that the trials say does not work. It is not that the dose is too small. Trials used doses up to 4 grams a day, four times the amount in a typical 1,000-milligram tablet, and still found nothing consistent. It is not that researchers tested the wrong thing. They tested precisely what millions of people do, and the colds ran their usual course regardless.

Why would a vitamin that slightly shortens colds when taken in advance do nothing when taken at the first sneeze? The honest answer is that nobody fully knows, but the leading explanation is about timing. A cold is not a single event but a cascade: a virus enters the nose, invades cells, triggers inflammation, and the immune system responds over hours and days. Vitamin C is involved in immune function, but by the time you feel the first symptoms, the cascade is already well under way. The vitamin may help to keep the machinery running smoothly if it is already present in the tissues, but arriving after the alarm has sounded appears to be too late to change the course of the illness. Whatever the mechanism, the practical conclusion is clear. If you are going to take vitamin C for colds at all, taking it every day through the winter is the only version of the habit with any evidence behind it — and even then, the benefit is half a day off a cold you will still catch.

## How Much Is Too Much? Doses, Side Effects and "Expensive Urine"

Pauling's recommended dose — 2.3 grams a day, every day — raises a question that the Cochrane review does not answer on its own: is it safe to take that much? The short answer is that vitamin C is remarkably safe, but not entirely without consequences, and the consequences begin well below Pauling's dose.

Vitamin C is water-soluble, which means the body cannot store it. Whatever you take beyond what your tissues can use is excreted in the urine. That is the origin of the critics' favourite phrase about Pauling's regimen: "expensive urine." It is also why a daily intake of 40 milligrams in the United Kingdom, or 75 to 90 milligrams in the United States, is enough for most adults, and why a normal varied diet — citrus fruit, peppers, strawberries, broccoli, potatoes — supplies it easily. The body has no use for a gram of vitamin C a day, and no way to keep it. It simply passes through.

Above about 2 grams a day, the excess starts to cause trouble. Diarrhoea and gastrointestinal distress are common at doses over 2 grams a day, and the NHS warns that taking more than 1,000 milligrams a day from supplements can cause stomach pain, diarrhoea and flatulence. For most people these effects are mild and reversible, and they stop when the dose is reduced. But there are rarer concerns as well. Very high doses increase the amount of oxalate in the urine, which in susceptible people can contribute to kidney stones. People with certain conditions, including the iron-overload disorder haemochromatosis, are advised to be cautious, because vitamin C increases the absorption of iron from food. None of this makes vitamin C dangerous in the ordinary sense. It makes the mega-dose habit pointless in a way that is also mildly unpleasant: you pay for grams of a vitamin your body cannot use, and the main result is that you spend more time in the bathroom.

There is a useful comparison to make here, and it is one the supplement industry rarely invites. A single orange contains roughly 70 milligrams of vitamin C — nearly double the UK daily requirement. A red pepper contains more than twice that. The idea that you need a 1,000-milligram tablet to get enough vitamin C is strange when set against the contents of a salad. The food-first argument is not a piece of folk wisdom. It is the position of the NHS, which states that adults aged 19 to 64 need 40 milligrams of vitamin C a day and should usually be able to obtain all of it from a varied diet. If your diet is poor, the fix is a better diet, not a bigger pill.

## Why the Myth Refuses to Die

If the evidence is this clear, and has been this clear for more than a decade, why does the claim survive? The answer is not that people are foolish. It is that the claim is protected by a set of forces that have very little to do with evidence, and a great deal to do with how human beings think.

The first force is the Nobel halo. Pauling was not a crank. He was one of the greatest chemists of his century, and his authority spilled over from chemistry into nutrition in a way that no amount of careful reviewing could easily undo. When a double Nobel laureate tells you something, the burden of proof feels reversed: it feels as though the sceptics must prove him wrong, rather than him proving himself right. And in a sense the sceptics did prove him wrong, many times over, in trial after trial. But a correction published in a systematic review does not travel the way a bestseller does. Pauling's book reached millions. The Cochrane review reached specialists.

The second force is the half-day effect. As we have seen, regular vitamin C does shorten colds slightly, and people who take it can genuinely feel that. Personal experience is a powerful teacher, and it is a terrible statistician. If your colds are half a day shorter this winter than last winter, you will credit the vitamin, not the fact that this year's virus was milder, or that you slept better, or that chance simply favoured you. The small real effect acts as a shield for the large false claim. Every shortened cold feels like proof of prevention, and the distinction between "shorter" and "fewer" is exactly the kind of distinction that disappears in everyday conversation.

The third force is the structure of the claim itself. Colds are the perfect disease for a false remedy, because they always get better on their own. The average cold lasts a week to ten days, and the NHS notes that people often begin to feel better within one to two weeks. That means any remedy taken at the start of a cold will appear to work, because the cold would have ended anyway. The remedy gets the credit for the recovery, and the recovery, being inevitable, never contradicts the remedy. It is the same logic that keeps a hundred other cold cures alive, from hot toddies to steam inhalation. The cold leaves, the cure stays.

The fourth force is commercial. Vitamin C is cheap to make, safe to sell, and enormously profitable. The supplement industry has no incentive to correct the record, and every incentive to keep the claim alive in its softer, legally safer form: "supports immune health." That phrase is technically true — vitamin C does support normal immune function, in the same way that food supports normal immune function — and it is designed to be heard as something stronger. The gap between what the label says and what the customer hears is where the money is made.

And the fifth force is the simplest of all: the claim feels right. We associate vitamin C with oranges, and oranges with health, and health with protection. The chain of associations is pleasant, sunny, and entirely natural. It is also wrong. The body does not work by association. It works by biochemistry, and the biochemistry says that once you have enough vitamin C — which almost everyone in a developed country does — more of it does not buy you more immunity. It buys you expensive urine.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The claim that vitamin C prevents colds is not entirely false, and that is precisely what makes it misleading. Taken every day, vitamin C does slightly shorten colds — by about half a day in adults — and in people under short bursts of extreme physical stress, such as marathon runners, skiers and soldiers, it roughly halves the chance of catching a cold at all. But the claim as it is usually made and sold — that taking vitamin C, especially in large doses at the first sign of illness, will keep you from getting colds — is not supported by the evidence. Twenty-nine placebo-controlled trials involving 11,306 people found no meaningful reduction in how often ordinary people catch colds, and seven trials covering 3,249 cold episodes found no consistent benefit from starting vitamin C after symptoms appear. The vitamin is not a shield. It is, at best, a small pair of scissors, trimming a little off the end of a cold you were going to catch anyway.

That distinction matters, because it is the difference between a reasonable health habit and a wasted one. If you are a marathon runner training through winter, or a soldier heading into cold-weather exercises, the evidence gives you a genuine reason to take vitamin C. If you are an ordinary person reaching for a fizzy tablet at the first tickle in your throat, the evidence says you are spending money on a ritual, not a remedy. The orange juice is not harming you. It is simply not doing what you think it is doing.

The story of vitamin C and the common cold is, in the end, a story about the difference between a great scientist and a great claim. Pauling was right that vitamin C matters, right that it supports the immune system, and right that it does something to colds. He was wrong about how much, and about whom, and about when. Half a century of trials has refined his idea into something smaller and more precise than the bestseller promised — and something more honest. A half-day shorter cold is a real benefit. It is just not a cure, and it was never a shield. The next time someone offers you a mega-dose at the first sneeze, you can thank them, drink the orange juice if you like it, and know that the cold will run its course either way. The evidence has been saying so for years. It is time we listened.

## What to Do Instead

If vitamin C is not the answer, what is? The honest answer is that there is no pill that prevents the common cold, and the things that do help are unglamorous. Rest, fluids, and time are the treatment the NHS recommends, and the cold will usually resolve within one to two weeks. Washing your hands and avoiding close contact with people who are ill reduces your chance of catching the virus in the first place, which is the one form of prevention that actually works. Eating a varied diet rich in fruit and vegetables gives you all the vitamin C you need, with no tablets required — and the food brings fibre, flavour, and other nutrients the pill does not.

For the sore throat and the cough that come with a cold, other home remedies have been examined in their own trials, with their own mixed results. We have checked whether [chicken soup helps a cold](/blog/does-chicken-soup-help-a-cold-fact-check/), whether [honey helps a cough](/blog/does-honey-help-a-cough-fact-check/), and whether [vitamin D supplements prevent colds and flu](/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/) — each claim with its own evidence, and each verdict with its own surprises. None of them is a cure either. But knowing which ones do a little, and which ones do nothing at all, is the difference between treating a cold and merely performing a ritual while you wait for it to end.
