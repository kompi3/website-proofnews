You have probably heard it from a parent, a teacher, a doctor, or a cereal box: breakfast is the most important meal of the day. It is one of those sentences that arrives in childhood and never quite leaves, like "an apple a day keeps the doctor away" or "you have to drink eight glasses of water a day." It sounds like nutritional wisdom handed down through the ages. It turns out, however, that this particular piece of wisdom has a paper trail — and the paper trail leads to an advertising department.

The claim that breakfast is the most important meal of the day did not come from careful clinical research. It was popularized, in almost exactly its modern form, by a wartime advertising campaign for Grape-Nuts cereal in 1944. Before it was a nutritional truth, it was a slogan. And when researchers finally tested the underlying proposition the rigorous way — with randomized trials rather than surveys of people's existing habits — the dramatic effect the slogan promises mostly failed to appear. That does not mean eating breakfast is pointless or harmful. It means the superlative in the sentence is doing advertising work, not scientific work. This claim is misleading.

## The claim

The claim under review is simple and categorical: breakfast is the most important meal of the day. Taken at face value, it asserts a hierarchy among meals. Breakfast does not merely matter; it matters more than lunch, more than dinner, more than anything else you eat. If the claim were true, skipping breakfast should produce measurably worse outcomes — for weight, for metabolic health, for thinking — than skipping any other meal, and people who eat breakfast should be systematically healthier than comparable people who do not.

The first thing to notice is that the sentence is doing two jobs at once. One job is nutritional: it tells you what to eat and when. The other job is commercial: it tells you to buy breakfast products. As the history will show, the second job came first.

## Where the sentence was born: Battle Creek

The story of the American breakfast begins in Battle Creek, Michigan, in the late 1800s, in a health resort called the Battle Creek Sanitarium. It was run by Dr. John Harvey Kellogg, a Seventh-day Adventist physician, health reformer, and moral enthusiast who believed, among other things, that meat stirred up the passions and that a bland, grain-based diet would calm them. Out of that sanitarium came corn flakes, developed as a therapeutic food.

Kellogg's brother, Will Keith Kellogg, saw the commercial potential that his brother's moral medicine project lacked. He added sugar over John's objections, founded the company that still bears the family name, and set out to sell America on cereal as a morning food. Kellogg and the early cereal industry were aggressively promoting the idea that a light, easy-to-digest grain breakfast was healthier than the heavy, meat-based morning meals common at the time. A magazine owned by the Battle Creek camp is where the exact phrase — "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" — first appears in print, in 1917, eleven years after Will Kellogg began selling corn flakes. So the sentence's first documented appearance is already inside the orbit of a man selling breakfast cereal.

Historians note that the general belief that morning food mattered was already floating around — Good Housekeeping printed a similar notion as early as 1889 — but it was the cereal industry that gave the slogan legs, a budget, and a nationwide distribution network. What began as a health reformer's hobbyhorse became a marketing asset.

## The bacon interlude: how a PR man manufactured a medical consensus

The story takes an instructive detour through bacon. In the 1920s, the Beech-Nut Packing Company, which sold bacon among other foods, had a problem: most Americans ate a light breakfast of coffee, a roll, and maybe some fruit. Bacon was not selling as well as it could. The company hired Edward Bernays, the nephew of Sigmund Freud and the man widely called the father of public relations — the author of *Propaganda* (1928), who described his trade as "the engineering of consent."

Bernays understood that he could not simply tell people to buy bacon. So, as he later recounted it himself, he went to his agency's in-house physician and worked up a chain of questions. Was a heavy breakfast sounder from a health standpoint than a light one? The doctor said yes, because the body loses energy during the night and needs it during the day. Would the doctor write to 5,000 physicians asking whether their judgment matched his? About 4,500 answered back, all concurring that a more substantial breakfast was better for the health of the American people than a light one.

Bernays then arranged for this "finding" to be published in newspapers across the country with headlines like "4,500 physicians urge bigger breakfast," while other articles stated that bacon and eggs should be a central part of breakfast. Bacon sales went up. No trial was run. No controlled experiment was conducted. A PR man's leading questionnaire, dressed up as medical consensus and delivered through third parties, changed what America ate for breakfast.

The Bernays episode matters for this fact check because it is the clearest surviving demonstration of the industry's standard technique: commission or orchestrate something that looks like expert opinion, strip out the commercial motive, and let newspapers report it as science. The cereal companies used the same playbook repeatedly — seeking out and commissioning research that "proved" their claims, which helped entrench the breakfast message because it appeared to be backed by experts. Some of that industry-funded research went onto cereal boxes without disclosing who paid, and sometimes the published findings omitted the half of the data that cut the wrong way.

## 1944: the slogan goes nationwide

Then came the version everyone quotes. In 1944, General Foods — the manufacturer of Grape-Nuts, then known as Post's Grape-Nuts — ran a wartime radio campaign called "Eat a Good Breakfast, Do a Better Job." The radio ads ran the line: "Nutrition experts say breakfast is the most important meal of the day." The nutrition experts were not named. The claim was not substantiated. It was a slogan dressed as science.

The timing was deliberate. Wartime America was a nation being asked to sacrifice, to ration, to be productive. Telling citizens that a good breakfast was their patriotic duty — that it would make them do a better job — wrapped a cereal box in the flag. Advertisements echoed the sentiment on the radio, in magazines, in newspapers, everywhere. It worked so well that over the following eight decades the slogan ossified into something most people on Earth believe to be a nutritional fact. Trusted people — teachers, parents, even doctors — have been repeating it ever since, despite it lacking any scientific backing.

The clearest proof that the sentence was installed rather than discovered is that in some countries it simply failed to take. The slogan's global reach followed the marketing budgets, not the biochemistry. Where cereal companies spent, breakfast became sacred; where they did not, morning eating habits stayed diverse and local.

## What the observational studies say — and why they are tricky

If you survey people about their breakfast habits and their health, a pattern shows up reliably: people who eat breakfast tend to be healthier than people who skip it. A 2025 meta-analysis involving more than 2.3 million participants found that breakfast skipping was associated with a 17% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, including higher risks of coronary artery disease and stroke. A 2024 meta-analysis of prospective studies involving more than 240,000 adults found associations between breakfast skipping and higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Prospective studies have also linked breakfast skipping to a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes. In one large US cohort, people who never ate breakfast had an 87% higher risk of cardiovascular death than daily breakfast eaters after adjusting for a long list of confounders.

These are real studies with real numbers, and they are the reason the slogan still sounds evidence-based. But every one of them is observational: researchers watched what people already did and recorded what happened to them. They did not assign anyone to skip breakfast. And the people who choose to skip breakfast are, on average, different from the people who choose to eat it in ways that go far beyond the breakfast itself.

Look at the Harvard men's study that found a 27% higher heart disease risk among breakfast skippers. The skippers in that cohort were more likely to smoke, drank about 40% more alcohol per day, slept less, exercised less, and were more likely to be on medication for depression. They ate fewer calories overall but crammed them into bigger, fewer meals. In another large cohort, people who never ate breakfast were more likely to be former smokers, heavy drinkers, physically inactive, poorer eaters, and of lower socioeconomic status. Researchers can statistically adjust for these factors — and they do — but adjustment only handles what was measured, and the people who voluntarily skip breakfast differ in hundreds of unmeasured ways: their schedules, their stress, their health literacy, their food budgets. This is the confounding problem, and it is the reason observational data can never quite answer the question the slogan pretends to answer.

There is also reverse causation to worry about: people who feel unwell, who have poor appetites from illness or depression, or who work chaotic shift schedules are both likelier to skip breakfast and likelier to have worse health outcomes. The skipping may be a symptom of the underlying trouble, not its cause. Genetics-based research has begun probing this with Mendelian randomization — a method that uses inherited genetic variants as a kind of natural experiment. One such analysis of nearly 194,000 participants found a causal association between genetic liability to breakfast skipping and heart failure risk (odds ratio about 1.38), mediated partly through blood metabolites like glucose. That is intriguing, but it estimates the effect of a lifelong genetic propensity to skip breakfast, which is not the same thing as telling a healthy adult whether eating breakfast tomorrow morning matters.

And there is an important nuance emerging from chrononutrition — the study of when we eat, not just what. In a cohort study following more than 3,000 participants over five years, two habits were independently linked to lower BMI: eating breakfast early in the day, and extending the overnight fasting window by finishing dinner early. What was not linked to weight benefit: skipping breakfast as a form of intermittent fasting. Among a subset of men who practiced intermittent fasting specifically by skipping breakfast and eating their first meal after 2 p.m., researchers found no weight advantage at all — and that group also tended to smoke more, drink more, exercise less, and eat less healthily. The emerging picture is that meal timing matters, but the benefit seems to come from eating earlier in the day overall, not from breakfast having some special metabolic status.

## What the randomized trials show

Observational studies ask what kinds of people skip breakfast. Randomized trials ask what happens when you tell someone to skip breakfast. The difference is everything.

A systematic review led by researchers at Monash University collected every randomized trial in adults from high-income countries that assigned people to eat breakfast or skip it. Of 13 included trials, seven examined weight change and ten examined energy intake. The pooled result on weight favored skipping — by a little under half a kilogram, about a pound. The authors concluded they found no evidence to support the notion that breakfast consumption promotes weight loss, or that skipping breakfast leads to weight gain. Read that again: when people were randomly assigned, eating breakfast did not help them lose weight.

The largest single trial, conducted in Alabama, randomly assigned 309 adults to be told to eat breakfast, told to skip it, or placed in a comparison group, and followed them for 16 weeks. Nearly everyone did as asked. Weight change did not differ between the groups — whether people had been breakfast eaters or skippers to begin with.

The Bath Breakfast Project in Britain looked more closely at where the calories went. Researchers assigned 33 lean adults to a substantial breakfast or nothing before noon for six weeks, measuring everything. Those eating breakfast did not compensate by eating less later in the day — their total intake stayed 539 calories per day higher than the fasting group's. But they also moved more: physical activity thermogenesis was markedly higher with breakfast, roughly canceling out the extra calories. Body mass and body fat did not differ between the groups at the end. So breakfast did something — it made people more active — but it did not do what the slogan implies: it did not make them leaner or healthier by the trial's measures.

What about thinking and mental performance? The slogan's promise includes sharper mornings. A major evidence review published in November 2025 in *Psychological Bulletin*, synthesizing 71 studies involving nearly 3,500 healthy adults, found that short-term fasting generally did not impair mental performance. Adults who fasted performed just as well on memory, decision-making, and reaction-time tests as those who had recently eaten. As senior researcher David Moreau of the University of Auckland put it, the findings contradicted "the widespread assumption that fasting inherently compromises thinking ability." There were caveats: fasts longer than about 12 hours produced slight declines, and children and teenagers — whose developing brains need steady energy — did worse when skipping meals. But for adults, the "brain fog from skipping breakfast" idea did not hold up.

## The intermittent fasting chapter

Breakfast's throne faces a challenge from the other side now: intermittent fasting, which in its most popular form is simply breakfast-skipping rebranded as 16:8 time-restricted eating. As of 2026, the evidence picture for fasting is mixed and sobering for enthusiasts. The Cochrane Collaboration reviewed more than 20 trials with nearly 2,000 adults with overweight or obesity and found no clear advantage of intermittent fasting over standard calorie restriction. A separate 2025 network meta-analysis of 99 randomized trials reached a similar conclusion: intermittent fasting works about as well as continuous calorie restriction for weight loss — the active ingredient is the calorie deficit, not the fasting window. Only alternate-day fasting clearly outperformed standard restriction, and even that came with adherence caveats.

Meanwhile, safety signals complicate the story. A 2024 analysis of NHANES data presented to the American Heart Association linked eating windows shorter than eight hours to a 91% higher risk of cardiovascular death over eight years — an observational finding that has not been resolved by trials. And a 2026 study found that when calories were held equal, time-restricted eating produced no cardiometabolic benefit over standard meal timing. On the brighter side, a 12-month follow-up from the University of Granada found that people who followed an eight-hour eating window for just 12 weeks kept more weight off a full year later, with adherence mattering more than the exact hours chosen. The honest 2026 summary: intermittent fasting is neither a miracle nor automatically dangerous. It works mainly by helping people eat less, and skipping breakfast as the mechanism is not required to get there.

## The honest case for breakfast

None of this means breakfast is worthless. The verdict below is "Misleading," not "False," and the distinction matters. There is a genuine, honest case for eating a good breakfast, and it deserves to be stated plainly.

First, for children and teenagers, the evidence is on breakfast's side. Developing brains need steady energy, and the 2025 Psychological Bulletin review found that children and teens performed worse when they skipped meals. School breakfast programs exist for good reasons.

Second, breakfast is a convenient delivery vehicle for nutrition. Morning foods — fiber, fruit, yogurt, whole grains — tend to be the healthiest foods many people eat all day. People who eat breakfast get more of those nutrients. Breakfast contributed roughly 30 to 47% of several nutrients in one study of schoolchildren. The slogan's grain of truth is this: a nutritious breakfast is a good way to get nutrition, especially for kids.

Third, the trials did find that breakfast eaters moved more. Physical activity thermogenesis was markedly higher in the breakfast group of the Bath experiment. If a morning meal gets you moving, that is a real benefit — it just does not prove the meal itself is metabolically special.

Fourth, meal timing science suggests that front-loading calories — eating earlier in the day — aligns better with the body's circadian rhythms than back-loading them into the evening. But notice what this argument actually supports: eating early, not breakfast as a rank above all meals. A person who eats their first meal at noon and their last at 6 p.m. is also front-loading.

What none of the evidence supports is the superlative. No trial has shown that breakfast is more important than other meals. No study has identified a unique metabolic property of morning food that lunch or dinner lacks. The randomized evidence on weight goes, if anything, slightly the other direction. The observational health associations are entangled with lifestyle differences that statistical adjustment cannot fully untangle.

## Why the sentence survived a century of counterevidence

If the science never supported the superlative, why does everyone still say it? Partly because the sentence is self-reinforcing: it sounds like wisdom, so it gets repeated by people in positions of trust, so it keeps sounding like wisdom. Partly because it has a commercial constituency that never went away. Cereal companies, juice companies, and breakfast-restaurant chains all profit when breakfast is treated as non-negotiable.

And partly because it feels true. Eating breakfast does make you feel good. Logically, the first meal of the day sets the pace for the day, and starting with something healthy and nutritious boosts mood and energy. The slogan borrowed this genuine, modest feeling and inflated it into a categorical ranking. It is the difference between "a good breakfast is a good habit" and "breakfast is the most important meal of the day." The first is sensible. The second is marketing that has been masquerading as science since 1944.

There is also a regulatory footnote worth remembering. When regulators have tested specific breakfast-related claims — the kind a company must substantiate before printing — the burden of proof has applied. But the slogan itself, floating free in culture, in conversation, in parenting advice, has never been subject to that burden. It circulates below the level at which evidence is demanded, which is exactly why an ad line could become folk wisdom.

## The Verdict: Misleading

**"Breakfast is the most important meal of the day" is misleading.** The sentence was popularized as an advertising slogan — first in print in 1917 in a cereal magnate's orbit, then nationwide in 1944 as a Grape-Nuts wartime radio campaign whose "nutrition experts" were never named. It was preceded by a 1920s PR stunt in which Edward Bernays manufactured a "4,500 physicians" consensus to sell bacon. When the claim was finally tested in randomized trials rather than surveys of existing habits, the promised benefits did not materialize: a Monash-led review of 13 trials found no evidence that eating breakfast promotes weight loss or that skipping it causes weight gain; the largest trial, in 309 adults, found no difference in weight change; and a 2025 review of 71 studies found that short-term fasting does not impair adults' thinking.

The observational studies that link breakfast skipping to heart disease and diabetes are real, but the skippers in those studies smoked more, drank more, exercised less, and were poorer — confounders that no statistical adjustment can fully remove. Meanwhile, the newest evidence points toward meal timing (eating earlier in the day) rather than breakfast itself as the active ingredient.

Breakfast can be a fine, healthy habit — especially for children and teenagers, and especially when it delivers fiber, fruit, and whole grains. But no meal is more important than another, and no study has shown that one is. The superlative belongs to advertising. The science supports a far humbler sentence: eat well, eat enough, and eat at reasonable hours.

So the next time someone tells you that breakfast is the most important meal of the day, you might ask them where they heard it. If the answer is a radio ad from 1944 — well, now you know.
