Every angler has seen it: a fish thrashing on the end of a line, mouth hooked, fighting with everything it has. And every angler has heard the reassurance that follows — *don't worry, fish don't feel pain*. It is one of the most convenient beliefs in the modern world, quietly underwriting everything from weekend catch-and-release to industrial fish farming. It lets billions of people eat fish, hook fish, and keep fish in tanks without a second thought.

But is it true? For most of the twentieth century, science had no firm answer, and the default assumption was no. Fish were treated as swimming reflex machines: they could detect damage and react to it, the thinking went, but they could not *feel* anything about it. Then, in 2003, a team of researchers in Scotland injected bee venom into the lips of rainbow trout — and the fish behaved in ways that looked unmistakably like suffering. The debate that followed has now run for more than twenty years, through courtrooms, parliaments, and some of the most prestigious journals in neuroscience. This is what the evidence actually says.

## The Claim

The claim comes in two versions. The soft version says fish probably do not feel pain the way we do, so how we treat them matters less than how we treat a dog or a pig. The hard version says fish cannot feel pain at all — that a hooked trout is no more sentient than a wind-up toy, and that its thrashing is pure reflex. Both versions rest on the same anatomical argument: fish lack a neocortex, the folded outer layer of the mammalian brain where conscious pain is processed in humans, so they cannot have the conscious experience of pain.

That argument was the scientific orthodoxy for decades, and it was given its most forceful expression in 2002, when neurobiologist James D. Rose of the University of Wyoming published a review in *Reviews in Fisheries Science* arguing that awareness of pain in humans depends on specific regions of the cerebral cortex, that fish lack those regions or any functional equivalent, and that it is therefore "untenable" that fish can experience pain. In Rose's view, fish display "robust, nonconscious, neuroendocrine, and physiological stress responses to noxious stimuli" — stress, yes; suffering, no.

It is a serious argument, made by a serious scientist, and it has not gone away. But in the years since Rose published it, the evidence has piled up on the other side — and it has piled up in the form of experiments, not philosophy.

## Pain Is Not the Same as Nociception

Before the evidence makes sense, one distinction matters more than any other. *Nociception* is the detection of a harmful stimulus: specialised nerve endings called nociceptors fire when tissue is damaged, and the body reacts — withdrawing, flinching, fleeing. Nociception can happen without any conscious experience at all. Touch a hot stove and your hand pulls back before you have even registered the burn; the reflex runs through the spinal cord, not the thinking brain.

*Pain*, by contrast, is the conscious, emotional experience of that damage — the part that hurts, that you remember, that changes what you do next time. The entire fish-pain debate is a dispute about whether fish cross the line from the first to the second. Nobody serious disputes that fish have nociception. The question is whether what happens in a fish's brain after nociception amounts to something we should call pain.

That is why the experiments that matter are not the ones showing that fish flinch. They are the ones showing that fish remember, weigh trade-offs, and seek relief — behaviours that, in a mammal, we would unhesitatingly call evidence of pain.

## The Trout That Changed Everything

The turning point came in 2003, when Lynne Sneddon and her colleagues at the University of Edinburgh published a study in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* titled "Do fishes have nociceptors? Evidence for the evolution of a vertebrate sensory system." It was the first study to look systematically for pain receptors in a fish — and it found them.

Using electrophysiological recordings from the trigeminal nerves of rainbow trout, Sneddon's team identified polymodal nociceptors on the head of the trout, with physiological properties similar to those described in higher vertebrates. These receptors responded to the same three categories of noxious stimuli that human nociceptors respond to: mechanical pressure, temperatures in the noxious range (above 40°C), and a chemical irritant, 1% acetic acid. In higher vertebrates, the nociceptive nerves are mostly unmyelinated C fibres; in the trout, A-delta fibres were most common — a difference that offers insights into how nociceptive systems evolved, but not one that changes the basic finding. The hardware was there.

The behavioural half of the study was what made it famous. When the researchers injected <a href="/blog/do-bees-die-after-they-sting-fact-check/">bee venom — the same substance behind the question of whether bees die after they sting</a> or acetic acid into the lips of trout, the fish did not simply flinch and carry on. They rocked back and forth on the tank bottom, rubbed their lips against the gravel, breathed faster — their opercular beat rate rose sharply — and took far longer to resume feeding. One summary of the study reports feeding behaviour fell by about half. Crucially, these effects lasted far longer than a reflex should: behavioural responses persisted for three hours to two days after the injection. And when the fish were given morphine, a painkiller, their behaviour returned to normal.

That last detail is the one the researchers keep coming back to. A reflex does not need morphine. If the trout's behaviour were a mindless, hardwired response to tissue damage, an analgesic should not have switched it off. The fact that it did suggests that something in the fish's brain was processing the noxious stimulus in a way that morphine could interrupt — the same logic, in miniature, as giving a human patient a painkiller after surgery.

Rose was not convinced. In a rebuttal published shortly after the study, he called the research "deeply flawed" and argued that it dealt only with nociception, not pain — that bee venom contains toxins that trigger hormonal stress responses on top of stimulating nociceptors, and that nothing in the study proved conscious awareness. The argument, in his words, was that "in order to show that a fish (or any organism) experiences pain, it is necessary to show that a fish has consciousness. Without consciousness, there is no pain." That is a fair standard to demand. The question is whether the research since 2003 has met it.

## What Is Happening Inside a Fish's Brain?

The strongest objection to fish pain has always been anatomical, so the most important research since 2003 has been anatomical too. Fish do not have a neocortex. But the question is whether a neocortex is the *only* structure that can generate conscious experience — and modern neuroscience increasingly says no.

In 2012, a group of prominent neuroscientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, which stated that "the absence of a neocortex does not appear to preclude an organism from experiencing affective states" and that convergent evidence indicates non-human animals "have the neuroanatomical, neurochemical, and neurophysiological substrates of conscious states along with the capacity to exhibit intentional behaviours." The declaration was written mainly about mammals and birds, but its logic — that consciousness does not live in one privileged fold of brain tissue — applies directly to the fish debate.

Fish brains do have structures that do related work. Research by Victoria Braithwaite, author of the 2010 book *Do Fish Feel Pain?*, showed that when fish experience a painful stimulus, neurons fire in the pallium and the telencephalon, the front part of the fish brain — regions that perform functions analogous to the cortex in mammals. A 2020 neuroimaging study published in *Comparative Neurology* found that these activations mirror those seen in rodents under similar conditions. And a 2018 review in *Fish and Fisheries* concluded that pain-related behaviours in fish involve forebrain processing, not just spinal circuits — the difference, in other words, between a reflex and something the brain is actively working on.

Fish also possess the chemistry of pain. They have the neurotransmitters associated with pain transmission in mammals, including substance P and endorphins, the body's own pain-modulating opioids. Nociceptors have now been identified not only in trout but in zebrafish and goldfish — and if you want to know how much scientists have learned about goldfish in particular, our earlier fact-check on <a href="/blog/goldfish-three-second-memory-myth-fact-check/">Do Goldfish Really Have a 3-Second Memory?</a> shows that these animals are far more cognitively capable than their reputation suggests.

None of this proves that a fish's experience of pain is identical to a human's. Nobody in the mainstream of this research claims that. What it does is dismantle the cleanest version of the sceptical argument: the idea that fish *cannot* feel pain because they lack the necessary equipment. The equipment is different, but it is there, and it does recognisably similar work.

## The Behavioural Evidence: Trade-Offs, Memory, and Seeking Relief

If the anatomy is suggestive, the behaviour is harder to dismiss — because it is exactly the kind of behaviour that pain researchers use as evidence of pain in animals that cannot talk.

First, fish make trade-offs when they are in pain. In a 2009 study, trout were given a painful injection and then placed in a tank where they could choose between a safe but boring side and a risky but food-rich side. Uninjured fish chose the food. Pain-injected fish chose safety, even when hungry. That is not a reflex. A reflex does not weigh hunger against danger and decide that avoiding further harm matters more than eating. It is a decision, and it is the kind of decision that implies an internal state the animal is trying to protect.

Second, fish seek pain relief — and will pay a cost to get it. In experiments conducted by Braithwaite's group, injured fish were given a choice between a neutral part of a tank and a part enriched with an analgesic substance. The injured fish preferred the analgesic-enriched environment, suggesting they could detect the relief and actively sought it out. Other studies have shown fish swimming through less-preferred environments to reach areas where pain-relieving substances are available. An animal that merely flinches does not go looking for medicine.

Third, fish remember pain. Subsequent studies have shown that fish avoid locations where they previously experienced painful stimuli — a capacity for pain memory that requires the experience to be stored and recalled, not merely reacted to in the moment. And in 2019, researchers at the University of Liverpool found that zebrafish exposed to painful stimuli displayed anxiety-like behaviours, and that those behaviours could be reduced with anxiety-reducing drugs — a finding that points to an emotional, not merely sensory, dimension of the experience.

Fourth, the stress physiology is unambiguous. When fish are caught in nets or handled out of water, their cortisol levels — the hormone linked to stress — rise significantly. Cortisol alone does not prove pain; a stressed animal is not necessarily a suffering one. But combined with the nociceptors, the forebrain activation, the trade-offs, and the relief-seeking, the stress response is one more strand in a rope that is getting hard to cut.

A 2021 review in the journal *Animal Cognition* pulled these strands together and counted more than 100 studies documenting pain-related behaviours in fish. That is the number the sceptics have to explain away — not one famous trout experiment, but a literature.

## The Dissent: Why Some Scientists Still Say No

None of this has ended the debate, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. The sceptical case has three pillars, and each deserves a fair hearing.

The first is the anatomical argument, restated most recently by Brian Key, head of the Brain Growth and Regeneration Lab at the University of Queensland. In a 2016 paper in *Animal Sentience* titled "Why fish do not feel pain," Key argued that fish lack "the necessary neurocytoarchitecture, microcircuitry, and structural connectivity for the neural processing required for feeling pain." In other words: similar is not the same, and the fish pallium is not a cortex in disguise.

The second is the methodological argument. Rose and his colleagues, in a 2014 paper in *Fish and Fisheries* — "Can fish really feel pain?" — argued that the behavioural evidence is over-interpreted: that lip-rubbing, reduced feeding, and place avoidance can all be explained by nociception and stress without invoking conscious experience. They also pointed out that experiments in which the cerebral hemispheres of fish were removed showed that fish could maintain normal function and behaviour without those parts of the brain — suggesting that the forebrain is not doing the work its defenders claim.

The third is the philosophical argument, and it is the hardest to resolve: we cannot directly access the subjective experience of any non-human animal. Every inference about animal pain is indirect, built from anatomy, physiology, and behaviour. The sceptics argue that this gap matters more for fish than for, say, dogs, because the anatomical distance from humans is so much greater. The defenders argue that the same logic would deny pain to any animal that is not a mammal — and that the Cambridge Declaration already rejected that logic. The same problem of reading animal minds shows up across species: our fact-check on <a href="/blog/can-dogs-smell-fear-fact-check/">Can Dogs Smell Fear?</a> found that dogs do detect human fear through scent, yet what a dog actually *feels* about it remains as hard to pin down as what a trout feels about a hook.

The Australian Museum, in its summary of the debate, puts it plainly: "The jury is still out! Different researchers have come to different conclusions." That is a fair description of the state of the argument — but it is worth noting what the jury is disagreeing about. Almost no one in the debate now argues that fish detect nothing. The disagreement is about the *quality* of what they detect: reflex or experience, nociception or pain.

## Where the Science Stands Now

If you zoom out from the individual papers, the direction of travel over the last decade is unmistakable: institutions, laws, and scientific consensus statements have moved toward recognising fish pain, even as a minority of researchers hold the line.

In 2022, the United Kingdom passed the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, which formally recognises all vertebrates — including fish — as sentient beings, and created an Animal Sentience Committee to review whether government policy pays due regard to their welfare. The Act's definition of "animal" covers any vertebrate other than *Homo sapiens*, plus cephalopod molluscs and decapod crustaceans. Fish had, in a sense, been recognised as sentient long before; the Act made it law.

In 2024, the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness — initiated by researchers at New York University, York University, and the London School of Economics, and signed by more than 500 scientists and academics — concluded that "the empirical evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates." The declaration's language is deliberately careful — "a realistic possibility," not certainty — but 500 signatures is not a fringe position.

In May 2025, the Scottish Animal Welfare Commission published a report on ascribing sentience to fish, concluding that "the threshold for evidence of sentience has been exceeded for vertebrate fish" and that considering their welfare is "ethically incumbent upon policymakers." The Commission noted that fish are vertebrates, members of a phylogenetic group in which all healthy adult members are deemed sentient — a group that includes humans.

And in 2025, a peer-reviewed analysis in *Scientific Reports* applied the Welfare Footprint Framework to commercial rainbow trout slaughter by air asphyxiation — the way many farmed fish die — and estimated a median of approximately 10 minutes of moderate-to-extreme pain per fish, with a 95% credible interval of 1.9 to 21.7 minutes, or roughly 24 minutes per kilogram. The paper was co-authored by Lynne Sneddon herself. Its figures are inferred from behavioural indicators and neurophysiological proxies rather than direct subjective report, as in all non-human pain research, and the study covers a single species — but it is the first attempt to put a number on what the debate is actually about.

## Claim vs. Evidence

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Fish have no pain receptors. | False. Sneddon et al. (2003) found polymodal nociceptors on trout heads, responding to pressure, heat above 40°C, and acetic acid; nociceptors have since been found in zebrafish and goldfish too. |
| Fish behaviour after injury is just reflex. | Mostly false. Injured trout rock, rub their lips, and stop feeding for hours to days; they trade food for safety and seek out analgesics — behaviours that require forebrain processing, per a 2018 review. |
| Morphine would not change a reflex. | Correct — and it does change fish behaviour. Morphine returned the trout in Sneddon's study to normal behaviour, implying the behaviour was pain-driven. |
| Without a neocortex, fish cannot feel pain. | Contested. Rose (2002) and Key (2016) make this case; the Cambridge Declaration (2012) and a 2021 *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* review argue subcortical structures can support conscious perception. |
| The science is settled that fish feel pain. | Not settled. Genuine disagreement remains, but the weight of evidence — 100+ studies, the 2024 New York Declaration, the UK's 2022 Sentience Act — now leans clearly toward pain capacity. |

## The Verdict

**Mostly True.** The claim that fish feel pain is supported by the preponderance of the evidence — but it is not unanimous, and the verdict has to carry that caveat honestly.

Here is what is solid: fish possess nociceptors that work like ours; noxious stimuli change their behaviour for hours to days in ways that morphine reverses; they weigh pain against hunger, remember painful places, seek out pain relief, and show anxiety-like responses that anxiolytic drugs reduce; and their brains process noxious stimuli in forebrain regions whose activity mirrors that of rodents. More than 100 studies now document pain-related behaviours in fish, and the scientific community's formal statements — from the Cambridge Declaration in 2012 to the New York Declaration in 2024, signed by more than 500 scientists — have moved steadily toward recognising fish as sentient.

Here is what keeps the verdict from being a flat "True": a serious minority of researchers, led by James Rose and Brian Key, argues that none of this proves conscious experience, because fish lack the neural architecture that generates it — and because we cannot directly observe another animal's inner life, their objection cannot be disproved by behaviour alone. The Australian Museum's assessment that "the jury is still out" is a fair description of a live scientific disagreement.

But "the jury is still out" is not the same as "there is no evidence." The old reassurance — that fish are unfeeling reflex machines and that a hook in the lip is nothing to them — is no longer the scientific consensus. It is a minority position, defended by fewer researchers each year, while the evidence for pain capacity grows. If you hook a fish, the best available science says it probably feels something, and that something is probably bad. The honest answer to "do fish feel pain" is: the evidence says yes, most likely — and the longer science looks, the stronger that yes becomes.

## What This Means in Practice

A fact-check is not a sermon, but this one has practical consequences that are worth stating plainly, because they follow directly from the evidence.

For anglers, the research supports what many already do instinctively: handle fish quickly, keep them in the water where possible, use barbless hooks, and dispatch a fish you intend to keep swiftly rather than leaving it to asphyxiate. The 2025 estimate of roughly ten minutes of moderate-to-extreme pain per trout during air asphyxiation is a number about slaughter, not sport fishing — but the mechanism it describes, a fish slowly suffocating in air, is the same one at work on a riverbank.

For fish farming, the stakes are larger. The same welfare logic that now governs how chickens and pigs are stunned and slaughtered is increasingly being applied to fish, and the science reviewed here is the reason. Stunning before slaughter, rather than asphyxiation in air or on ice, is the direct policy implication of accepting that fish feel pain.

And for the rest of us, the question is simpler. The belief that fish do not feel pain was never really a scientific conclusion; it was a convenient assumption that science has now spent twenty years testing — and, on the balance of the evidence, overturning. The trout in Sneddon's tank, rubbing its lip against the gravel for hours after a venom injection, was not performing a reflex. It was telling us something. It took science two decades to learn how to listen.

The next time someone tells you fish don't feel pain, you know what to ask: *then why does morphine make them stop acting like they do?* It is a short question. Twenty years of research has not produced a good answer to it — because, most likely, there isn't one.
