Every summer, the same scene plays out on patios and in parks around the world. One person sits untouched, sipping a drink in peace, while the person next to them swats frantically at a cloud of mosquitoes and counts a dozen fresh bites by the end of the evening. The bitten person usually has a theory. It is the perfume. It is the beer. It is the sweet blood. And increasingly, it is the blood type.

The claim has become one of the most repeated pieces of mosquito folklore on the internet: mosquitoes prefer people with type O blood, while people with type A blood get left alone. It shows up in listicles, in travel advice, in comment sections under every article about summer pests, and in the worried questions of parents whose children seem to attract every mosquito within a mile. It sounds plausible enough. Mosquitoes bite us to drink our blood, so why would they not have a preference for one flavour over another?

The evidence, it turns out, is real — but it is also much thinner, messier, and less useful than the viral version suggests. There is a genuine peer-reviewed study behind the claim, and its numbers are striking. There are also other studies that point in a different direction entirely, and a much larger body of research showing that blood type is a minor player compared with the gases you exhale, the chemicals on your skin, and the genes you inherited. The honest answer is that mosquitoes do show a preference for certain blood types in controlled laboratory tests, and type O usually comes out on top — but in the real world, on a real summer evening, your blood type is one of the least important reasons you are being bitten.

This is the story of what the research actually found, where it disagrees with itself, and what really decides whether a mosquito chooses you or the person sitting next to you.

## The Claim: Type O Blood Is a Mosquito Magnet

The version of the claim that circulates online is simple and confident. People with type O blood are bitten roughly twice as often as people with type A blood. People with type B blood fall somewhere in the middle. If you are type O, the story goes, you are a mosquito magnet, and there is not much you can do about it.

The claim is usually traced to a single study, and that study is real. In 2004, a team of Japanese researchers led by Yoshikazu Shirai published a paper in the Journal of Medical Entomology, the peer-reviewed journal of the Entomological Society of America, examining how the Asian tiger mosquito, Aedes albopictus, responded to human volunteers of different blood groups. The researchers ran 64 human landing tests, placing mosquitoes near volunteers and recording where the insects chose to land.

The results were memorable. Mosquitoes landed on people with type O blood about 83 percent of the time, compared with roughly 46 percent for people with type A blood. People with type B and type AB blood fell in between. The difference between type O and type A was statistically significant, meaning it was unlikely to be a coincidence, and the finding has been quoted ever since.

A later study added to the picture. In 2019, researchers offered mosquitoes a choice of feeders containing all four blood types, and the insects picked the type O feeder most often. That result, too, gets cited in almost every article about the topic. Together, the two studies form the backbone of the viral claim — and they are the reason the claim cannot simply be dismissed as a myth.

But the details of the 2004 study matter, and they are usually left out of the viral version. The famous 83 percent and 46 percent figures apply to people known as secretors. About 85 percent of people secrete chemical signals indicating their blood type through their skin and saliva, while the remaining 15 percent do not. In the 2004 study, mosquitoes were more attracted to secretors than to non-secretors regardless of blood type, and the difference between type O and type A was the only one that reached statistical significance. The study did not show a meaningful difference between type O and type B, or between type O and type AB. In other words, the single most quoted study in this debate contains, inside its own results, a strong argument that blood group is not the main thing mosquitoes are responding to.

That nuance matters, because the way a claim travels online is rarely the way the science reads on the page. A statistically significant difference between two groups in one small laboratory study becomes, after a few rounds of retelling, a universal law of nature. The mosquito story is a textbook case of that drift.

## What Other Species Say: Type B Wins Sometimes

If the type O claim were a universal rule, it should hold across mosquito species. It does not, and this is where the story gets genuinely interesting.

When researchers tested Aedes aegypti, the mosquito responsible for spreading dengue, Zika, and yellow fever, in controlled tunnel chambers, the winner was blood group B, not O. A separate study on Anopheles stephensi, a malaria vector in Asia, also found a strong preference for type B blood, with air currents carrying the scent of type B blood triggering a significantly stronger host-seeking response than other blood groups.

The differences do not stop at landing preference. When Anopheles stephensi females were fed different blood types through membrane feeders in the laboratory, those fed type B blood laid an average of about 216 eggs per female, compared with roughly 98 for type O and just 65 for type A. For Aedes aegypti, a laboratory study found that blood groups B-negative and O-negative produced the highest fecundity, with larger eggs and greater egg viability. These are laboratory findings using artificial feeders, not free-flying mosquitoes choosing human hosts, so they say nothing directly about which person a mosquito will bite at a barbecue — but they show that the relationship between blood type and mosquitoes varies by species in ways the viral claim never mentions.

Meanwhile, a study published in the American Journal of Entomology tested female Aedes aegypti with membrane feeders offering all four human blood groups at the same time. In that experiment, group O was the overwhelming favourite, chosen significantly more often than A, B, or AB, although the feeding preference had no effect on how many eggs the mosquitoes laid afterward. So even within a single species, different experimental setups can produce different answers.

The honest summary is this: blood type preference depends on which mosquito is biting you, which experimental method the researchers used, and whether the person is a secretor. None of these studies has been replicated at the scale that would make the conclusion airtight. The type O finding is real for one species in one well-known study, contradicted for other species, and never shown to be a strong effect in the messy conditions of real life.

That is why the verdict here is Mostly True rather than True or False. The core of the claim — that mosquitoes show a preference for certain blood types in laboratory tests, with type O usually leading — is supported by published research. But the claim as most people understand it, that your blood type decides whether mosquitoes eat you alive, is an exaggeration of what the science actually shows.

## The Secretor Twist: It May Not Be Your Blood at All

One of the most important details in the blood type research is also the least discussed. About 85 percent of people are secretors, meaning their bodies release blood-group antigens — the chemical markers that define blood type — into their saliva, tears, and skin secretions. The other 15 percent are non-secretors, and their blood type is effectively invisible to the outside world.

In the 2004 study, mosquitoes were significantly more attracted to secretors than to non-secretors, regardless of which blood type the secretor had. That finding points to a subtle but important possibility: what mosquitoes are detecting may not be blood type itself, but the chemical signals that happen to travel with it. A type O secretor broadcasts a strong chemical signature through the skin. A type O non-secretor broadcasts almost nothing. If the signal is the thing that matters, then secretor status could be a more powerful predictor of bites than blood type — and blood type would be, in part, a proxy for something else.

This is a pattern that shows up again and again in mosquito research. A factor that looks like a clean, simple explanation turns out to be entangled with a dozen other variables, and the clean explanation is usually the weakest of them. Blood type is one of those factors. It is real, it is measurable, and it is small.

## The Signals That Actually Decide: Carbon Dioxide, Heat, and Skin Chemistry

If blood type is a minor player, what actually decides who gets bitten? The research points to a hierarchy of signals, and blood type sits near the bottom of it.

At the top is carbon dioxide. Mosquitoes can detect the carbon dioxide in exhaled breath from dozens of metres away, using specialised organs called maxillary palps located between their antennae. Carbon dioxide acts as a long-range beacon, telling a female mosquito — only females bite, because they need the protein in blood to develop their eggs, while males feed on plant sap and flower nectar — that a warm-blooded animal is nearby. People who exhale more carbon dioxide are easier to find: larger adults, people exercising, and pregnant women. A landmark study published in The Lancet found that pregnant women attracted roughly twice as many mosquito landings as non-pregnant women, and that during late pregnancy women exhale about 21 percent more breath, widening the carbon dioxide signature that mosquitoes follow.

Once carbon dioxide has brought a mosquito close, a second set of signals takes over. Body heat matters, which is one reason mosquitoes are more active on warm, humid evenings and why people who have been exercising are bitten more. Skin chemistry matters even more. Human skin carries a complex mixture of chemicals produced by sweat glands and the bacteria that live on the skin, and mosquitoes are exquisitely sensitive to that mixture.

A study published in the journal Cell in 2022 gave a striking demonstration of how powerful skin chemistry can be. Researchers collected skin odours on fabric from volunteers and tested how attractive each sample was to Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. The most attractive person in the study was more than 100 times as attractive to the mosquitoes as the least attractive volunteer. Chemical analysis showed that the most attractive people produced significantly higher levels of certain carboxylic acids on their skin — particularly pentadecanoic, heptadecanoic, and nonadecanoic acids — which are produced when skin bacteria break down the fatty compounds in sebum. Receptors in the mosquito antennae, known as IR75k1 and IR75k3, respond to related compounds, nonanoic and octanoic acids, at levels roughly ten times greater than to any other odour blend tested.

Lactic acid is another major player. Humans carry unusually high levels of L-lactic acid on their skin compared with other animals, and it is part of why some mosquito species evolved to specialise in biting us. On its own, lactic acid does not draw mosquitoes in, but it dramatically amplifies the attractiveness of other skin odours and carbon dioxide. In one elegant experiment, researchers found they could make one of a person's index fingers more attractive to mosquitoes than the other simply by adding lactic acid to the airstream around it, within the natural range of variation that a human hand already produces.

Genetics ties all of this together. A twin study published in PLOS ONE exposed female Aedes aegypti mosquitoes to the scent of identical and non-identical twins, and found that identical twins were more similar in their attractiveness to mosquitoes than fraternal twins — evidence that how appealing you are to a mosquito is partly inherited. That makes sense, because the composition of your skin chemistry, the bacteria living on your skin, and even your metabolic rate are all influenced by your genes. Roughly 20 percent of people are thought to be especially attractive to mosquitoes, and for them the difference is not a matter of willpower or hygiene. It is chemistry.

Alcohol adds another layer. A study in the Journal of the American Mosquito Control Association examined mosquito landing rates before and after volunteers drank beer, and found that beer consumption increased landing rates. Alcohol raises skin temperature and increases carbon dioxide output, and it also changes the volatile compounds released through the skin. So the person at the barbecue with a beer in hand is, measurably, a better target than the person drinking water — which may be why the bitten person at every summer gathering is so often the one holding a drink.

Dark clothing plays a role too. Research published in Nature Communications in 2022 showed that sniffing carbon dioxide triggers mosquitoes to visually search for host colours, and they are drawn to dark colours such as black and red more than to light ones. Mosquitoes also prefer warm and humid conditions, which is why they are worst at dusk on still, muggy evenings.

Put together, the picture is clear. A mosquito finds you first by the carbon dioxide you exhale, then by your body heat and the colour of your clothing, and finally by the chemical signature of your skin — a signature shaped by your genes, your skin bacteria, your sweat, and what you have been drinking. Blood type, if it registers at all, is a whisper at the end of that sequence, not the shout at the beginning.

## Why the Claim Spread So Far

If blood type is such a small part of the story, why has the type O claim become one of the most repeated mosquito facts on the internet? Part of the answer is that it is built on a real study with a memorable number. Eighty-three percent versus forty-six percent is the kind of statistic that survives every round of retelling, because it feels precise and scientific. The caveats — 64 landing tests, one species, secretors only, only the O-versus-A comparison statistically significant — are the kind of detail that gets trimmed in the first retelling and never returns.

Part of the answer is that the claim offers something people want: an explanation for an experience that feels personal and unfair. Being bitten while everyone around you is untouched is irritating, and it is natural to look for a reason. A blood type is a satisfying answer because it is fixed, it is part of your identity, and it is not your fault. It is far less satisfying to be told that the reason is a complicated interaction between your exhaled breath, your skin bacteria, your genes, and the drink in your hand — and that none of those things is easily changed either.

There is also a commercial incentive. The claim appears in marketing for mosquito-repellent products, in travel health articles, and in content designed to be shared, because it is surprising, it is easy to understand, and it invites readers to check their own blood type and share the article with friends who share it. A claim that invites self-identification spreads faster than a claim that does not. Type O is also the most common blood type in many populations, which means a large share of readers can recognise themselves in the story — and a large share of those readers have, at some point, been bitten.

The pattern is familiar from other popular health claims. A single study with an interesting result is stripped of its context, repeated until it hardens into common knowledge, and then defended as settled science by people who have never read the study. The mosquito story is unusual only in that the underlying study is genuinely respectable. The problem is not that the research is bad. The problem is that the research is small, species-specific, and surrounded by larger effects that the viral version ignores.

It is worth noting that the same dynamic shows up in other animal-sensing claims. Our earlier fact-check on <a href="/blog/can-dogs-predict-earthquakes-fact-check/">can dogs predict earthquakes</a> found a similar gap between a popular belief and the evidence: animals can detect things humans cannot, but the leap from that ability to a reliable prediction is far larger than the stories suggest. Mosquitoes, like dogs, really do sense things we cannot — the difference is that mosquitoes sense them through chemistry, not through some mysterious sixth sense.

## What Blood Type Means for Disease Risk

There is a more serious question behind the claim than itchy evenings. Mosquitoes are the deadliest animals in human history, not because of their bites but because of the diseases they carry. Malaria alone killed an estimated 597,000 people in 2023, according to the World Health Organization, and dengue infects an estimated 390 million people a year. If blood type genuinely changed who gets bitten, it would also change who is most at risk — and that would matter for public health.

The evidence does not support treating blood type as a risk factor in any practical sense. The differences found in laboratory studies are small, inconsistent across species, and dwarfed by other variables. A person with type A blood who exhales a lot of carbon dioxide, exercises outdoors at dusk, and carries high levels of carboxylic acids on their skin will be bitten far more often than a type O person sitting still indoors in light clothing. In epidemiological terms, where you live, whether you sleep under a treated bed net, and whether you use repellent matter enormously more than your blood group.

That is an important corrective, because the viral version of the claim can create a false sense of security. Someone with type A blood who believes mosquitoes do not like them may skip repellent on a trip to a dengue-endemic region, and someone with type O blood may feel that protection is pointless because they are doomed to be bitten anyway. Both reactions are wrong. Repellent works regardless of blood type, and no blood type makes a person invisible to mosquitoes. The single most useful thing the research says about disease risk is that everybody is at risk, and everybody should protect themselves.

There is also a lesson here about how to read health news. When a study reports that one group was bitten 83 percent of the time and another 46 percent of the time, those numbers describe what happened in a small, controlled laboratory experiment with one mosquito species. They do not describe your odds at a barbecue. Laboratory landing rates are not the same as real-world bite rates, and a statistically significant difference is not the same as a large or practically important one. The researchers who ran the 2004 study were careful about this. The internet was not.

## What Actually Works Against Mosquitoes

Since blood type cannot be changed — and since it matters so little — the useful question is what does work. The good news is that the same research that downgrades blood type also points to defences that are genuinely effective.

The strongest protection is a registered repellent. Products containing DEET, picaridin, IR3535, or oil of lemon eucalyptus are recommended by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and they work by interfering with the mosquito's ability to detect the chemical signals it follows. They do not make you invisible, but they make you much harder to find, and they work the same way whether your blood is type O or type AB. Apply repellent to exposed skin, reapply as directed on the label, and use it even if you believe mosquitoes do not like you.

Clothing is the second line of defence. Long sleeves and long trousers reduce the amount of skin available to bite, and light-coloured clothing is less attractive to mosquitoes than dark colours, because mosquitoes use vision to home in on hosts once carbon dioxide has drawn them close. This is a small effect, but it is real, and it costs nothing. If you are exercising outdoors at dusk — the time when your carbon dioxide output, body heat, and sweat are all at their peak — covering up is the single cheapest thing you can do.

Timing and place matter too. Mosquitoes are most active at dawn and dusk, and they thrive in warm, humid, still air. A fan on a patio is surprisingly effective, because mosquitoes are weak fliers and a breeze disperses the carbon dioxide plume they follow. Removing standing water around a home — in plant saucers, buckets, and blocked gutters — removes breeding sites, and that helps everyone in the household regardless of blood type.

None of this is as exciting as discovering that your blood type makes you a target. But it has the advantage of being true, and of working. The research is unanimous on one point: the factors that decide whether you get bitten are mostly things you can influence, and the one factor you cannot influence — your blood group — is the one that matters least.

It is also worth keeping the scale of the problem in perspective. Most mosquito bites are a nuisance, not a medical emergency, and the itch comes from your immune system's reaction to mosquito saliva, not from anything the mosquito took from you. But in regions where dengue, malaria, Zika, or West Nile virus circulate, the same advice applies with more urgency. That is why health authorities focus on repellent, nets, and source reduction rather than on blood type screening. They are following the evidence, and the evidence says blood type is a footnote.

For readers who enjoy testing popular health claims against the evidence, the pattern here is a useful one to remember. A claim can be built on real research and still be misleading, because what makes a claim misleading is rarely an invented fact. It is a real fact, presented without the context that would show how small it is. The same test applies to other beliefs we have examined, from whether <a href="/blog/does-sugar-cause-diabetes-fact-check/">sugar causes diabetes</a> to whether <a href="/blog/does-honey-help-a-cough-fact-check/">honey helps a cough</a>: the question is never just whether a study exists, but what the study actually measured, how large the effect was, and whether it survives contact with the real world.

## The Verdict

Do mosquitoes prefer certain blood types? Mostly True — with important qualifications that the viral version of the claim leaves out.

The evidence in favour is real. In a 2004 study published in the Journal of Medical Entomology, Asian tiger mosquitoes landed on type O secretors about 83 percent of the time, compared with roughly 46 percent for type A secretors, and a 2019 study also found mosquitoes choosing type O blood most often when offered all four types. Those are genuine, peer-reviewed results, and they are the reason the claim earns a Mostly True rather than a False.

The evidence against the claim as it is usually understood is equally real. The 2004 study involved 64 landing tests with one mosquito species, and the only statistically significant difference was between type O and type A. Other studies found different winners: Aedes aegypti and Anopheles stephensi both showed preferences for type B blood in controlled tests. Secretor status — whether a person broadcasts blood-group signals through the skin at all — mattered as much as blood type itself. And a much larger body of research shows that carbon dioxide output, body heat, skin chemistry, genetics, and even alcohol consumption all influence mosquito attraction more strongly than blood type does. In the Cell study, the most attractive person was more than 100 times as attractive as the least attractive, a difference blood type alone could never explain.

So if you have type O blood, the laboratory evidence says mosquitoes may land on you a little more often than on a type A person standing next to you — in a controlled test, with one species, if you are a secretor. Outdoors, on a warm evening, with the carbon dioxide you exhale, the heat of your body, the bacteria on your skin, and the colour of your shirt all in play, your blood type is one of the smallest factors in the room. It is a real effect, and it is not the reason you are covered in bites. The mosquito that found you did not read your blood donor card. It followed your breath.
