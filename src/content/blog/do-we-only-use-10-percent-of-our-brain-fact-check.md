It is one of the most repeated "facts" in the world. You have heard it in classrooms, in self-help books, in films, and probably from someone trying to sell you a brain-training app. The claim is simple, flattering, and endlessly useful: human beings use only 10 percent of their brains, which means 90 percent of your mind is sitting there unused, waiting for the right technique, the right drug, or the right guru to switch it on. If it were true, it would be the greatest news in the history of self-improvement. A whole hidden continent of intelligence, memory, and creativity, already installed in your head, free of charge.

It is also false. Not partly false, not true in some limited sense, but false in the way that a claim about anatomy can be false: brain scans show that we use virtually every part of the brain, and that most of it is active almost all the time. There is no dormant 90 percent. There never was. The figure was never measured, never published in a scientific paper, and never uttered by the famous scientists to whom it is usually credited. It began as a vague remark about human potential, got a number attached to it by a self-help writer, and then spent a century being repeated by people who had every reason to believe it and no reason to check it.

That is what makes this myth worth a full investigation rather than a quick dismissal. The 10 percent claim is not a random piece of folklore. It is a case study in how a real observation can be misquoted, simplified, commercialised, and then protected by its own flattery. The reason it survives is not that the evidence for it is strong — there is none — but that the claim tells people exactly what they want to hear: that they are far more capable than they think, and that the only thing standing between them and genius is a secret they have not yet been told.

## The Claim

The popular version of the claim comes in several strengths, and they are worth separating, because they are not equally wrong. The strongest version says that 90 percent of the human brain is completely unused — dark, silent tissue that plays no part in thinking, feeling, or remembering, and that could in principle be "activated" to unlock telepathy, photographic memory, or genius-level intelligence. A softer version says that we use only 10 percent of our brain *at any one time*, which is closer to a real observation but still misleading. A third version, common in self-help literature, says that we use only 10 percent of our brain's *capacity* or *potential*, which sounds scientific but means almost nothing, because nobody has ever defined what 100 percent of human potential would look like.

All three versions share the same structure: a precise-sounding number, an appeal to a famous authority, and a promise. The number is always 10 percent. The authority is usually Albert Einstein, who supposedly said it, or sometimes the psychologist William James. And the promise is always the same: that enormous unused reserves are waiting inside you, and that whoever is telling you about them knows how to release them.

The claim has been used to sell books, courses, supplements, meditation programmes, and "brain-training" gadgets. It has appeared in films, television shows, and advertising campaigns. It is taught, or at least repeated, in schools: one survey cited in the research for this article found that nearly half of schoolteachers worldwide believed it. And in the United States, a 2013 survey found that around 65 percent of Americans believed that we only use 10 percent of our brains. A belief held by two out of three people, and by nearly half of the people whose job is to teach children how the world works, deserves to be taken seriously — not because it is true, but because its persistence tells us something important about how myths survive.

The question is simple enough to state. Is there any part of the human brain that sits permanently idle? Does any brain scan show a vast dark region that never lights up? Has any neurologist ever found a patient who lost a large part of their brain and noticed nothing? The answer to all three questions is no, and the evidence has been available for decades.

## Where the Myth Came From: A Misquote With a Century of Momentum

Nobody knows exactly who first said "10 percent," and that uncertainty is itself revealing. A real scientific finding has a paper, a date, and an author. This claim has none of those. What it has instead is a genealogy — a chain of misquotations, each one slightly bolder than the last, stretching back more than a hundred years.

The trail usually starts with William James, the Harvard psychologist and philosopher who is often called the father of American psychology. In 1907, James published an essay called *The Energies of Men*, in which he argued that people generally draw on only a small part of their "possible mental and physical resources." James was writing about motivation and effort, not about anatomy. His point was that most of us live well below what we are capable of when we are pushed — by danger, by love, by duty, by a crisis — to use our full energy. He never mentioned a percentage. He never said anything about unused brain tissue. He was describing the gap between what we do on an ordinary day and what we might do in extraordinary circumstances, a gap anyone who has ever surprised themselves under pressure will recognise.

That distinction — between potential and anatomy — was lost almost immediately. James's remark about human potential was retold as a remark about the brain itself, and then somebody attached a number to it. The first appearance of the specific "10 percent" figure in print is usually dated to 1929, when an advertisement in the *World Almanac* made the claim. An advertisement, not a laboratory. From there it spread through the self-help literature of the 1930s, where it found its most important amplifier.

That amplifier was Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, published in 1936 and one of the best-selling books of the twentieth century. Carnegie himself did not invent the figure. It appeared in the book's foreword, written by the journalist Lowell Thomas, who paraphrased James and proposed that we use only about 10 percent of our brain. Thomas took a philosopher's observation about human potential, added a number James never used, and attached it to the brain. Millions of readers met the claim for the first time in the pages of a book they trusted, written by a man they admired, and the number stuck. Carnegie went on to sell tens of millions of copies, and the 10 percent figure travelled with them into living rooms, offices, and classrooms all over the world.

The myth then did what myths do: it acquired a better authority. Somewhere along the way, the claim began to be attributed to Albert Einstein. Einstein never said it. The psychologist Barry Beyerstein, who spent years tracing the claim's history, contacted the Albert Einstein Archives at the California Institute of Technology in 2004 and asked them to search for any record of Einstein making such a statement. They found nothing. There is no letter, no lecture, no interview, and no notebook entry in which Einstein says anything of the sort. The attribution survives because it is useful: if Einstein said it, who are you to argue? But the archive says he did not, and the people who repeat the attribution have never checked.

A second, more respectable-looking origin is sometimes offered: the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield, the first director of the Montreal Neurological Institute, who mapped the human brain by stimulating the cortex of conscious patients during surgery in the mid-twentieth century. Penfield found that stimulating some regions produced obvious sensations or movements, while stimulating others — large areas of the cortex — produced no obvious response at all. Those regions were sometimes called "silent cortex," and the name encouraged the idea that they did nothing. In fact, Penfield's finding meant only that those regions were not wired directly to muscles or senses. They were doing other work — the higher work of memory, planning, language, and thought — which cannot be switched on and off with a probe. The "silent cortex" was never silent. It was simply speaking a language the probe could not hear.

By the end of the twentieth century, the myth had everything it needed to survive indefinitely: a number, a famous name, a plausible-sounding origin story, and a promise that flattered everyone who heard it. All it lacked was evidence. It still does.

## What Brain Scans Actually Show

The decisive evidence against the 10 percent myth comes from a technology that did not exist when the myth was born: functional brain imaging. Functional magnetic resonance imaging, known as fMRI, and positron emission tomography, known as PET, allow researchers to watch a living brain at work in real time, tracking blood flow and energy use as different regions switch on and off. If 90 percent of the brain were permanently idle, these scans would show it at once — a vast dark continent with a small bright patch in one corner. They show the opposite.

What the scans show is that no part of the brain is completely inactive, and that most of the brain is in use most of the time, even when a person is doing something very simple. The pattern of activity changes from moment to moment — the visual cortex lights up when you look at something, the motor cortex when you move, the language areas when you speak — but the baseline never drops to zero anywhere. Even during sleep, large parts of the brain stay active, consolidating memories and maintaining the body. As the Society for Neuroscience puts it on its public education site, BrainFacts.org: "It's a myth! We use 100% of our brains, and they're active even when we're asleep."

The neurologist Barry Gordon of the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine has been the myth's most quoted critic, and his verdict is blunt. "We use virtually every part of the brain," he told *Scientific American*, "and [most of] the brain is active almost all the time." He added a detail that makes the point in a single sentence: the brain represents about 3 percent of the body's weight and uses 20 percent of the body's energy. Other estimates put the weight closer to 2 percent, but the ratio is the same either way. No organ that consumed a fifth of everything you eat would be left 90 percent idle. Evolution does not build expensive machinery and then leave it switched off. A body that spent that much energy on a mostly unused brain would be outcompeted by one that spent it on muscle, immunity, or reproduction — and natural selection is not sentimental about waste.

John Henley, a neurologist at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, put it even more plainly in the same *Scientific American* article: "Evidence would show over a day you use 100 percent of the brain." Note the phrase "over a day." At any single instant, only some regions are working hard. That is the grain of truth inside the myth, and it is worth understanding properly, because it explains how the myth fooled so many people for so long. But over the course of a normal day — walking, talking, eating, reading, remembering, sleeping — every region gets used. The brain is not a building with 90 percent of its rooms locked. It is a building where different rooms are busy at different times, and over a day, all of them are used.

One of the most elegant pieces of evidence comes from the discovery of the brain's "default mode network." In the early 2000s, the neuroscientist Marcus Raichle and his colleagues showed that a vast, interconnected system of brain regions remains highly active and synchronised even when a person is lying quietly with their eyes closed, doing nothing at all. The brain at rest is not idle. It is running a background process — replaying memories, planning, imagining, monitoring the body — that consumes nearly as much energy as focused thought. Raichle's research, published in the *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, found that the brain's energy consumption rises by less than 5 percent when you shift from rest to a focused mental task. If 90 percent of the brain were dormant, focused thinking would require a huge surge of energy as dormant tissue switched on. Instead, the surge is tiny, because the tissue was already working.

## The Evidence From Brain Damage

If the 10 percent claim were true, it would make a very specific prediction: damage to the "unused" 90 percent should have little or no effect. A person could lose a large part of their brain and carry on as normal, because the lost tissue was doing nothing anyway. That prediction has been tested, unintentionally and tragically, thousands of times — in strokes, tumours, accidents, and surgery — and it fails every time.

The psychologist Barry Beyerstein, who became the world's leading expert on what he called "neuromythology" — the study of myths about brain structure and function — set out six kinds of evidence against the 10 percent myth, and the evidence from brain damage was the most direct. Research on brain-damaged patients shows that a lesion to almost any brain area produces at least some psychological deficit. There is no safe zone. Damage to the frontal lobes changes personality and judgement. Damage to the temporal lobes damages memory and language. Damage to the parietal lobes disrupts spatial awareness and attention. Even small strokes in seemingly minor regions leave measurable traces. If 90 percent of the brain were spare capacity, neurosurgeons would have found the spare parts long ago, and stroke wards would be full of people who had lost half their brain and felt fine. They are not.

The same point is made by the history of brain surgery itself. When surgeons remove brain tissue to treat severe epilepsy, they do so with extreme care, mapping the tissue first, because removing even a small piece of the wrong region can cost a patient their speech, their memory, or their ability to recognise faces. The fact that such mapping is necessary — that every region has a job worth protecting — is itself proof that the brain has no large unused reserves. Surgeons do not tiptoe around 90 percent of an organ because it is decorative.

There is also the simple matter of what happens when the brain is injured and then recovers. After a stroke, patients often regain lost abilities over weeks and months, and this recovery is sometimes offered as evidence that unused brain areas take over. That is a misunderstanding of what recovery actually involves. The brain recovers by rewiring — by forming new connections between surviving regions and by shifting work to neighbouring tissue that was already busy with related jobs. It is more like a company reorganising after losing staff than a company discovering an empty floor it never knew it had. The tissue that takes over was never idle. It was doing something else, and now it does two things, less well.

A related piece of evidence comes from the energy argument, which Beyerstein also listed. The brain is enormously costly to the rest of the body in oxygen and nutrients. At rest, it consumes roughly a fifth of the body's energy while accounting for only about 2 percent of its weight. That ratio — ten times more energy per gram than the body as a whole — is the signature of an organ working hard, not an organ mostly switched off. And the evolutionary argument is equally simple: a brain that was 90 percent unused would be a huge liability. Our ancestors' brains tripled in size over a few million years, and that growth was paid for with longer childhoods, harder births, and enormous calorie demands. Natural selection would not have funded that expansion for tissue that did nothing. The brain is expensive precisely because it is used.

Finally, there is the evidence from electrical stimulation, which goes back to Penfield himself. When researchers stimulate the cortex with electrodes, they find no large region that fails to respond. Some regions respond with movement or sensation, others with memory, emotion, or language. None of them respond with nothing. The "silent cortex" was an artefact of asking the wrong question — of looking only for movement and sensation and missing everything else the brain does.

## The Kernel of Truth: Why the Myth Feels Right

A myth that survives for a century usually contains a grain of truth, and the 10 percent myth is no exception. The grain is real, and it is worth stating clearly, because understanding it is the difference between debunking the myth and merely dismissing it.

At any given moment, only a fraction of your brain is highly active. When you are reading this sentence, your visual cortex and your language areas are working hard, while the regions that control your legs are comparatively quiet. When you go for a run, the balance reverses. Brain imaging makes this visible: scans show bright patches of increased activity against a dimmer background, and it is easy to look at such an image and conclude that only the bright patches are "on." But that is a misreading of what the scan shows. An fMRI scan measures *relative change* — regions where blood oxygen rises above a baseline — not regions that are otherwise switched off. The dim areas are not dormant. They are active at their baseline level, doing the quiet, constant work of keeping you alive, balanced, and aware. The bright patch is simply working harder than usual at that moment.

Barry Gordon acknowledged this nuance directly. "What is correct," he said, "is that at certain moments in anyone's life, such as when we are simply at rest and thinking, we may be using only 10 percent of our brains." At rest, in other words, the fraction of the brain working at peak intensity can be small. But that is a statement about a moment, not about a lifetime, and it says nothing about unused tissue. A car uses only a fraction of its engine's power while idling at a traffic light, but no one concludes that 90 percent of the engine is decorative.

There is a second grain of truth, and it is the one Gordon himself offered as the myth's real meaning. "Ultimately," he said, "it's not that we use 10 percent of our brains, merely that we only understand about 10 percent of how it functions." That is a humbling and probably accurate statement about neuroscience. We know a great deal about which regions do what, but the deeper questions — how consciousness arises, how memories are stored, how a network of cells produces a mind — remain largely unsolved. The myth takes our genuine ignorance about the brain and turns it into a false claim about the brain's idleness. The first is honest. The second is not.

The myth also survives because it flatters us. It tells us that our failures are not our fault — that we are not limited, merely unactivated — and that the gap between who we are and who we could be is a technical problem with a purchasable solution. That is a much more comfortable story than the truth, which is that intelligence and ability are built slowly, through learning, practice, and effort, and that there is no shortcut. The myth sells the shortcut. The truth sells the work. It is not hard to see which one moves more product.

This is also why the myth overlaps with other brain myths this site has examined. The idea that <a href="/blog/does-alcohol-kill-brain-cells-fact-check/">does alcohol kill brain cells</a> is a separate claim, but it comes from the same place: a simple, scary story about the brain that spreads faster than the evidence. So does the idea that <a href="/blog/does-listening-to-mozart-make-babies-smarter-fact-check/">does listening to Mozart make babies smarter</a>, which also began with a real finding and ended as a sales pitch. And the claim that <a href="/blog/can-stress-turn-your-hair-gray-fact-check/">can stress turn your hair gray</a> follows the same pattern — a kernel of biology, wrapped in exaggeration, sold as certainty. The 10 percent myth is the oldest and most successful member of this family, and it survives for the same reason the others do: it is simple, it is flattering, and it is wrong in a way that is hard to notice without a brain scanner.

## Why Hollywood Keeps It Alive

No myth survives for a century on books alone. The 10 percent claim owes its modern fame to the screen, where it has been repeated so often that many people first heard it not from a book but from a film. The most famous recent example is *Lucy*, the 2014 action film starring Scarlett Johansson, in which a character explains that humans use only about 10 percent of their brain capacity — and then gains superhuman powers as her brain usage climbs toward 100 percent. The film was a global hit, and the myth rode along with it into cinemas on every continent. As of August 2024, *Lucy* was still appearing in Netflix's Global Top 10, a decade after its release, which means a new generation of viewers is still meeting the claim for the first time in exactly the form that makes it most believable: delivered with confidence, by an authority figure, in the middle of an exciting story.

The film's premise is dramatic, but it is also a precise demonstration of the myth's logic. If 90 percent of the brain is unused, then unlocking it should produce something extraordinary — telekinesis, time travel, omniscience. And since no drug, device, or technique has ever produced any of those things, the premise is self-refuting. A century of people trying to unlock the unused 90 percent has produced no superhumans, no sudden geniuses, and no measurable jump in ability. What it has produced is a long list of products that do not work and a long list of customers who were disappointed quietly enough that the next product could be sold to someone else.

Television has played its part too. In 2010, the science entertainment show *MythBusters* took the claim seriously enough to test it. The hosts put a volunteer through a complicated mental task while scanning their brain with magnetoencephalography and functional magnetic resonance imaging — the most sensitive tools available — and measured how much of the brain was involved. They found that as much as 35 percent of the brain was active during a single test, far more than the myth allows, and that was during one task, measured at one moment. Over a whole day, the figure would be far higher. The episode's conclusion was unambiguous: the myth was busted. It is worth noting what the test did *not* find. It did not find a dark 90 percent. It did not find any region that never lit up. It found a brain working hard, all over, all at once.

## What the Myth Costs

It is tempting to treat the 10 percent myth as harmless — a piece of trivia that is wrong but fun, like the idea that goldfish have three-second memories or that lightning never strikes the same place twice. But it has real costs, and they fall on the people least able to afford them.

The first cost is money. The promise of untapped brain power is one of the oldest sales pitches in the self-improvement industry, and it has been used to sell everything from "brain-training" software to supplements, headsets, and courses that claim to raise intelligence by activating dormant regions. None of them can do what they promise, because there is nothing dormant to activate. The customers who buy them are usually people who are anxious about their memory, their children's school performance, or their own cognitive decline — people who deserve honest help and instead get a myth with a price tag.

The second cost is to education. When nearly half of the world's schoolteachers believe that their students are using only a tenth of their brains, that belief shapes how they teach. It encourages the idea that learning is about unlocking hidden potential rather than building knowledge step by step, and it makes teachers and parents vulnerable to programmes that promise dramatic results with no effort. The truth about learning is less exciting and more useful: the brain changes with practice. New experiences alter grey and white matter. Skills grow through repetition, feedback, and sleep. That is the real story of how brains improve, and it does not need a myth to make it interesting.

The third cost is to how we think about the brain itself. The 10 percent myth teaches people to think of the brain as a machine with a hidden reserve — a fuel tank that is 90 percent full and waiting. The reality is more interesting and more hopeful. The brain is not an underused engine. It is a living organ that is already working at full capacity, all the time, on your behalf: keeping you breathing, balancing, remembering, deciding, and dreaming, even while you sleep. The wonder is not that we use so little of it. The wonder is that we use all of it, every day, without noticing.

## The Verdict

Do we only use 10 percent of our brain? No. The claim is **False**.

There is no scientific evidence for it, and there is overwhelming evidence against it. Brain imaging with fMRI and PET shows that no region of the brain is permanently inactive, and that most of the brain is in use most of the time. Damage to almost any part of the brain causes measurable harm, which would be impossible if 90 percent of it were spare. The brain consumes about a fifth of the body's energy while accounting for only about 2 percent of its weight — the signature of an organ working hard, not one mostly switched off. Over the course of a normal day, as the Mayo Clinic's John Henley put it, "you use 100 percent of the brain." The Society for Neuroscience says the same thing in plainer language: "It's a myth! We use 100% of our brains, and they're active even when we're asleep."

The claim's history explains its survival. It began as a philosopher's remark about human potential, acquired a number from an advertisement and a self-help foreword, was falsely attributed to Einstein, was propped up by a misunderstanding of brain surgery, and was then broadcast to the world by Hollywood. At every step, it was repeated by people who had no reason to doubt it and every reason to believe it — because it flatters us, because it promises a shortcut, and because it is much easier to repeat than to check.

The real story is better than the myth. Your brain is not a locked room with 90 percent of its space empty. It is a busy, expensive, fully occupied organ that is already doing everything it can for you — and the way to make it better is not to unlock a secret reserve but to use it well: to learn, to practise, to sleep, and to pay attention. That is slower than a miracle and less profitable than a myth. It also happens to be true.
