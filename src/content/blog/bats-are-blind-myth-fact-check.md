On a warm night, somewhere far from city lights, you might stand beneath the emergence of a bat colony and feel the sky move. Thousands of small shapes pour out of a cave mouth or a forest canopy, flickering against the last blue of dusk, splitting into individual flight paths with the precision of a murmuration turned inside out. It is a sight that has unnerved people for centuries — silent wings, erratic flight, creatures threading a darkness in which human eyes see almost nothing.

And for centuries, a great many people drew exactly the wrong conclusion from that spectacle. If bats fly in darkness where we cannot see, they reasoned, they must not need their eyes at all. They must be blind. The idea was so confident that it hardened into language itself: "blind as a bat," an English idiom for having terrible eyesight, recorded in print as early as the 1600s. Shakespeare-era proverb books treated bat blindness as settled fact.

Modern science has treated it as a testable claim — and found it empty. Bats are not blind. No bat species is blind. They have functioning eyes, they use them every night, and many see quite well by any standard you care to name. Some species can see ultraviolet light, a capability no human possesses. The largest bats see details at dusk that humans can only make out in bright daylight, and fruit bats — the flying foxes of the tropics — navigate, find food, and recognize landmarks primarily with vision and smell rather than sound. Echolocation, the famous "seeing with sound," is real and spectacular, but it is not the whole story. It is, for most bats, half of a two-channel perception system in which the eyes do far more work than the myth allows.

ProofNews investigated the claim that bats are blind, consulted the research literature and conservation experts, and reached the verdict that the saying's premise is false. What follows is how two thousand years of misunderstanding survived, what scientists actually know about bat eyes, and why the answer matters more than a debunked proverb.

## The Claim, As It Circulates

"Bats are blind" wears several costumes. In its bluntest form, it holds that bats have no working eyes at all — that evolution, having given them sonar, dispensed with vision entirely. In its softer form, it claims bats' eyes are vestigial or useless, decorative leftovers from an earlier stage of evolution, and that they fly and hunt purely by echolocation. A third version, more common in classrooms than anyone would like, concedes that bats have eyes but insists they are "virtually blind," seeing little more than light and dark. The idiom "blind as a bat" packages all of these into a single shrug: everybody knows bats can't see.

The softer versions are the ones worth engaging with seriously, because they contain a grain of truth — and that grain is what keeps the myth alive. Echolocation is genuinely central to the lives of many bats. But "important for hunting" is a long way from "the only sense that works," and the science is unambiguous about which of these is true. The Smithsonian Institution states it flatly: all bats can see, even though vision may be less important than other senses in many species. Bat Conservation International is even more direct: bats are not blind — despite the common phrase, all bats have functional eyes and can see. A dedicated fact-check of the claim "bats are blind and rely only on echolocation" rated it false with high confidence, citing behavioral experiments, eye-movement research, anatomical comparisons, and genetic evidence for the visual machinery of the bat eye.

Note the structure of the claim being checked. The literal proposition "bats are blind" does not mean "bats see poorly compared with eagles." It means bats cannot see. And that is false for every one of the more than 1,400 bat species alive today — roughly a fifth of all mammal species on Earth, making bats one of the most diverse groups of mammals anywhere.

## The Core Fact: Every Bat Can See

Let us settle the headline fact first, because everything else is commentary on it. Bats are mammals, and like other mammals they have eyes with retinas, lenses, and optic nerves that work. There is no known bat species in which vision is absent. Their eyes are packed with rod photoreceptors — the light-sensitive cells that make night vision possible — which is why the nocturnal lifestyle of bats is a product of excellent low-light eyesight, not of eyes that have given up.

That bats use their eyes, not merely possess them, is established by several independent lines of evidence. Behavioral experiments show bats responding to visual cues in the wild and in the laboratory: light cues help bats know when to leave the roost for the night, and the movement of fluttering wings can help orient hunting bats. Controlled eye-movement studies have provided direct evidence of visual reflexes in bats — the kind of involuntary ocular responses that only a functioning visual system produces. And at the genetic level, research into opsin genes — the genes that build the light-sensing proteins in the eye — shows that bats carry the machinery for color vision, though some lineages have lost short-wavelength (blue and ultraviolet) sensitivity while others have retained it. You do not evolve and maintain that machinery if you cannot see.

The variation, though, is real and important, and any honest account must state it plainly. Not all bats see equally well. Bats comprise an enormous range of species with different eyes, diets, habitats, and echolocation strategies. Some bats see far better than others. The myth is false, but its mirror image — "all bats see perfectly" — would be false too. The interesting story is the one in between.

## Two Bats, Two Toolkits: Megabats and Microbats

Bat diversity sorts, for most purposes, into two broad groups: the megabats, or fruit bats — flying foxes and their relatives — and the microbats, the smaller insect-eaters that make up most of the order. Their senses are organized along different lines, and the difference is the key that unlocks the whole myth.

Megabats, as a rule, do not echolocate at all. The great fruit bats that sail over tropical orchards at dusk find their food the way a sharp-eyed primate might: with large eyes, keen vision, and an excellent sense of smell. Their eyes are big — disproportionately large for their heads — and well-developed retinas serve them in the dim light of twilight and night. Rob Mies, the former executive director of the Organization for Bat Conservation, told National Geographic that bigger bats can see up to three times better than humans. That figure should be taken as the vivid comparison it is rather than a laboratory precision, but the direction is right: for a fruit bat, eyesight is the primary instrument, and it is a good one.

Microbats are the echolocators — the bats that hunt moths and mosquitoes by bouncing ultrasound off them and reading the echoes. But even here, vision has not been discarded. The worldanimalrescuenetwork's review of bat senses notes that many microbats combine vision with echolocation, and researchers studying how bats hunt have found that the two senses work together constantly. Vision handles what it handles best — detecting the horizon, orienting at dusk, avoiding large obstacles, and seeing at distances where echolocation fails — while echolocation takes over the fine detail of tracking small prey in darkness. To call these animals blind is roughly like calling a human pilot blind because the plane also has radar.

## Vision by the Numbers

How good is bat eyesight, exactly? Scientists cannot hand a bat an eye chart, so they measure visual acuity in cycles per degree — a measure of how fine a detail an eye can resolve, with more cycles meaning sharper vision. Human visual acuity falls between about 45 and 60 cycles per degree, depending on lighting. Against that benchmark, Bat Conservation International reports a revealing spread. The rufous horseshoe bat, a small insect-eating bat with correspondingly small eyes, manages only about 0.35 cycles per degree — genuinely poor detail vision by human standards, though its eyes still function for orientation and light detection. But the larger-eyed species tell a different story: the carnivorous Australian ghost bat and the Indian flying fox, a fruit-eating megabat that does not echolocate, resolve around two cycles per degree.

Two cycles per degree is a long way from human 20/20, but the comparison undersells what those eyes accomplish, because it measures detail in bright light rather than performance in darkness. Bat eyes are specialized for a niche humans barely occupy: the dim edge between day and night, when rods outperform cones and the premium is on sensitivity rather than sharpness. In that niche, many bats see far better than the numbers suggest to a human reader. And the sheer spread of the figures — from 0.35 to 2 cycles per degree across species — is itself evidence against the myth. A group of animals converging on the same sensory organ with wildly different refinements is not a group that has abandoned vision. It is a group that has invested in it in different ways.

## The Eyes That See Ultraviolet

Among the most striking discoveries about bat vision is that some bats can see colors humans cannot. Researchers reported in a 2009 study in the journal PLOS ONE that two small bats from South and Central America — Pallas's long-tongued bat and Seba's short-tailed bat — have visual receptors enabling them to see in daylight and to see some colors, with some of the receptors possibly enabling ultraviolet vision. Other bat species have since been found to share this ultraviolet capability.

The finding makes ecological sense. Many flowers reflect ultraviolet light, and both of the studied species rely on plants for food — the long-tongued bat laps nectar like a hummingbird, while the short-tailed bat eats a mix of fruit, flowers, and insects. An eye that can read UV patterns on blossoms is an eye tuned to its owner's livelihood. Live Science's coverage of this research put the implication bluntly: bats sometimes prefer using eyesight over sound when hunting, depending on the circumstances. The blind-bat narrative has no room for a creature that selects flowers by their ultraviolet signatures, and that is because the narrative was never built from observation in the first place.

## What Echolocation Really Does

Echolocation deserves its due, because it is the grain of truth around which the myth grew. The system was first documented in 1939, when Donald Griffin working in a Harvard laboratory showed that bats were navigating with sound waves and echoes — and subsequent decades revealed it as one of evolution's great sensory masterworks. Bats vocalize calls from low-pitch to ultrasonic frequencies — in some species above 200 kilohertz, far beyond human hearing — and paint a picture of their surroundings from the returning echoes. The large, intricately shaped ears of many microbats exist to gather and process as much of that sound data as possible, much as a radar antenna is shaped to its task. Some moths have evolved countermeasures, dropping out of the air when hit by a bat's vocalizations — an evolutionary arms race measured in milliseconds.

But the most important research finding about echolocation is not that it exists. It is that it works together with vision rather than replacing it. Researchers at Tel Aviv University and elsewhere have shown that bats integrate two streams of information — one from vision and one from echolocation — into a single composite image of the world. Their measurements found that echolocation was about twice as effective as vision for detecting insects in medium to low light — picking out insects from roughly 40 feet away versus 20 feet by vision — and that echolocation, unlike vision, is unaffected by visual clutter like vegetation, which degrades visual range three- to five-fold. Dr. Yovel of the university's Sagol School of Neuroscience summarized the finding: "We believe that bats are constantly integrating two streams of information — one from vision and one from echolocation — to create a single image of the world." The composite, the researchers concluded, has a higher definition than vision alone could provide.

Note what that implies. An animal that builds its picture of the world from two channels — enhancing one with the other — is not an animal whose eyes are decorative. It is an animal with a hybrid perceptual system, and it uses the visual channel where vision wins. Where does vision win? At distance. Echolocation is poor at detecting large objects far away, while vision can detect large objects at distances orders of magnitude greater than echolocation's range. So bats use vision mostly for orientation, navigation, and avoiding large distant objects, and echolocation for the close work of finding small prey. Different species blend the two in different proportions, which is one reason there are so many bat species with so many specializations — and why they own the night.

## Why We Got It Wrong for Two Thousand Years

If the evidence is so one-sided, why did the myth survive so long? The answer is a story about how humans observe — and about how easily we mistake the limits of our own senses for the limits of other animals' senses.

Written evidence of the belief that bats are blind reaches back to the fourth century BCE. In his *Metaphysics*, Aristotle compared the eyes of bats to the light of day: just as bats' eyes, he wrote, cannot take in the brightness of daylight, so the human intellect cannot fully grasp the most self-evident things. He was not conducting zoology — he was borrowing a natural image for philosophy — but the borrowing reveals what educated Greeks already assumed: that bats' eyes were somehow defective. Modern commentators suggest Aristotle was working from the sight of bats roosting in caves and other dark places, and he understood nothing of the rod-packed retinas that make those eyes superb instruments for darkness.

The idea persisted through the millennium that followed largely because nobody checked. Natural history before the modern era leaned heavily on received opinion, and the nocturnal bat — visible only as a flicker at the edge of lamplight, impossible to examine closely in the wild — was a blank canvas for assumption. When the English idiom took shape, the Oxford English Dictionary finds "blind as a bat" in a 1639 compendium of English and Latin proverbs, with variants like "blind as a bat at noon" and a mid-17th-century flavor suggesting the phrase was already old when it was written down. The Oxford Dictionary of Idioms traces the image to the bat's nocturnal habits and its disoriented fluttering when disturbed by day: a bat dragged blinking into noon sunshine behaves clumsily, and observers concluded — from the performance of a night creature in daylight — that its eyes were defective. It is the equivalent of judging a whale's fitness by watching it on a beach.

Usage of the phrase tapered off around the mid-20th century, perhaps as popular zoology caught up with the laboratory evidence. But the belief outlived its peak popularity, carried forward by habit, cartoons, and the undeniable theatricality of a creature that hunts in pitch darkness. Nineteenth-century periodicals and natural history journals described nocturnal animals as mysterious or deprived of sight, and the assumption — night equals darkness equals incapacity — went unexamined because the instruments to examine it, like ophthalmoscopy and electroretinography, did not yet exist.

## The Honest Nuance: Not Every Bat Sees Equally

A fact-check that simply replaced "bats are blind" with "bats all see brilliantly" would be trading one oversimplification for another. The evidence requires the qualifier, and stating it strengthens rather than weakens the debunking.

Visual acuity and low-light performance vary substantially among bat species, as the cycles-per-degree figures showed. Some lineages have lost short-wavelength sensitivity in their opsin genes, meaning their color vision is more limited than that of their ultraviolet-seeing cousins. Small insect-eating bats with tiny eyes genuinely rely more on their ears than their eyes for the critical work of catching prey. Egyptian fruit bats, by contrast, have been shown to use vision alone for discriminating object shapes, and to deploy vision and echolocation differently depending on the task at hand.

This is the sense in which the myth's distant cousins survive: "bats rely mostly on hearing" is true for many microbats in the context of hunting, and "some bats see poorly by human standards" is true of some species. Neither is remotely the same as "bats are blind." The fact-checkers who rated the original claim false with high confidence were careful to add exactly this caveat — that replacing the myth with a claim that all bats see equally well would be inaccurate. Precision is not pedantry here. It is the difference between mythology and biology.

## The Verdict

The claim that bats are blind is **False**.

Every bat species has functional eyes, and bats use them — to orient at dusk, to detect the horizon, to avoid large obstacles at distances echolocation cannot reach, to time their nightly emergence, and, for the great fruit bats, to find food and navigate as their primary sense. Many bats see quite well in low light, some species see ultraviolet light humans cannot perceive, and the largest bats see details in the dimness that would challenge human vision. Echolocation is real and remarkable, but it is a complement to vision, not a replacement for it: research shows bats continuously integrate both streams into a single, higher-definition picture of the world.

The claim failed on anatomy, behavior, and genetics — three independent lines of evidence pointing the same way. Its longevity tells us about human habits of mind, not about bat eyes: about Aristotle's borrowed metaphor, about 17th-century observers judging a night creature by its performance at noon, and about the ease with which a vivid phrase outlives the ignorance that coined it. No bat is blind. They were never blind. We were simply looking at the wrong thing.

The next time the phrase "blind as a bat" comes up — at the dinner table, in a pub quiz, in a piece of writing that could use a sharper metaphor — you have something better to say. Tell them the bats can see the sky. Tell them some of them can see colors we cannot. And if the night sky obliges with a passing colony, watch them thread the dark on two senses at once, and ask yourself which of you is really the one flying blind.
