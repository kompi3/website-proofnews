It is one of those facts that sounds too good to check. Every summer, the story goes, the Eiffel Tower grows about 15 centimetres — roughly six inches — taller, as the Paris sun warms its iron frame and the whole monument stretches toward the sky. The claim appears in trivia books, classroom posters, travel guides and listicles, usually stated with total confidence and no caveats. It is the kind of fact people repeat at dinner parties because it makes the world feel stranger and more alive than expected.

Here is the awkward part: the most interesting version of this story is not quite the version being told. The Eiffel Tower really does change height with the temperature. That part is solid physics, confirmed by the tower's own operator and by more than a century of engineering practice. But the famous 15-centimetre figure — the number that makes the claim memorable — describes something else entirely. It is a real measurement, attached to the wrong phenomenon. The tower's seasonal height change, according to its own operator, is measured in millimetres, not centimetres. The 15 centimetres belongs to a daily sideways wobble, not a summer growth spurt.

So does the Eiffel Tower grow taller in summer? Yes. By 15 centimetres? That is where the story goes wrong, and the way it goes wrong is a perfect little lesson in how a true fact can be bent into a misleading one without anyone inventing anything at all.

## The claim, as it is usually told

The claim in its standard form runs like this: because the Eiffel Tower is made of iron, and because iron expands when heated, the tower stands up to 15 centimetres taller on a hot summer day than on a cold winter day. Some versions phrase it as a seasonal fact — the tower is taller in July than in January. Others phrase it as a daily fact — the tower is taller in the afternoon than in the morning. The number is almost always the same: 15 centimetres, or six inches.

The claim has everything a viral fact needs. It involves a world-famous landmark, it is counterintuitive, it is easy to picture, and it comes with a precise-sounding number that makes it feel measured rather than guessed. It also has a grain of genuine science at its core, which is exactly why it survives. Claims that are entirely false tend to collapse under the first serious check. Claims that are half true can run for decades, because every check confirms the true half and the false half rides along unexamined.

The Eiffel Tower is a particularly good host for this kind of claim. It is one of the most measured structures on Earth, monitored with modern sensors, and its engineering history is unusually well documented. That means the evidence needed to sort the true half from the misleading half actually exists — you just have to look at what each source is really measuring.

## The physics: why the tower must change height

The underlying physics is not in dispute anywhere. When a metal warms up, its atoms vibrate more energetically and push slightly further apart, so the material gets longer. When it cools, the atoms settle back and the material shrinks. Engineers call the size of this effect the coefficient of linear thermal expansion, and for the puddled iron used in the Eiffel Tower it is about 12 millionths of a metre per metre per degree Celsius — written as 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C.

That sounds vanishingly small, and on a one-metre bar it is: a single degree of warming stretches a metre of iron by just 0.012 millimetres, far too small to see. But the Eiffel Tower is not a one-metre bar. Its iron structure rises about 300 metres above the Champ de Mars (330 metres including the antennas added later, in 1957, 2000 and 2022). Multiply a tiny effect by 300 metres and by a temperature swing of tens of degrees, and the result stops being invisible.

Take a plausible summer-to-winter comparison. If the iron swings by 40 °C between a freezing winter night and a sun-baked summer afternoon, the arithmetic gives 300 × 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 40 = 0.144 metres, or about 14.4 centimetres. With a more modest 15 °C swing, the same calculation gives 5.4 centimetres. The answer, in other words, depends entirely on which temperature swing you assume — and that dependence turns out to be the whole story.

There is a second complication that matters just as much. The sun does not heat the tower evenly. At any given moment it shines on one face of the lattice while the others sit in shade, so the sunlit side expands more than the shaded sides and the tower bends very slightly, leaning away from the sun. As the sun crosses the sky, the lean follows it. Over the course of a clear day, the tip of the tower traces a small roughly circular path. This daily dance is real, measurable, and — crucially — it is where the famous 15 centimetres actually comes from.

## What the tower's own operator says

The Eiffel Tower is operated by the Société d'Exploitation de la Tour Eiffel, usually shortened to SETE, and its official site, toureiffel.paris, publishes a short explainer on the tower's thermal behaviour. That explainer, dated 3 February 2021, is the single most authoritative source on this question, and it says something that should surprise anyone who knows only the viral version.

According to the operator, the seasonal change in the tower's structural height is "a few millimetres" — the framework gains a few millimetres in warmer conditions and gives them back as winter temperatures fall. That is the official figure for the tower actually getting taller. It is not fifteen centimetres. It is not even one centimetre.

On the very same page, the operator gives a second figure: on a clear day, the uneven heating of the tower's faces causes its tip to trace a circular path "measuring approximately 15 centimetres in diameter." That is a sideways movement — the tip wandering in a small circle as the tower leans first one way and then another — not a vertical gain. The 15 centimetres is real, official, and precisely documented. It is just not a height increase.

This is the mix-up at the heart of the claim. Somewhere along the chain of retelling, the 15-centimetre circle traced by the tip became 15 centimetres of extra height. The number survived; its meaning did not. And because the number is genuine and comes from an official source, the distorted version feels sourced even though the sourcing actually contradicts it.

## What the experts say: the 12 to 15 centimetre estimate

If the operator says a few millimetres, why do so many serious explainers still say 12 to 15 centimetres? The answer is that there are two different ways of answering the question, and they give different numbers because they make different assumptions.

The most recent well-sourced treatment comes from an article published on 6 August 2025 in The Conversation, written by Federico de Isidro Gordejuela, an architecture professor at Universidad CEU San Pablo in Spain, and syndicated onward to outlets including Phys.org. That article states that specialists have estimated the tower grows between 12 and 15 centimetres when comparing its size on cold winter days with the hottest days of summer. The reasoning is exactly the arithmetic shown above: 300 metres of iron, a coefficient of 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, and a large temperature swing.

The key detail is what counts as the temperature swing. Air temperatures in Paris range from winter minimums below −20 °C to summer maximums around 40 °C, a span of 60 degrees. But the iron itself, sitting in direct sunlight, runs considerably hotter than the air around it — sun-heated metal on a clear July afternoon can be far warmer than the afternoon air temperature. If you feed the metal's own temperature into the calculation, a 40-degree swing is entirely plausible, and the 12 to 15 centimetre estimate follows naturally. If you feed in air temperatures only, or the average temperature of the whole structure rather than its hottest face, you get a much smaller number — closer to the operator's few millimetres.

So who is right? Both calculations are honest; they are answering subtly different questions. The operator is describing the change in the structure's overall height as measured in a practical, averaged sense. The 12 to 15 centimetre estimate describes the maximum expansion implied by the hottest iron on the hottest day compared with the coldest iron on the coldest night. Neither side has published an actual before-and-after measurement of the tower's height across a heatwave — every centimetre figure in circulation is a calculation from an assumed temperature swing, not a reading from a tape measure. That absence of a direct measurement is itself telling: if the tower reliably gained 15 centimetres every summer, someone would long ago have published the two measurements side by side.

## The lean: the tower's daily dance

The most charming part of the real story is the lean. Because the sun heats one side of the tower more than the others, the sunlit face expands a little more, and the whole structure curves almost imperceptibly away from the sun. The top shifts by a few centimetres. Then, as the sun moves, the warm face changes, the lean follows, and by the end of a clear day the tip has traced its roughly 15-centimetre circle.

The effect is far too small for a visitor to notice — the operator is explicit that the thermal movements are imperceptible to people on the tower — but it is large enough for instruments to track, and it is a beautiful demonstration of a principle every structural engineer has to respect. Gustave Eiffel and his engineers knew about thermal expansion when they designed the tower for the 1889 World's Fair, and the structure was detailed to accommodate movement rather than fight it. Expansion joints and the lattice design itself give the iron room to breathe.

This daily lean is also a useful reality check on the viral claim. If the tower genuinely grew 15 centimetres taller every summer, that change would be a seasonal event, not a daily one. The fact that the confirmed 15-centimetre figure is a daily lateral circle — something that happens on any clear day, in any season, whenever the sun shines unevenly — is strong evidence that the viral version has attached the right number to the wrong motion.

## Why this matters beyond one tower

Thermal expansion is not a curiosity confined to Paris. It is one of the first things engineers design around in any large structure. Bridges have expansion joints precisely so that their decks can lengthen in summer without buckling. Railway tracks are laid with gaps or pre-stressed to handle the same effect. The Garabit Viaduct, another Gustave Eiffel project, is 565 metres long, and the Forth Bridge stretches over 2.5 kilometres — on structures of that scale, a few tens of degrees of temperature change produces movements measured in tens of centimetres, and ignoring them would be catastrophic.

The Eiffel Tower is simply the most famous example, and fame is what made it vulnerable to distortion. A bridge's expansion joint is boring; a tower that grows six inches in summer is a story. The story then gets simplified in retelling — the caveats fall away, the daily circle becomes a seasonal gain, and the estimate becomes a measurement. The same simplification process shapes many of the myths this site has examined before, from the idea that you can see the Great Wall from the Moon to the belief that old window glass has flowed downward like a slow liquid. In each case, a real observation gets polished into a punchier claim than the evidence supports.

That pattern is worth noticing because it is the same pattern behind the more consequential misinformation in circulation. A genuine number, stripped of its context, is far more persuasive than an invented one — which is why checking what a number actually measures is often more useful than checking whether the number exists.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: Misleading.**

The direction of the claim is true: the Eiffel Tower really is taller in summer than in winter, because its puddled-iron frame expands as it warms and contracts as it cools. The physics is undisputed, the coefficient of expansion is well established, and the tower's own operator confirms a seasonal height change.

But the claim as it is usually told — that the tower grows 15 centimetres taller in summer — is misleading. The tower's own operator puts the seasonal height change at a few millimetres, not centimetres. The genuine official 15-centimetre figure describes something else: the roughly 15-centimetre-diameter circle traced by the tower's tip over a clear day as uneven sunlight makes it lean first one way and then another. The 12 to 15 centimetre seasonal estimate published by specialists is a calculation based on assuming a large temperature swing in sun-heated metal, not a direct measurement, and it conflicts with the operator's own figure rather than confirming it.

So the tower grows in summer — just not by the famous 15 centimetres, and not in the way the viral version suggests. The real story is subtler and, in its own way, more interesting: a 300-metre iron giant that breathes with the temperature, leans gently away from the sun each day, and returns to its shape each night, moving by amounts its visitors will never feel but its engineers planned for from the very first drawing.

## A giant thermometer

There is a final image worth keeping. Because the tower's height tracks temperature so faithfully, it has been described as a giant thermometer — a 300-metre instrument that registers the seasons in iron. That metaphor is more accurate than the viral claim, and it captures what is genuinely wonderful here without exaggerating it. The tower does not need an extra six inches to be remarkable. A structure that leans away from the sun, traces a small circle with its tip each clear day, and quietly expands and contracts with the weather for more than 130 years is remarkable enough on its own terms.

The next time the 15-centimetre fact comes up at a dinner party, you can now agree with the spirit of it and correct the letter of it — which is, after all, the most satisfying kind of fact-check. Yes, the tower grows in summer. No, not by 15 centimetres. And the real 15 centimetres, the little circle the tip draws in the sky each sunny day, is a better story than the one it replaced.
