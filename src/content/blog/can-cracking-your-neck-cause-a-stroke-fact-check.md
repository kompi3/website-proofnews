It is one of those small, satisfying rituals millions of people perform without thinking. Your neck feels stiff after a long day hunched over a laptop, so you tilt your head to one side, maybe add a gentle push with your hand, and — pop. Instant relief. Then, somewhere on social media, you see a headline about a young man who cracked his neck and suffered a stroke, and the ritual suddenly feels a lot less innocent.

So which is it? Is neck cracking a harmless habit, or a genuine gamble with the arteries that feed your brain? The honest answer sits in an uncomfortable middle ground: cracking your neck can cause a stroke, and doctors have documented it happening, but the risk is very small, and the cases that make headlines usually involve more than a single innocent pop. Understanding why requires a short tour of the anatomy of your neck, the evidence behind the warnings, and the important difference between a gentle stretch and a forceful twist.

## What actually makes the popping sound

First, a reassuring fact: the pop itself is not your bones cracking. The joints between the vertebrae in your neck are lined with capsules filled with synovial fluid, which contains dissolved gases. When you stretch or twist the joint, the joint surfaces separate slightly, pressure inside the capsule drops, and a gas bubble forms rapidly in the fluid. That sudden formation — and partial collapse — of the bubble is what you hear. It is the same basic mechanism behind cracking your knuckles, which, as we examined in our fact-check on <a href="/blog/cracking-knuckles-cause-arthritis-myth-fact-check/">whether cracking your knuckles causes arthritis</a>, does not appear to damage healthy joints in the way folk wisdom suggests.

That distinction matters, because it reframes the question. The pop is not the danger. The danger, when it exists, comes from what the movement around the pop can do to soft tissues — specifically, to two arteries that run through the bones of your neck on their way to your brain.

## The artery at the centre of the warning

Your brain is fed by four main arteries in the neck: two carotid arteries at the front and two vertebral arteries at the back. The vertebral arteries are the ones that matter here. They thread upward through small bony canals in the side arms of the cervical vertebrae, then join at the base of the brain to form the basilar artery, which supplies the brainstem and the back of the brain.

That routing is the vulnerability. Because the vertebral arteries are tethered inside those bony canals, they get stretched when the neck is rotated or bent sharply. Stretch them too far or too forcefully, and the inner lining of the artery wall can tear. Doctors call this a vertebral artery dissection, a type of cervical artery dissection.

A dissection does not always cause a stroke. Often the body repairs the tear quietly and nothing further happens. But sometimes blood leaks into the wall of the torn vessel, narrowing the channel, and a clot forms at the injury site. If that clot blocks the artery, or breaks off and lodges in a smaller vessel downstream, the part of the brain supplied by that artery is starved of oxygen. That is an ischemic stroke — the most common kind of stroke, and in this case one that strikes the brainstem, which controls balance, eye movement, swallowing, and other basic functions. That is why strokes of this type can be so frightening even when they are survivable: the territory involved is small, but it is critical.

## The cases that made headlines

The story most people have seen involves Josh Hader, a 28-year-old from Guthrie, Oklahoma. In March 2019, after about two weeks of neck stiffness, Hader stretched his neck at home, using his hand to add a little extra pressure, and heard a pop. Almost immediately, the left side of his body went numb. He struggled to walk straight, his vision doubled, and he was taken to hospital, where doctors diagnosed a stroke caused by a tear in his vertebral artery. He was treated with a clot-dissolving drug, spent four days in intensive care, and went through months of rehabilitation. He recovered enough to walk unaided and live independently, but with lingering fatigue, balance problems, and altered sensation on one side of his body — and an eye patch for several weeks while an injured nerve recovered.

Earlier in 2019, the British press reported a similar case: Natalie Kunicki, a 23-year-old paramedic, stretched her neck and suffered a stroke caused by a vertebral artery dissection. And the medical literature contains graver cases still. In one published case report, a 40-year-old man with no significant medical history was observed cracking his neck at work, developed neck pain and stroke-like symptoms soon after, and died several days later. Imaging showed dissection of both vertebral arteries, and autopsy confirmed brainstem and cerebellar infarcts.

These stories are real, and they are terrifying precisely because the people involved were young and healthy. But they are also, by every available measure, rare — and the details complicate the simple moral that "one crack equals one stroke."

Take Hader's case. Stroke specialists who reviewed it publicly noted an important wrinkle: a clot takes more than a few minutes to form after an arterial tear, so the pop he heard may not have caused the tear in that instant. Hader had been experiencing neck pain for two weeks before the event, and neck pain is itself a common early symptom of a dissection already in progress. It is quite possible, specialists argued, that the tear and clot were already developing, and that the stretch dislodged the clot rather than creating the tear from scratch. That does not make the story a hoax — the movement was still part of the chain of events — but it does mean the headline version, in which a single crack creates a stroke out of nowhere, is an oversimplification.

## What the big studies and the Heart Association actually say

The link between neck manipulation and stroke has been debated for decades, and the most authoritative summary comes from the American Heart Association and the American Stroke Association. In a scientific statement published in the journal Stroke in August 2014, a panel of neurologists and stroke specialists reviewed the evidence and concluded that cervical artery dissection is an important cause of ischemic stroke in young and middle-aged adults, that most population-based studies had found an association between cervical manipulative therapy and vertebral artery dissection stroke in young patients, and that patients should be informed of that association before undergoing neck manipulation. The statement was careful on one point, however: it said the biomechanical evidence was insufficient to prove that manipulation causes dissection, and that the incidence of manipulation-associated dissection was "probably low."

That caution reflects a genuine scientific problem. Neck pain is often the first symptom of a dissection in progress, which means some patients seek out a chiropractor or crack their own neck because of pain caused by a tear that already exists. When a stroke follows, it looks as though the manipulation caused it, even if the dissection was already under way. Researchers call this confounding, and it is the main reason the statistics are so hard to interpret. A well-known population study by Cassidy and colleagues found that the risk of stroke after a chiropractic visit was no higher than after a visit to a family doctor for the same neck pain — a finding that defenders of manipulation cite, and that critics argue underestimates the association because people with obvious stroke symptoms go to doctors, not chiropractors.

The United States National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health takes a middle position that is probably closest to the consensus: spinal manipulation by a properly trained and licensed professional is relatively safe, but people considering neck manipulation should be aware of the associated risks. Note the qualifier "properly trained and licensed." The evidence base is mostly about clinical manipulation, not about forceful self-manipulation at home, where there is no screening, no control of force, and no one checking whether your symptoms might already signal something wrong.

## Why the risk is small — but not zero

How small is small? Strokes caused by vertebral artery dissection account for only about 2 percent of ischemic strokes overall, according to Healthline's review of the evidence — though they are a disproportionately common cause of stroke in younger adults, precisely because younger adults have fewer competing stroke risks. One estimate cited in sports-medicine coverage puts injuries of any kind from neck manipulation at roughly five to ten per ten million manipulations. That figure is not a precise measurement — the true rate is genuinely uncertain, because mild dissections that heal on their own never come to medical attention — but it gives a sense of scale. Millions of people crack their necks every day; emergency departments are not filled with the consequences.

That rarity is itself part of the evidence, and it cuts both ways. It means an ordinary person who occasionally cracks their neck gently is very unlikely to come to harm. It also means that when a healthy 28-year-old does suffer a stroke after cracking his neck, doctors take it seriously rather than dismissing it as coincidence, because the mechanism is anatomically plausible, the timing fits, and the pattern has repeated in case after case.

There is also a cumulative concern for habitual crackers. Sports-medicine physicians warn that repeatedly forcing the neck can loosen the ligaments that hold the vertebrae together, a condition called ligament laxity. Looser joints allow more extreme neck movement, which in turn leaves the arteries more vulnerable to injury — and the relief itself is temporary, because cracking does nothing to fix the muscle tension or posture problem that made the neck feel stiff in the first place. The result can be a cycle: stiffness, crack, brief relief, more looseness, more stiffness. As with <a href="/blog/is-it-dangerous-to-hold-in-a-sneeze-fact-check/">holding in a sneeze</a>, another everyday action with rare but documented severe consequences, the fact that something usually ends harmlessly is not the same as proof that it is always safe.

## Self-cracking versus professional adjustment

Not all neck cracking is equal, and this is where practical advice actually helps. There is a meaningful difference between the gentle, unforced pop that happens when you turn your head during a stretch and a forceful self-manipulation in which you grip your head with one hand, your jaw with the other, and twist sharply. The second version applies far more rotational force, and rotational force is exactly what stretches the vertebral arteries. Chiropractic neck adjustments use similar high-speed, low-amplitude thrusts, which is why the professional debate exists — but a trained practitioner is at least supposed to screen for red-flag symptoms first, and to avoid extreme rotation of the upper neck.

That screening point is crucial. If your neck pain is new, severe, unlike any stiffness you have had before, or accompanied by headache, dizziness, visual changes, or numbness, that is not the moment to crack anything. Those can be symptoms of a dissection already in progress, and forceful movement can make it worse. The same logic applies in reverse: if you crack your neck regularly and the relief never lasts, the underlying problem — posture, weak neck muscles, stress, a poor sleeping position, hours of looking down at a phone — is still there, and it is worth addressing directly rather than chasing the pop.

## The warning signs you should never ignore

Most neck cracks end with nothing worse than relief. But the symptoms of a vertebral artery dissection and the stroke it can cause are worth knowing, because early treatment makes a real difference, and because the first symptoms can be subtle. Seek emergency care immediately if neck cracking or a sudden neck movement is followed by any of the following: a sudden, severe headache or neck pain unlike your usual stiffness; dizziness, loss of balance, or difficulty walking; double vision or other sudden visual changes; numbness or weakness on one side of the body or face; difficulty speaking or swallowing; or a drooping eyelid or constricted pupil on one side. Do not wait to see whether it passes, and do not try to crack the neck again to fix it.

If someone collapses or shows stroke symptoms, the correct response is to call emergency services and keep them still and comfortable — not to manipulate their neck or force anything into their mouth. ## Safer ways to deal with a stiff neck

For the ordinary stiffness that drives most neck cracking, the evidence-backed alternatives are unglamorous but effective. Gentle range-of-motion stretches — slowly turning the head side to side, tilting the ear toward the shoulder, tucking the chin — without forcing any movement to a popping endpoint. Alternating heat and cold on sore muscles. Massage, whether professional or a few minutes with a tennis ball against a wall. Over-the-counter pain relievers for short-term relief. And, most importantly, fixing the cause: raising screens to eye level, taking breaks from looking down at a phone, sleeping with a pillow that keeps the neck neutral, and strengthening the deep neck and upper-back muscles that hold the head up all day.

If neck pain persists for more than a week or two, keeps coming back, or comes with any neurological symptom — tingling in the arms, weakness, headaches that are new or worsening — see a clinician rather than self-treating. A physiotherapist can assess whether the problem is muscular, postural, or something that needs imaging, and can teach stretches that relieve stiffness without the forceful twist. And if you do choose chiropractic care, the American Heart Association's position is worth taking literally: you should be told about the statistical association with dissection before anyone manipulates your neck, and a practitioner who dismisses the risk entirely is not giving you the full picture.

None of this means you must live in fear of turning your head. Normal neck movement — looking over your shoulder while driving, stretching after a long meeting, rolling your shoulders — does not carry the same concern as a forceful, hand-assisted twist at the end of the neck's range. The human neck is built to move. The problem is only when that movement is pushed, repeatedly and forcefully, past the point where it stops being a stretch and starts being a wrench.

## The Verdict

So, can cracking your neck cause a stroke? Mostly True — with a vital qualifier. The claim is supported by documented cases, a plausible anatomical mechanism, and a scientific statement from the American Heart Association and the American Stroke Association acknowledging the association between neck manipulation and vertebral artery dissection stroke. Josh Hader's case and others like it are real, and the 40-year-old man who died after cracking his neck at work is a sobering reminder that the worst outcome, while rare, is not hypothetical.

But the viral version of the claim — that cracking your neck is likely to give you a stroke, or that one pop can tear a healthy artery out of nowhere — is misleading. The absolute risk is very low, the causal chain is often more complicated than the headline suggests, and gentle neck movement is not the same as a forceful self-manipulation. The accurate statement is this: cracking your neck can, rarely, contribute to a stroke, and the risk is highest with forceful twisting, habitual cracking, and cracking when the neck pain might already be a symptom of something wrong.

That is a nuanced verdict, but it is also a useful one. It tells you what to stop doing — gripping your head and wrenching it to chase a louder pop — without demanding that you fear every stretch. And it tells you what to watch for, which is the part that could actually matter one day: sudden severe neck pain, dizziness, double vision, or one-sided numbness after any forceful neck movement means emergency care, immediately, no exceptions.

The pop feels good because it is quick, easy, and instantly rewarding. The safest habits usually are not. A slow stretch, a raised screen, a stronger upper back — none of them delivers that satisfying crack. But none of them can tear an artery either, and when the stakes include the blood supply to your brainstem, boring is a perfectly good trade.
