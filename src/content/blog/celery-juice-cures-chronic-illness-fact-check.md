The video always starts the same way. A glass of pale green liquid, held up to the morning light. Sixteen ounces of pure celery juice, strained, no apple, no ginger, no ice — just celery, juiced alone, on an empty stomach. The person behind the camera takes a long sip and delivers the testimony like a sermon: "This cured my autoimmune disease." "My chronic fatigue is gone." "My eczema vanished. My thyroid healed. My mystery illness — gone."

Scroll through TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube, or Facebook wellness groups, and you will find thousands of testimonials like these, each more miraculous than the last. The claims are not modest. Celery juice is presented not as a healthy beverage but as a cure — for autoimmune diseases, for chronic infections, for Lyme disease, for psoriasis, for "mystery illnesses" that conventional medicine supposedly cannot name or treat. According to its loudest proponents, this unremarkable stalk vegetable, once the wallflower of the crudité platter, is the most powerful healing medicine on the planet.

It is a spectacular story. It is also, according to the scientific evidence available through early 2026, false. Celery juice is a hydrating, low-calorie vegetable drink with some real nutritional virtues. It is not a cure for chronic illness, and there is no credible evidence that it is one.

We traced the claim to its source, examined what celery actually contains, reviewed what human research has and has not shown, and asked registered dietitians and nutrition experts to weigh in. Here is what we found — the claim dissected, piece by piece.

## The Claim: A Miracle Juice for Chronic Disease

The modern celery juice craze did not grow organically out of nutrition science. It has a named inventor: Anthony William, a self-described "Medical Medium" whose book *Medical Medium*, published in 2017, launched the movement. William holds no medical or scientific certification. His own account is that he communicates with a "Spirit of Compassion" — a voice only he can hear — which provides him with what he calls "extraordinarily accurate health information that is often far ahead of its time."

Writing on Gwyneth Paltrow's wellness website Goop, William described celery juice as "a miracle juice" and "one of the greatest healing tonics of all time," adding: "I've seen thousands of people who suffer from chronic and mystery illnesses restore their health by drinking sixteen ounces of celery juice daily on an empty stomach."

Among his specific claims — none of them supported by published clinical research or consistent with basic human physiology — are that celery juice reverses inflammation by starving pathogenic bacteria and viruses, that it rebuilds hydrochloric acid in the stomach, and that it contains what he calls "cluster salts," an "undiscovered subgroup of sodium" that attacks pathogens and flushes toxins from the liver. He has said these salts neutralize Epstein-Barr virus and repair the adrenal glands.

The prescription is strikingly specific: sixteen ounces of plain celery juice, every morning, on an empty stomach, with nothing added. No mixing with fruit. No exceptions. The ritual purity matters, followers are told, because any deviation dilutes the healing power.

Within a few years of William's book, the hashtag #celeryjuice accumulated millions of posts on Instagram. In the spring of 2019, grocery stores in several American cities briefly ran out of celery; prices roughly doubled in some areas. By 2026, the movement had survived for nearly a decade, sustained by a daily drumbeat of testimonials and the endorsement of celebrities who drink it on camera.

So the central claim under review is this: **that drinking plain celery juice cures chronic illness — autoimmune diseases, chronic infections, inflammatory conditions, and undiagnosed "mystery" ailments.** Let us check it.

## What Celery Actually Contains

Before examining the cure claim, it is worth establishing the honest, unglamorous nutritional facts about celery. Nobody in nutrition science disputes that celery is a perfectly healthy vegetable. It is composed of roughly 95 percent water, making it naturally hydrating. It is low in calories — a medium stalk contains only about six calories — and low in fat, cholesterol, and carbohydrates.

Celery also contains real nutrients. It supplies vitamin K, which is important for blood clotting and bone health, along with potassium, folate, calcium, magnesium, and vitamins A and C in modest amounts. It contains flavonoids and phenolic acids, including apigenin and luteolin, which are antioxidants — plant compounds that have shown anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory settings.

This is the grain of truth at the heart of the claim: celery is a nutritious vegetable, and celery juice retains many of those nutrients. For someone whose previous breakfast was a sugary coffee drink or a soda, swapping to celery juice is plausibly an improvement.

But here is the important context that the cure claims skip. None of these nutrients are unique to celery. Broccoli, spinach, kale, and cauliflower offer the same nutrients — often in greater quantities. As registered dietitian Kristen Kirkpatrick has noted, "Your money would be better spent if you buy the whole celery stalk and incorporate it into a whole-food diet full of fruits, vegetables, healthy fats, whole grain, and lean proteins." The nutritional argument for celery juice is not an argument for celery juice specifically; it is an argument for eating vegetables generally.

## The Evidence: Zero Human Trials for the Cure Claims

The decisive question for any cure claim is: what do rigorous human studies show? In the case of celery juice curing chronic illness, the answer is stark. No rigorous human clinical trial has been published demonstrating that drinking celery juice cures autoimmune disease, resolves chronic infections, or treats any of the "mystery illnesses" William's followers describe.

There is not even the kind of evidence that would normally serve as a stepping stone toward such trials. The compound research most often invoked by believers — studies on apigenin and other flavonoids — has largely been conducted in petri dishes and in animals, using concentrated extracts rather than ordinary juice. Cell-culture and animal studies are useful for generating hypotheses, but they are not evidence that drinking juice treats human disease. Human digestion, absorption, and metabolism change everything: apigenin's oral bioavailability in humans is low, meaning much of it never reaches the body's tissues in meaningful amounts. What happens to a concentrated extract in a laboratory dish does not predict what happens to a glass of juice in a human body.

The expert response to the cure claims has been consistent for years. Debbie Petitpain, a registered dietitian and spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, has said of the miracle-juice promises: "Unfortunately, there is no magic bullet or, in this case, 'miracle food' that can deliver on such extraordinary claims." She has further emphasized that research continually shows the best nutrition comes from consuming a variety of foods and food groups — not from a single "superfood."

On the detox claims specifically — a load-bearing pillar of the celery juice doctrine — the physiology simply does not cooperate. Registered dietitian Lindsay Krasna has stated: "There is no evidence that drinking celery juice, or any juice, has any detoxing or cleansing benefits. Our kidneys and liver are the vital organs responsible for purifying our blood and ridding our bodies of harmful toxins. They do that whether we're consuming celery juice or eating a burger." The liver does not need juice to detoxify the body; it does that continuously, on its own, for free.

The "cluster salts" claim — the idea that celery contains a previously undiscovered subgroup of sodium with pathogen-destroying powers — is, as several commentators have noted, the epitome of pseudoscience. It has no basis in chemistry, and no independent researcher has ever identified such substances. The theory was not derived from laboratory analysis; by William's own account, it came to him through "spiritual clairvoyance" — a spirit speaking in a voice only he can hear.

## Why the Fiber Problem Matters

There is an irony at the center of the celery juice doctrine that its followers rarely mention: the recommended preparation method actively removes one of celery's most valuable components. Juicing strips out most of the vegetable's insoluble fiber — the part that slows nutrient absorption, regulates blood sugar, feeds beneficial gut bacteria, and supports bowel regularity.

Fiber is not a minor bonus; it is central to digestive and metabolic health. For people who drink celery juice specifically for "gut healing" — one of the movement's core promises — the juicing process discards the very component most associated with gut benefits. Eating whole celery stalks delivers identical vitamins and minerals plus the fiber. As multiple dietitians have pointed out, for digestive health the whole vegetable is the better choice, not the juice.

This is a common pattern in wellness trends: a real health mechanism (fiber supports digestion) is cited to promote a product that has been stripped of the thing that provides the mechanism (the fiber), while the claim migrates onto the juice on faith alone.

## Why It Feels Like It Works

If celery juice does not cure chronic illness, why are the testimonials so passionate? Why do millions of people swear it changed their lives? This is the most important part of the story, because the testimonials are sincere — and sincerely wrong about the cause.

First, the ritual usually replaces something worse. A person who begins drinking sixteen ounces of celery juice each morning on an empty stomach has changed their morning routine. Often the juice replaces a sugary drink. Sometimes the commitment to a "healing protocol" brings a cascade of other changes: more vegetables at meals, less alcohol, better sleep, more attention to health overall. When someone improves after adopting five healthier habits at once, the celery juice gets the credit — but the credit is not apportioned by evidence.

Second, many chronic and inflammatory conditions fluctuate naturally. Eczema flares and fades. Irritable bowel symptoms wax and wane. Fatigue has good weeks and bad weeks. People typically adopt a new remedy at the lowest point — when they are desperate — which is statistically the moment most likely to be followed by improvement regardless of what they do. This is the well-documented phenomenon of regression to the mean, and it generates sincere, vivid, entirely mistaken testimonials.

Third, the placebo effect is real and powerful, especially for subjective symptoms like pain, fatigue, and bloating. Believing you are drinking a powerful healing tonic can produce genuine, measurable relief — relief that comes from the mind's influence on the body, not from the celery.

Fourth, the celery juice community is an exceptionally supportive social environment. People share daily check-ins, swap success stories, and receive encouragement from strangers. Social support improves perceived well-being. It is not the juice; it is the belonging.

And fifth, there is survivorship bias. The movement's forums are filled with success stories because people whose symptoms did not improve tend to leave quietly. The failures are invisible; the successes are spotlighted. The result is a deeply skewed sample that looks like evidence but is not.

None of this means people are lying. It means human beings are pattern-seeking creatures in bodies with natural healing rhythms, and testimonials — however emotional — are not clinical evidence.

## The Cost of False Hope

A false cure claim is not a victimless error, even when the product is as benign as vegetable juice. The most serious concern raised by health professionals is what economists would call the opportunity cost: time, money, and attention spent on a ritual with no proven benefit are diverted from treatments that actually work.

For people with chronic illnesses — autoimmune diseases, thyroid disorders, Lyme disease, chronic infections — effective medical care exists for many conditions, or at minimum, honest monitoring and symptom management by qualified clinicians. The celery juice doctrine does not merely offer an unproven supplement alongside real care; its central narrative is that conventional medicine is failing "mystery illness" patients and that the Medical Medium's spirit-guided protocol succeeds where doctors fail. That narrative can steer genuinely ill people away from the doctors they need.

Nutrition writers who have studied the movement put it bluntly: the social media campaign "exploits chronic illness sufferers by giving them false hope." These are often people who are exhausted, in pain, and have been told by multiple specialists that their condition is difficult to treat. They are the most vulnerable audience for a cure claim, and the most harmed when it fails.

There is also a straightforward financial cost. Store-bought celery juice, cold-pressed subscription services, and the sheer volume of celery required for sixteen ounces a day add up. Some health food stores sell pre-made bottles for around ten dollars each. A daily ritual at that price costs thousands of dollars a year — spent on a drink whose benefits could be matched, exceeded, and made more complete by simply eating the whole vegetable, which also provides the fiber the juice removes.

## A Pattern, Not a Discovery

It helps to see the celery juice phenomenon in the lineage it belongs to. Every few years, a single food is elevated to miracle status: kale, quinoa, coconut oil, apple cider vinegar, bone broth, turmeric. Each arrives with celebrity endorsements, a library of testimonials, and claims that collapse the complexity of human health into a single daily ritual. Each fades — or persists as a folk remedy — when the evidence never materializes.

The celery juice version has a distinguishing feature, though: its claims originated not from a misreading of research but from claimed supernatural revelation. William does not point to studies; he points to a spirit. That is not a detail that can be set aside, because it determines the entire epistemology of the movement. When a claim is founded on revelation rather than evidence, no study can refute it in the eyes of believers — every negative result becomes proof that science is not ready for the truth. This is the classic structure of unfalsifiable health claims, and it is why the movement has persisted for nearly a decade without producing a single published clinical trial supporting its central assertions.

Consider what the movement has had nearly ten years to produce. It has the funding, the audience, and the motivation to test its flagship claim: that daily celery juice cures chronic illness. A randomized controlled trial would not be difficult to design. The fact that no such trial has been published — by supporters, by universities, by anyone — is itself informative. Proponents of the claim bear the burden of proof, and they have not met it.

## What the Experts Recommend Instead

None of the dietitians and health writers who have evaluated this trend advise against drinking celery juice. Their objection is narrower and more precise: do not mistake it for medicine.

The consistent professional guidance, through early 2026, runs as follows. Celery juice is a hydrating, low-calorie vegetable drink that supplies potassium, vitamin K, and some antioxidants, and it can be a reasonable part of a balanced diet — particularly as a replacement for sugary beverages. But it does not detoxify the liver or kidneys beyond what those organs do on their own; it does not cure autoimmune disease, chronic infections, or inflammatory conditions; and the grand claims attached to it are supported by testimonials, not trials. If you enjoy it, drink it. If you are managing a chronic illness, see a qualified clinician — and do not let a juice protocol delay, dilute, or replace evidence-based care.

The broader lesson is one that nutrition science returns to again and again: the best-supported dietary pattern is variety. A whole-food diet rich in many different fruits, vegetables, whole grains, healthy fats, and lean proteins delivers more of everything celery offers, plus fiber, plus nutrients celery lacks. No single food, however photogenic in a morning glass, can carry the load of an entire diet.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that drinking celery juice cures chronic illness is false. It rests on no published human clinical evidence. Its foundational theory — "cluster salts" that destroy pathogens — is an untested assertion attributed to spiritual revelation by a man with no medical or scientific credentials, not a finding of chemistry or biology. The real nutrients in celery are modest, non-unique to celery, and available in greater quantity — with the added benefit of fiber — from eating whole celery and a varied diet. The movement's sincere testimonials are explained by placebo effects, natural symptom fluctuation, concurrent lifestyle changes, social support, and survivorship bias — not by the juice.

This verdict should not be read as an attack on celery. Drink it if you like it. Juice it if you prefer it. But keep it in its proper place: the kitchen, not the pharmacy. The most dangerous thing about the celery juice cure claim was never the juice. It was the story — the promise, sold to exhausted, chronically ill people, that a glass of green liquid could do what their doctors could not. Nearly a decade in, the evidence has not arrived, the trials have not been published, and the thousands of "miracles" remain, as they were from the beginning, anecdotes.

Real healing is rarely a ritual. It is usually a routine: varied food, movement, sleep, and care from people trained to provide it. Celery can be part of that. A cure, it is not.
