It is one of the most quoted "amazing facts" in the English language, right up there with the claim that you eat eight spiders a year in your sleep. It appears in pub quizzes, on trivia cards, in forwarded messages, and in the small talk of people who love a fact that sounds too strange to be true: a duck's quack doesn't echo, and nobody knows why.

The second half of that sentence is the giveaway. Science is not in the habit of shrugging at a sound. Sound is a pressure wave travelling through air. When it hits a hard surface, part of it bounces back. That is what an echo is, and it does not make exceptions for ducks. If a quack genuinely produced no echo, it would break the laws of physics — and physicists, being the sort of people who notice that kind of thing, would have been all over it decades ago.

Instead, the opposite happened. In 2003, researchers at the University of Salford in England decided to settle the matter properly, and they did it the only way that counts: they borrowed a duck, put her in a laboratory, and listened. The duck was called Daisy. The result was exactly what physics predicted. A duck's quack echoes. The reason so many people believe it doesn't is not that the echo is absent, but that it is devilishly hard to hear — and the story of why that is turns out to be more interesting than the myth itself.

## Where the claim comes from

Nobody knows precisely where the quack claim began. It was already circulating by the 1990s, passed around as one of those facts that is fun precisely because it seems to defy common sense. It may be older still; the trail goes cold because the claim travelled by word of mouth, pub conversation, and later by email forwards and social media posts, none of which leave tidy records.

What is clear is why it stuck. The claim has a perfect shape for a viral fact. It is short, it is surprising, and it ends with a mystery — "and nobody knows why" — that dares the listener to explain it. A fact that came with its own explanation would be less shareable. A fact that ends in a shrug invites repetition.

It also gained a second life in popular culture. A British comedy panel show took the claim as its title, which kept the phrase in circulation for years. By the turn of the century the myth was so widespread that it reached the ears of Professor Trevor Cox, an acoustics researcher at the University of Salford's Acoustics Research Centre. Cox had spent his career on serious problems: designing rooms for music, improving public announcements at railway stations for people who wear hearing aids, and studying how sound behaves in built spaces. The BBC contacted him to fact-check the quack claim, and he realised that nobody had ever actually tested it under controlled conditions. So he decided to test it himself.

## How an echo actually works

Before Daisy enters the story, it helps to know what an echo is and what it needs. An echo is simply a sound wave that has travelled outward, struck a surface, and returned to the listener. Three conditions decide whether you will hear one.

First, there has to be something to reflect the sound. Open water, flat fields, and empty skies do not reflect much; cliffs, canyon walls, large buildings, and the hard walls of a tunnel do. Second, the reflecting surface has to be far enough away that the returning sound arrives as a separate event rather than blending into the original. The human ear and brain need a delay of roughly a tenth of a second to tell an echo apart from the sound that made it. Since sound travels through air at about 343 metres per second, that means the surface generally needs to be at least 17 metres away. Third, the returning sound has to still be loud enough to hear. Every reflection loses energy, so a quiet original sound produces an even quieter echo.

A quack, it turns out, is a poor candidate on all three counts in the places where people usually hear ducks. Ducks live on ponds, lakes, rivers, and open parkland — landscapes with very few large, hard, nearby reflecting surfaces. A quack is also relatively quiet compared with, say, a shout or a gunshot. And, crucially, a quack does not stop cleanly. It fades. That fading tail is the heart of the whole mystery, and it is what the Salford experiment was designed to expose.

## The duck named Daisy

Cox borrowed a duck called Daisy and recorded her quacks in two very different rooms at Salford. The first was an anechoic chamber: a room whose walls, floor, and ceiling are covered in sound-absorbing wedges, so that reflections are deadened almost completely. In that room, the researchers could hear what a quack sounds like with no echo at all — the pure, original sound, stripped of its surroundings.

The second room was a reverberation chamber: a large room with hard, exposed surfaces and cathedral-like acoustics, where sound bounces around for a long time. When Daisy quacked there, the result was unmistakable. As Cox later told The Guardian, the sound produced was "rather sinister" — but it did echo. The experiment had answered the question in the most direct way possible: put a duck in a room built to produce echoes, and the quack echoes.

But Cox went further, because a laboratory result alone does not explain why the myth feels so convincing outdoors. The team also used a computer to simulate Daisy's recorded quack in other environments: a concert hall, and the open air in front of a cliff face. The simulations showed the same pattern. The echo was there, but it was often too quiet to notice, and it arrived while the original quack was still fading away, so the two sounds merged into one long, drawn-out noise.

Cox summed up the finding in a sentence that has been quoted ever since: "A quack is a fading sound. It has a gradual decay, so it's hard to tell the difference between the actual quack and the echo." That is the whole trick. Most sounds that echo clearly — a handclap, a shout, a bark — start and stop abruptly, leaving a gap of silence into which the echo can arrive and be recognised. A quack trails off gradually, like a note held on a wind instrument. The echo returns on top of that fading tail, and the ear hears a single, long quack instead of a quack followed by its echo.

As Cox put it in another summary of the work: "What all this shows is that the duck's quack fades away; it sounds like it quacks for a long time. Because the duck's quack is rather quiet anyway and the echo comes on the back of a fading sound field, it is as if the echo is being masked. You just don't hear the echo very well and that's probably how the myth arose."

## It had already been debunked — twice

The Salford experiment was the most rigorous test, but it was not the first debunking. The earliest recorded one dates to 1998, when Cecil Adams, author of the long-running Straight Dope newspaper column, took a duck to a place where echoes are easy to produce and coaxed it into quacking. After some reluctance on the duck's part, it finally obliged — and the quack echoed just as well as any other sound. Adams' verdict was characteristically blunt: the myth was nonsense.

Then, later in 2003, the television show MythBusters took up the claim and tested it in its own, less formal way, with the same result. A duck's quack echoes. Three independent tests, in three different styles — a newspaper columnist in the field, a university acoustics laboratory, and a television show — all reached the same conclusion. In the world of fact-checking, that kind of convergence is about as settled as a claim gets.

Cox himself was candid about the limits of his own work. In his book The Sound Book, he admitted that some elements of the research were crude, including field experiments that amounted to birdwatching near promising echo spots. In none of those outdoor locations, he wrote, could he hear a clear, audible quack separate from the original call. His conclusion was a careful reformulation of the myth: the phrase should really say that a duck's quack might echo, but that it is nearly impossible to hear unless the bird quacks while flying under a bridge, where hard surfaces are close overhead and the echo returns quickly and loudly.

That distinction matters. The myth, as usually stated, claims that a quack cannot echo — a statement about physics. The truth is subtler: a quack can echo, but in everyday conditions you will rarely hear it do so. The myth survives because it confuses "I have never heard it" with "it cannot happen."

## Why the myth refuses to die

If the claim has been debunked three times over, why is it still doing the rounds more than twenty years after the Salford experiment? The answer is a small catalogue of how misinformation works.

First, the myth is harmless, which makes it low-priority for correction. Nobody loses money or health by believing that a duck's quack has no echo, so the debunking never gets the urgency that, say, a medical myth would. Harmless myths survive because nobody is motivated enough to kill them.

Second, the myth is self-sealing. If you go to a pond and listen, you will almost certainly fail to hear an echo — for exactly the acoustic reasons Cox identified. Your own experience then appears to confirm the claim. The debunking requires either a laboratory or an understanding of masking and decay, neither of which is available at the pondside.

Third, ducks usually come in groups. In a flock, one duck's quack overlaps with another's, and any echo is buried under a chorus of live quacks. Even if an echo were loud enough to hear, it would be indistinguishable from a second duck calling a moment later.

Fourth, the myth has the structure of a good story. "Scientists cannot explain it" is a more exciting sentence than "scientists explained it in 2003." The original claim, with its built-in mystery, is simply more fun to repeat than the correction. Corrections, as a rule, are less shareable than the claims they correct — a pattern that repeats across almost every animal myth in circulation, from the idea that <a href="/blog/do-cats-always-land-on-their-feet-fact-check/">cats always land on their feet</a> to the tale that <a href="/blog/do-lemmings-commit-mass-suicide-fact-check/">lemmings commit mass suicide</a> and the image of <a href="/blog/ostriches-bury-heads-sand-myth-fact-check/">ostriches burying their heads in the sand</a>. Each contains a grain of real observation, wrapped in an exaggeration that is more memorable than the truth.

## The wolf-howl cousin

The quack myth even spawned a sequel. In 2003, the same year as the Salford experiment, a television programme suggested that a wolf's howl does not echo either. It doesn't take an acoustician to see the problem: the same physics applies. All sounds echo, given a suitable reflecting surface at a suitable distance. The howl claim is false for the same reason the quack claim is false, and it illustrates a useful rule of thumb for viral animal facts. Whenever a claim says that one particular animal's sound is exempt from the laws of physics, it is almost certainly wrong. Physics does not grant exemptions to charismatic species.

There is one genuine nuance worth noting. In some environments, sound waves can cancel each other out through destructive interference, where a reflected wave meets the original wave out of phase and the two partially cancel. That is a real phenomenon, and it is one reason why echoes can be hard to hear in complicated spaces. But it is not a property of ducks, and it does not happen reliably, in varied environments, for one species' call. The odds of any animal producing that effect consistently are, for practical purposes, zero.

## Try it yourself — with the right setup

If you want to hear a quack echo for yourself, the Salford work suggests how. You need a duck, a hard reflecting surface at least 17 metres away, and quiet surroundings — and ideally, a recording you can play back and compare with a recording made in a non-echoey space. The reason the laboratory mattered is that the ear alone, in the field, is a poor instrument for this particular job. The echo hides inside the fading tail of the original sound, and without a clean reference recording, your brain will file the whole thing under "one long quack."

Under a bridge is the classic spot, because the hard surfaces are close and the echo returns fast and loud. A large empty hall or a canyon wall can work too, provided the duck cooperates — and ducks, as Cecil Adams discovered, are not always keen to quack on cue. That reluctance may itself have helped the myth along: a duck that won't perform in an echoey place is a duck whose echo nobody hears.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: False.** A duck's quack does echo, like every other sound. The claim that it doesn't, and that nobody knows why, fails on both counts: it does echo, and scientists know exactly why people think it doesn't.

The evidence is unusually complete for a piece of folklore. Cecil Adams demonstrated the echo in the field in 1998. Professor Trevor Cox and his team at the University of Salford proved it under laboratory conditions in 2003, recording Daisy the duck in an anechoic chamber and a reverberation chamber and simulating her quack in a concert hall and in front of a cliff. MythBusters confirmed it again the same year. Three independent tests, one answer.

The reason the myth feels true is real, and it is worth understanding, because it explains a whole family of false beliefs. A quack is quiet, it fades gradually rather than stopping abruptly, and ducks usually live where there is nothing for sound to bounce off. The echo arrives quietly, on top of the fading original, and the ear merges the two into a single sound. Add in flocks, where one duck's call masks another's echo, and the absence of an audible echo at the pond becomes almost guaranteed — without the echo itself being absent at all.

So the next time the claim comes up at a quiz night or in a forwarded message, you can answer with confidence. A duck's quack echoes. The only thing that doesn't echo is the myth's original mystery — because that mystery was solved, by a duck named Daisy, more than twenty years ago.
