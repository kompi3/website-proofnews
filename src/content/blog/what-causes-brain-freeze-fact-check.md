You know the moment. The first spoonful of ice cream, the first long pull on a slushie, the first gulp of an iced drink on a hot afternoon — and then, out of nowhere, a sharp stab of pain blooms across your forehead, as if someone pressed an ice cube directly against the inside of your skull. It is so sudden and so intense that for a second or two you genuinely wonder whether something has gone wrong inside your head. Then, almost as quickly as it arrived, it is gone, leaving nothing behind but the memory of the pain and a newfound respect for the speed at which you eat frozen things.

The experience is nearly universal, and so is the question it inspires. Millions of people have asked it, usually while clutching their foreheads: what causes brain freeze — and is my brain actually freezing? The name itself suggests something dramatic and a little alarming, as though a mouthful of ice cream could drop the temperature of the most delicate organ in your body until it ices over. It is a vivid image. It is also, as a description of what is happening inside your skull, entirely wrong.

Nothing in your head freezes when you eat ice cream too fast. Your brain never drops to anything approaching freezing temperature, no tissue is damaged, and no ice forms anywhere in the process. What you are feeling is real — the pain is genuine, and it has a precise, measurable cause — but the cause is not cold reaching your brain. It is a rapid, protective reflex in the blood vessels of the roof of your mouth, a reflex so fast and so automatic that scientists have used it as a laboratory model for studying migraines. The story of how researchers figured that out, in an experiment involving thirteen volunteers, a straw, and a glass of ice water, is one of the neatest pieces of headache science ever done.

## The Claim: Your Brain Is Freezing

The claim embedded in the name "brain freeze" is simple enough to state plainly: eating something very cold very quickly chills your brain, and the pain you feel is your brain reacting to being frozen. It is the kind of folk explanation that feels right because it maps neatly onto the sensation. Cold goes in, pain blooms in the head, and the obvious conclusion is that the cold reached the head and hurt it. Children reason this way, adults repeat it as a joke, and the phrase itself has become so common that almost nobody stops to ask whether it is literally true.

It is not. The literal version of the claim fails on the most basic physical grounds. Your brain sits inside a thick, well-insulated skull, bathed in warm blood that arrives at a steady 37 degrees Celsius, and it is one of the most carefully temperature-regulated structures in your body. A spoonful of ice cream touches the roof of your mouth for a second or two. It cannot meaningfully cool an organ that is several centimetres away, wrapped in bone, and constantly reheated by a rich blood supply. If ice cream could freeze your brain, so could a cold drink, a breath of winter air, or a splash of cold water on your face — and none of those things does.

What makes the claim worth fact-checking rather than dismissing with a shrug is that the pain is not imaginary, and the mechanism behind it turns out to be genuinely interesting. The headache has a formal medical name — cold-stimulus headache, listed in the International Classification of Headache Disorders as code 4.5.2 — and a formal definition that already hints at where the action really happens: "A short-lasting frontal or temporal pain, which may be intense, induced in susceptible people by passage of cold material (solid, liquid, or gaseous) over the palate and/or posterior pharyngeal wall." Note what the definition says and what it does not say. The pain is in the forehead or temples. The trigger is cold material passing over the palate — the roof of your mouth — or the back of the throat. The brain itself is not mentioned, because the brain itself is not where the event takes place.

## The Palate Is the Battlefield, Not the Brain

To understand what causes brain freeze, start with the anatomy of the place where it begins. The roof of your mouth is one of the most richly supplied with blood vessels of any surface in your body. That is why doctors take your temperature under your tongue, and why a mouthful of hot soup can feel scalding in an instant. The palate is thin, warm, and densely vascular, and just above it sit nerves and blood vessels that lead directly toward the brain — including, crucially, the junction where the internal carotid artery, which carries blood up into the brain, meets the anterior cerebral artery, where brain tissue begins.

When you eat ice cream slowly, this system copes easily. The cold is absorbed gradually, the tissues of the mouth warm it, and nothing dramatic happens. When you eat it fast — a big spoonful, a deep gulp of a slushie — the temperature at the back of the throat changes far too quickly for the local tissues to absorb. The body, which is obsessive about keeping its core temperature stable, treats that sudden chill as a threat. And the threat it imagines is not a frozen tongue. It is the possibility that cold blood might reach the brain.

That is the key to the whole phenomenon. The brain is the most temperature-sensitive organ you own, and it is also the most protected. When the sensors in the palate detect a sudden plunge in temperature, the body reacts as though the brain itself were in danger of cooling down. Blood vessels in the area first constrict, clamping down to limit the flow of cold blood toward the head, and then rapidly dilate again, flooding the area with warm blood to reheat the tissue. It is that violent swing — constriction followed by sudden dilation, a rush of blood through vessels that have just been squeezed shut — that the nervous system reads as pain.

Neuroscientist Dwayne Godwin of Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center, who has spent years explaining the phenomenon to the public, puts it plainly: "Brain freeze is really a type of headache that is rapid in onset, but rapidly resolved as well." The brain, he notes, cannot actually feel pain at all, despite its billions of neurons. The pain is sensed by receptors in the meninges, the protective outer covering of the brain, where the two arteries meet. When the cold hits, it causes a dilation and contraction of those arteries, and that is the sensation the brain interprets as pain. The ache is in your forehead because the nerves that carry the alarm happen to route there, not because anything in your forehead is cold.

## Why the Pain Lands in Your Forehead

The misdirection of the pain is one of the strangest and most elegant parts of the story. It has a name in medicine: referred pain. The trigeminal nerve, the largest nerve in your head, carries sensory information from your face, your mouth, and the front of your head. When the cold triggers pain receptors in the palate and the back of the throat, the signal travels up the trigeminal nerve, and the brain — which is very good at many things but not at pinpointing the origin of signals from this particular nerve — misreads the location. It concludes that the pain is coming from the forehead or the temples, the territory the same nerve serves on the outside of the head.

Harvard Health Publishing describes the sequence in exactly these terms: a cold substance touching the roof of the mouth or the back of the throat causes small blood vessels in those areas to first tighten and then rapidly open back up, and pain receptors near the vessels send a message along tiny nerve fibres to the trigeminal nerve, which carries it to the brain. The brain then reads the cold-stimulus sensations as coming from the head rather than the mouth. The pain you feel in your forehead is, in a very literal sense, your mouth's pain arriving at the wrong address.

This also explains why brain freeze feels so oddly specific. It is not a dull, spreading ache like a tension headache. It is sharp, focused, and usually centred on the forehead or behind the eyes — the exact territory of the trigeminal nerve's ophthalmic branch. And it explains why the pain vanishes so fast. The moment the palate warms back up, the vessels settle, the signal stops, and there is nothing left to hurt. There is no inflammation, no injury, no lingering damage. The alarm was false, and the system that raised it switches itself off within seconds.

## The Experiment That Watched It Happen

For years, the explanation above was a theory — a good one, supported by anatomy and by the testimony of everyone who has ever eaten ice cream too fast, but still a theory. Then, in 2012, a team of researchers found a way to watch it happen in real time, and the results were more dramatic than the theory predicted.

The study was led by Jorge Serrador, a cardiovascular researcher at Harvard Medical School and the War Related Illness and Injury Study Center of the Veterans Affairs New Jersey Health Care System, working with colleagues from the Department of Veterans Affairs and the National University of Ireland Galway. The team recruited thirteen healthy adult volunteers and asked them to do something deliberately uncomfortable: sip ice-cold water through a straw pressed against the roof of the mouth — the ideal position for triggering a brain freeze — while the researchers monitored blood flow in several brain arteries using transcranial Doppler ultrasound. The volunteers raised one hand the moment they felt the pain begin, and raised it again when the pain faded.

What the monitors showed was striking. At the precise moment the volunteers signalled pain, one particular artery — the anterior cerebral artery, which runs along the midline of the brain just behind the forehead — dilated rapidly and flooded the brain with blood. The surge was sudden and large. Then, just as the volunteers signalled that the pain was receding, the same vessel constricted, reducing blood flow back toward normal. The researchers could even trigger the constriction on demand by giving the volunteers warm water to drink. The pain and the blood flow rose and fell together, locked in step, as if the artery itself were the switch.

Serrador's interpretation, presented at the Experimental Biology 2012 meeting in San Diego in April 2012 and published in the FASEB Journal, was that the surge is a protective reflex. The brain is metabolically expensive and fiercely defended, and a blast of cold nearby may trigger an emergency delivery of warm blood to keep it at operating temperature. The problem is that the skull is a closed box. A sudden influx of blood raises pressure inside that box and stretches the walls of the vessels — and stretched vessel walls are exactly what pain fibres notice. When the flow normalises, the alarm stops. The researchers themselves framed the conclusion cautiously: this was a small experimental study, and the full mechanism of cold-stimulus headache is still not settled science. But the core observation — a surge of blood through the anterior cerebral artery, timed precisely to the pain — has held up, and it remains the clearest window anyone has opened into the phenomenon.

There is a second reason the study mattered, and it has nothing to do with ice cream. Because brain freeze is so easy to trigger in a laboratory — a straw, a glass of ice water, a willing volunteer — and because it produces no lasting harm, Serrador's team proposed using it as a stand-in for studying other headaches that are far harder to provoke on demand, especially migraine. As Godwin later put it: "We can't easily give people migraines or a cluster headache, but we can easily induce brain freeze without any long-term problems. We can learn something about headache mechanisms and extend that to our understanding to develop better treatments for patients." The ice-cream headache, in other words, became a research tool — a headache scientists can switch on and off like a light.


## How Fast You Eat Changes Everything

If the mechanism is a reflex triggered by a sudden temperature change, then one prediction follows immediately: speed should matter more than quantity. A small amount of ice cream eaten very fast should be more dangerous than a large amount eaten slowly, because what trips the alarm is not how much cold arrives but how quickly the temperature drops. That prediction has been tested, and it held up beautifully.

The test came from the Ice Cream Evoked Headaches study — the ICE-H study — published in the British Medical Journal in 2002 by Maya Kaczorowski and colleagues. The researchers gave schoolchildren ice cream under two conditions: eaten quickly, in under five seconds, or eaten slowly, taking more than thirty seconds. The fast eaters developed headaches 27 percent of the time. The slow eaters developed them only 12 percent of the time. Same ice cream, same children, same day — and eating fast more than doubled the odds of pain. The rate of intake, not the amount, was the deciding factor.

The finding matches everyday experience so precisely that it hardly needs a study to confirm it. Nobody gets a brain freeze from a single careful spoonful savoured over a minute. Everyone gets one from the enthusiastic first bite of a cone, or the deep, competitive gulp of a slushie. The palate needs time to absorb the cold gradually; deny it that time, and the alarm goes off. Children, who have not yet learned to slow down when eating something fun like an ice pop, are more likely to trigger it — the Cleveland Clinic notes this explicitly — which is why the loudest complaints about brain freeze tend to come from the youngest eaters at the table.

It is also why the folk remedies that actually work all do the same thing: they warm the palate back up. Pressing your tongue firmly against the roof of your mouth transfers body heat directly to the chilled tissue. Drinking something warm or at room temperature does the same thing from the inside. Cupping your hands over your mouth and nose and breathing warm air into them works on the same principle. In Serrador's laboratory, warm water ended the pain by constricting the dilated artery. In real life, the same physics applies: warm the palate, settle the vessels, stop the signal. There is no mystery cure and no special technique required. The only reliable prevention is the one Harvard Health recommends — eat very cold foods slowly — and the only reliable treatment is patience plus warmth.

## Who Gets Brain Freeze — and Why Migraine Sufferers Get It More

Not everyone gets brain freeze, and among those who do, some people get it far more easily than others. Harvard Health reports that cold-stimulus headaches occur in 30 to 40 percent of people who do not usually suffer from headaches at all — a remarkably high figure for a phenomenon most people treat as a joke. But the rate climbs sharply in one particular group: people who live with migraine.

The Cleveland Clinic states plainly that research has shown sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia — the scientific name for brain freeze — is more common in people who get migraine headaches. An older study, published in the journal Headache in 1992 by Nigel Bird, Anne MacGregor, and Marcia Wilkinson, measured the gap directly: after a palatal application or a swallow of ice cream, 17 percent of migraine patients developed a headache, compared with 46 percent of students in the comparison group — a result that has been read in both directions over the years, but which established early on that the two conditions travel together. More recent work points the same way, and the clinical advice is consistent: if you get migraines, you are more likely to get brain freeze, and you should sip carefully.

There are good reasons for the overlap. People with migraine tend to have blood vessels that react more intensely and more quickly to sudden stimuli, and a trigeminal nerve that is more sensitive than average — the same nerve, and the same vascular reactivity, that drive the brain-freeze reflex. Researchers have also noted that the vascular mechanism and the nerve implicated in brain freeze are involved in the aura and throbbing phases of migraine itself. That is precisely why scientists value brain freeze as a research model: it is a small, harmless, switchable version of some of the same machinery that produces one of the most disabling headaches in medicine.

Children are the other group that stands out. A 2023 systematic review confirmed that children and adolescents develop cold-stimulus headaches more often than adults, with a notable family history and significant overlap with migraine. Part of the reason is behavioural — children eat ice cream faster — but part of it may be physiological, with younger nervous systems reacting more vigorously to the sudden chill. Either way, the pattern is consistent: the people whose vessels and nerves are most reactive are the people who feel the ice cream most sharply.

## Your Brain Cannot Feel Pain — So What Are You Feeling?

There is a fact about the brain that surprises almost everyone who hears it for the first time: the brain itself has no pain receptors. A surgeon can touch, prod, or even cut brain tissue in a conscious patient — and brain surgery is sometimes performed with the patient awake — without the patient feeling any pain from the organ itself. The brain is a mass of nerve cells with no sensors for reporting damage to itself. It is, in the most literal sense, numb to its own injuries.

So when a brain freeze hurts, the pain is not coming from the brain. It is coming from the structures around it. The meninges, the layered membranes that wrap the brain, are richly supplied with pain receptors, as are the walls of the blood vessels that run through them. When the anterior cerebral artery dilates suddenly and floods the brain with blood, the vessel walls stretch, the pressure inside the closed box of the skull rises briefly, and the pain fibres in the meninges fire. The brain receives that alarm and presents it to you as a headache in the forehead — the referred pain described earlier — but the organ generating the signal is the plumbing, not the computer.

This is also why the name "brain freeze" is doubly misleading. Not only does nothing freeze, but the brain is not even the part that hurts. A more accurate name would be something like "palate-chill vascular headache," which is roughly what the medical term cold-stimulus headache already says, and roughly why nobody uses it at the ice-cream stand. The folk name survives because it is vivid and funny. The science survives because it is precise. Both can be true at once, as long as nobody mistakes the vivid name for the precise description.

It is worth pausing on how well defended the brain actually is, because it puts the whole phenomenon in perspective. Your brain consumes about a fifth of your body's energy despite being about two percent of its weight. It is kept at a fiercely stable temperature by a constant flow of warm blood, wrapped in bone, cushioned in fluid, and monitored by sensors throughout the body. A spoonful of ice cream is not a threat to that system. It is a false alarm tripped by a sensor that sits too close to the cold — like a smoke detector that goes off because someone lit a candle beneath it. The detector is working exactly as designed. It is simply responding to a signal that looks, for a fraction of a second, like an emergency.

## How to Stop a Brain Freeze in Seconds

Because the mechanism is simple, the fixes are simple too — and every major medical reference agrees on them. The Cleveland Clinic's advice is the clearest version, and it amounts to a single principle: get the temperature of your mouth and throat back to normal as quickly as you can.

First, stop eating or drinking the cold item, or get out of the cold if freezing air is the trigger. Continuing to pour cold onto an already chilled palate only extends the alarm. Second, drink something warm or at room temperature — not cold, and not hot enough to scald. Warm liquid reheats the palate from the inside and, as Serrador's experiment showed, helps the dilated artery constrict back to its normal size. Third, press your tongue or your thumb firmly against the roof of your mouth to transfer body heat directly to the chilled tissue. This is the trick most people discover by accident as children, and it works for exactly the reason the physiology predicts: it warms the sensor that raised the alarm.

There are variations on the same theme. Cupping both hands over your mouth and nose and breathing warm air into them reheats the palate and the back of the throat together. Pressing a warm hand against your forehead does nothing for the palate, but some people find the counter-sensation distracting — which is harmless, even if the relief is psychological rather than physiological. What does not work is any of the stranger folk remedies: holding your breath, pressing on the bridge of your nose, or drinking something even colder to "fight fire with fire." The goal is warmth, applied to the roof of the mouth, and anything that delivers that warmth will do.

Prevention is even simpler than treatment, and it is the only method with real evidence behind it. Eat cold foods slowly. Let each spoonful warm slightly in your mouth before swallowing. Use a smaller straw for cold drinks, and sip rather than gulp. Move the cold food around your mouth instead of letting it sit against the palate. These are small courtesies to the most sensitive patch of skin in your head, and they are enough to keep the alarm silent in most cases. The Cleveland Clinic notes that the only way to prevent brain freeze entirely is to avoid sudden, extreme temperature changes in the mouth, throat, and head — which is another way of saying that the speed of the cold matters far more than the cold itself.

## When a Cold Headache Is Not a Brain Freeze

For all its drama, a brain freeze is one of the most harmless experiences in medicine. Harvard Health states it plainly: the effect is "not considered dangerous," the symptoms are harmless, and they are not a sign of any underlying disease. The Cleveland Clinic agrees, noting that you do not need medical attention for a brain freeze, and that it goes away on its own, without medicine or rest, usually within a few seconds to two minutes. In the careful language of the evidence, that is about as close to a clean bill of health as any symptom ever receives.

But the reassurance has limits, and they are worth stating clearly, because the same features that make a brain freeze easy to recognise are also the features that mark something else when they are absent. A genuine cold-stimulus headache follows a strict pattern: it begins within seconds of cold touching the palate or the back of the throat, it peaks quickly, it sits in the forehead or temples, and it resolves within a few minutes at most once the cold is gone. The International Classification of Headache Disorders requires resolution within ten minutes. If a headache does not follow that script, it is not a brain freeze.

See a doctor promptly if a headache begins suddenly and severely without any cold trigger, if it lasts for hours rather than minutes, if it comes with fever, a stiff neck, confusion, weakness, changes in vision, or vomiting, or if headaches of any kind are becoming more frequent, more severe, or different from your usual pattern. None of those features belongs to a brain freeze, and all of them belong to conditions that deserve proper medical attention. The same advice applies if you find yourself getting cold-stimulus headaches constantly, or if they have started appearing without an obvious trigger — the phenomenon itself is harmless, but a change in your headache pattern is information your doctor should have.

It is also worth noting one more boundary. A brain freeze is not the same as the pain of sensitive teeth, which can also strike when cold food touches the mouth but follows a different path — dentin hypersensitivity, a dental problem with a dental solution. If cold food hurts your teeth rather than your forehead, and the pain is sharp, localised to a single tooth, and repeats with every cold bite, that is a question for a dentist, not a neurologist.

## The Verdict

**False.** Your brain does not freeze when you eat ice cream too fast. No part of your brain drops to freezing temperature, no tissue is damaged, and no ice forms anywhere in the process. The claim embedded in the name "brain freeze" is literally wrong — and the real explanation, established by anatomy, by the 2012 Serrador blood-flow experiment on thirteen volunteers, and by the 2002 BMJ study showing that eating fast more than doubles the odds of pain, is more interesting than the myth it replaces.

What actually happens is a rapid protective reflex. Cold food chills the roof of your mouth too quickly, blood vessels there constrict and then dilate violently to rush warm blood back, and the trigeminal nerve misreads the signal as pain in your forehead. The brain itself cannot feel pain; the ache is detected by receptors in the meninges and the vessel walls. It peaks within seconds, it fades within a couple of minutes, and it leaves nothing behind. Eat slowly, press your tongue to the roof of your mouth, and the whole event never happens.

So keep eating ice cream. Just give your palate a moment to catch up — and the next time someone at the table clutches their forehead and announces that their brain is freezing, you can tell them the truth: their brain is fine, their palate panicked, and the pain is a false alarm from the most overprotective security system in the human body.
