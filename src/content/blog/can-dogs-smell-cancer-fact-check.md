It started with a mole, a dog, and a letter to a medical journal. In April 1989, *The Lancet* published a short letter from two doctors describing a patient whose dog would not leave a mole on her leg alone. The dog sniffed at it constantly, and even tried to bite it off. Annoyed and worried in equal measure, the woman went to a dermatologist. The mole turned out to be a malignant melanoma — a dangerous skin cancer, caught early enough to treat.

That letter did not prove that dogs can smell cancer. It proved something narrower, but more interesting: that the idea was worth testing. A pet behaving oddly around a lesion is an anecdote, and anecdotes are where most medical myths begin. But this one survived the next 35 years of scientific scrutiny in a way few anecdotes do. Researchers trained dogs, built double-blind trials, measured sensitivity and specificity, and published the results in peer-reviewed journals. The findings are real — and they are messier, more qualified, and more fascinating than the viral version of the story suggests.

## The Claim

The claim, as it circulates online, goes something like this: dogs can smell cancer. Sometimes it comes with a number — a dog's nose is 10,000 or even 100,000 times more sensitive than a human's — and sometimes it comes with a promise, that dogs could one day replace biopsies or mammograms, or that your own pet might warn you before a doctor could.

There is a genuine scientific question underneath the hype. Cancer changes the chemistry of the body. Tumours are metabolically active, and the byproducts of that activity — volatile organic compounds, or VOCs — leave the body in breath, urine, sweat, and the air above a blood sample. If those compounds form a distinct scent pattern, and if a dog's nose is sensitive enough to detect it, then a trained dog should be able to tell a cancer sample from a healthy one. That is a testable idea. And it has been tested, many times, with real patients, real samples, and real statistics.

So can dogs smell cancer? The short answer is yes — under controlled conditions, trained dogs can distinguish cancer samples from healthy ones far better than chance. The longer answer is that the accuracy varies enormously from study to study, that no dog-based cancer test is approved for clinical use, and that your pet dog sniffing you is not a diagnosis. The claim is mostly true. The version of it that goes viral is not.

## A Nose Built for the Job

Before looking at the studies, it helps to understand why anyone took the idea seriously in the first place. A dog's sense of smell is not a slightly better version of ours. It is a different instrument altogether.

The American Kennel Club puts a dog's sense of smell at somewhere between 10,000 and 100,000 times more acute than a human's — a range wide enough to admit that nobody has a tidy number, but narrow enough to make the point. Dogs devote a far larger share of their brains to processing scent than we do, and they have a specialised sniffing technique: when a dog sniffs, air is drawn in through the nostrils and exhaled through slits at the side of the nose, so that incoming scent is not contaminated by outgoing breath. They can also move each nostril independently, which helps them locate the source of a smell the way we locate a sound with two ears.

According to a 2024 review in the *American Journal of Veterinary Research*, dogs can discriminate between volatile organic compounds at concentrations as low as one part per trillion — roughly the equivalent of a single drop of liquid in twenty Olympic-sized swimming pools. That is the kind of sensitivity that makes the cancer idea physically plausible. If a tumour changes the chemical composition of breath or urine by even a tiny amount, a dog's nose is, in principle, sensitive enough to notice.

But the same science that makes the claim plausible also adds a wrinkle. A 2025 analysis published in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* found that domestic dogs actually carry fewer functional olfactory receptor genes than gray wolves, their wild ancestors — and that scent breeds, the bloodhounds and beagles of the dog world, held no significant advantage over non-scent breeds in the genes that build the nose. The dog's nose is extraordinary, but it is not magic, and it is not evenly distributed. Training, temperament, and stamina matter at least as much as breed. As one recent review of the field put it, no published cancer-detection programme uses chihuahuas — not because their noses are bad, but because the work demands focus and endurance that small, easily distracted dogs do not have.

We have previously examined another claim about dogs' extraordinary senses — <a href="/blog/can-dogs-predict-earthquakes-fact-check/">Can Dogs Predict Earthquakes?</a> — and found the evidence there similarly mixed: a real ability, wrapped in a much bigger story than the headlines suggest.

## The First Real Test: Bladder Cancer, 2004

The 1989 Lancet letter was an observation, not an experiment. The first proper test came fifteen years later, in 2004, when Carolyn Willis and colleagues at Amersham Hospital in England trained six dogs to pick a bladder cancer urine sample out of a line of seven, and published the results in the *BMJ*.

The design was simple and honest. Each dog was presented with seven urine samples — one from a patient with bladder cancer, six from healthy controls or people with other urinary conditions — and had to indicate which one was the cancer sample. Across 54 test runs, the dogs chose correctly 22 times: a 41 percent success rate, against the 14 percent expected by chance alone. That is a long way from perfection, and it is also a long way from random guessing. The result was statistically significant, and it was the first time the claim had been tested under conditions a sceptic could respect.

The study also established the pattern that would repeat for the next two decades. Dogs could do the task, but not brilliantly, and not consistently. One dog in the Willis study performed much better than the others. The dogs were better at detecting some cancers than others. And the results depended heavily on how the dogs were trained, how the samples were collected, and how carefully the handlers were kept from accidentally tipping the dogs off.

## What the Studies Found Since

After 2004, the research spread across cancers and across bodily fluids. Lung cancer was tested in breath. Prostate cancer was tested in urine. Breast cancer was tested in sweat. Ovarian cancer was tested in blood and tissue. The results, taken together, tell a consistent story: the signal is real, and the noise is large.

A 2006 study of lung cancer reported that trained dogs correctly identified cancer from breath samples about 99 percent of the time, a figure that still gets quoted in headlines today. It is a striking number, and it deserves the context that usually gets left out: it was a small, early study, and later, larger, more rigorous trials produced much more modest figures.

Ovarian cancer produced some of the most dramatic results of all. In a series of studies by a team led by György Horváth, two Giant Schnauzers named Hanna and Lotti were trained to distinguish ovarian carcinoma tissue from healthy tissue. In double-blind tests, the dogs achieved 100 percent sensitivity and 97.5 percent specificity on tissue samples — meaning they found every cancer sample and almost never flagged a healthy one. When the team moved to blood plasma, the results were nearly as strong: 100 percent sensitivity and 98 percent specificity. The study also showed something important: the dogs could distinguish ovarian cancer from other gynaecological cancers, and early-stage tumours smelled the same as advanced ones — exactly what you would want from a screening tool.

Scent guides other animals too — mosquitoes, for instance, track people largely by smell, as we explored in <a href="/blog/do-mosquitoes-prefer-certain-blood-types-fact-check/">Do Mosquitoes Prefer Certain Blood Types?</a>.

Melanoma, the cancer from the original 1989 letter, was tested in living patients. In a proof-of-principle study, a single dog was asked to pick a melanoma sample from nine controls — three basal cell carcinomas, three benign moles, and three samples of healthy skin — in 20 double-blind tests. The dog chose correctly nine times, or 45 percent, against the 10 percent expected by chance. The result was statistically significant, but it also showed the limits of a single animal: the same dog, on familiar samples during training, had looked far more accurate than it proved to be on new, unknown samples.

The most honest summary of the field comes from a 2023 meta-analysis in the *World Journal of Oncology*, which pooled the studies of dogs detecting prostate cancer from urine. The pooled figures looked spectacular: 95 percent sensitivity and 92 percent specificity. Then the authors ran a sensitivity analysis — removing the single largest study from the pool — and the numbers fell to 72 percent and 78 percent. The authors traced the spread to breed, trainer qualification, and training duration. In plainer language, the studies were not measuring the same thing, and the headline number depended on which studies you counted.

The most rigorous test of all came in 2025, when a prospective trial of breast cancer detection was published in *Nature Communications*. Unlike earlier studies, this one tested dogs on women whose lesions had already been flagged by mammography, with biopsy results as the gold standard. Two dogs sniffed sweat samples collected on compresses worn overnight. The results were sobering. A single dog chosen at random identified 68 percent of cancers but correctly cleared only 27 percent of benign lesions. Counting a sample as positive if either dog flagged it pushed sensitivity to 80.4 percent, but specificity fell to 21.6 percent — meaning roughly four out of five women without cancer would have been flagged. Agreement between the two dogs was limited. The trial was suspended after the first 76 women with cancer were analysed. The contrast with the same team's earlier proof-of-concept study, which had reported 90.3 percent sensitivity, shows how much the answer depends on the conditions of the test.

## The 2026 Hemangiosarcoma Study

The newest evidence comes from veterinary medicine, and it is a useful reminder that the science is still moving. In early 2026, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania's School of Veterinary Medicine, led by Dr. Cynthia Otto and postdoctoral fellow Clara Wilson, published a study in *The Veterinary Journal* on hemangiosarcoma — an aggressive blood-vessel cancer in dogs for which there is currently no reliable, non-invasive screening tool.

The team recruited five trained bio-detection dogs — animals already trained to recognise odours associated with other diseases, including human ovarian and pancreatic cancer — and tested them on blood serum samples from three groups: dogs confirmed to have hemangiosarcoma, dogs with other non-cancerous diseases, and healthy dogs. The testing used olfactometers, specialised devices that record exactly when a dog's nose enters a sample port, under rigorous double-blind conditions. Across all trials, the dogs correctly identified the hemangiosarcoma samples about 70 percent of the time — a figure the researchers noted falls directly within the range consistently observed in studies of dogs detecting human cancers.

The study's real finding was not the 70 percent. It was that hemangiosarcoma has a distinct, detectable scent profile at all. That matters because the disease is usually caught late, when routine blood work still looks normal and the cancer is already advanced. The researchers were careful to say that the study does not solve that problem today. But it adds another cancer to the list of diseases with a smell — and another reason to believe the signal is real.

## Why the Numbers Jump Around

If you read the studies in order — 41 percent for bladder cancer, 99 percent for lung cancer, 100 percent for ovarian tissue, 70 percent for hemangiosarcoma, 68 percent for breast cancer — the obvious question is why the numbers vary so much. The answer is not that some studies are honest and others are not. It is that the task is genuinely hard, and the studies differ in almost every variable that matters.

A 2020 review in *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* catalogued the reasons, and they are unglamorous. The largest problem is handler expectancy, better known as the Clever Hans effect. Dogs read people extremely well — so well that a handler who knows which sample is positive can leak the answer without opening her mouth, through a glance, a pause, or a change in breathing. Studies that are not properly double-blinded, with neither the dog nor the handler knowing which sample is which, will overstate the dog's ability. The best studies, like the Penn Vet hemangiosarcoma work, use machines to record the dog's choices precisely so that human cues cannot creep in.

Sample sizes are small. The median study in the field involved around sixteen subjects — far too few to pin down a sensitivity figure with any precision. Training varies wildly: some dogs are trained for weeks, others for more than a year. Breeds differ. Sample collection differs — urine stored one way, breath collected another, sweat on a compress worn overnight. And there is the quiet problem of publication bias: a study in which dogs fail to detect cancer is much less likely to be published than one in which they succeed.

A 2024 review in the *American Journal of Veterinary Research* put the scale of the field in perspective: 226 dogs have participated in published disease-detection projects, and 68 percent of those projects focused on cancer, with lung, prostate, and breast cancers the most studied. That is a real body of work — but it is small compared with the evidence base for any approved diagnostic test, and it is fragmented across dozens of small teams using different methods.

## What Dogs Are Actually Smelling

It is worth pausing on the mechanism, because it explains both the promise and the limits of the whole idea. Dogs are not detecting cancer itself. They are detecting the chemical exhaust of a body in which cancer is present: volatile organic compounds, small molecules released by normal metabolism that change in type and concentration when a tumour is growing, when the immune system responds, or when tissue is damaged.

Those compounds leave the body in breath, urine, sweat, and blood. They can be measured with laboratory instruments — gas chromatography and mass spectrometry have identified candidate compounds for several cancers — and they can be detected by electronic noses. In one ovarian cancer study, an electronic nose correctly classified 84.4 percent of cancerous tissues and 86.8 percent of control material. That is worse than the best dog results and better than the worst, which is roughly the point: the scent is there, but it is faint, variable, and mixed in with everything else a body emits.

This is why the researchers most deeply involved in the field describe the endgame not as clinics full of dogs, but as machines. The Penn Vet team, working with Penn Medicine and the Monell Chemical Senses Center on ovarian cancer, has said its goal is to characterise the specific compounds, so that a sensor can do what the dog does — consistently, cheaply, and at scale. The dog is a proof of concept. The machine is the product. That work is still in progress, and it is the reason the field keeps publishing even though no dog will ever be approved as a medical device.

## Can Your Pet Dog Tell You Have Cancer?

This is the question that actually matters to most people who read about this research, and it is where the gap between the science and the story is widest. The studies tested trained dogs, working under controlled conditions, on prepared samples. They did not test untrained pets wandering around a living room. A dog that sniffs at you more than usual is not a screening test. It might be smelling a new soap, a change in your diet, or nothing at all.

That distinction is not a technicality. It is the whole point. The 1989 Lancet letter is sometimes retold as a story about a dog diagnosing its owner. What it actually described was a dog behaving persistently and unusually around a lesion — behaviour that prompted the owner to see a doctor, who made the diagnosis. The dog was not the test. The dermatologist was.

The same caution applies to the trained-dog studies. Even the best results come from conditions that do not resemble a clinic: small groups of patients, samples prepared in a laboratory, dogs working in short sessions with rest breaks. The 2025 breast cancer trial is a warning about what happens when the conditions get closer to the real world — specificity collapses, dogs disagree with each other, and a promising proof of concept becomes a test that would flag four healthy women out of five.

None of this means the research is pointless. Ovarian cancer, the disease behind some of the strongest dog results, is notoriously hard to catch early, and it kills precisely because it is usually found late. If the scent profile that dogs detect can be turned into a blood test or a breath test, it could save lives. But the path from a dog sniffing a sample to a test your doctor can order runs through chemistry, engineering, and large clinical trials — not through buying a beagle.

If you are worried about cancer, the advice is boring and unchanged: use the screening tests that exist and are proven — mammograms, colonoscopies, cervical screening, low-dose CT for heavy smokers where it is offered — and see a doctor about a symptom that persists, rather than waiting for an animal to notice it. Dogs have been part of this story for 35 years. They have never been a substitute for medicine. ## The Verdict

So, can dogs smell cancer? The evidence says yes — trained dogs can distinguish cancer samples from healthy ones by scent, repeatedly, across several cancers, at rates far above chance. The 2004 bladder cancer trial, the ovarian cancer studies, the lung cancer work, and the 2026 hemangiosarcoma study all point in the same direction, and the mechanism — volatile organic compounds in breath, urine, sweat, and blood — is well understood.

But the claim as it usually travels is bigger than the evidence. Dogs are not reliable diagnosticians. Accuracy varies from 41 percent to near-perfect depending on the study, the cancer, the dog, and the conditions. The most rigorous recent trial, on breast cancer, found that dogs would flag most healthy women as positive. Handler cues can inflate results, sample sizes are small, and no dog-based test is approved for clinical use anywhere.

That is why the verdict is **Mostly True**. The ability is real, and the science behind it is serious. But a dog smelling cancer in a laboratory is not the same as a dog diagnosing cancer in your home, and anyone selling the second idea is selling something the evidence does not support. The nose knows something. It just does not know enough — yet.
