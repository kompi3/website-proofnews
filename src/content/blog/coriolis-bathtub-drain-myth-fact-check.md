Pull the plug on a bathtub anywhere on Earth and watch the water spiral down the drain. Now imagine doing it in Sydney, or Boston, or exactly on the equator line in Kenya. For generations, a piece of dinner-table physics has insisted the spiral would flip direction with the hemisphere: counterclockwise north of the equator, clockwise south of it, and straight down — no spin at all — right on the line. It sounds like science. It wears the costume of science. It is, as stated, almost entirely wrong.

This is one of the most durable myths in popular physics. It has been repeated in classrooms, travel guides, and sitcoms. Tourists in Africa have paid good money to watch a man demonstrate it with a basin at the equator. And yet the claim, in the form most people believe it, is false. The evidence against it includes a famously patient experiment at MIT in 1962, a mirror-image confirmation in Australia in 1965, and a devastatingly simple observation you can make in your own home: drains in the same hemisphere swirl in both directions.

The truth is more interesting than the myth. The Coriolis effect is real. It does bend moving air and water. But your bathtub is not a hurricane, and the universe, it turns out, is not required to announce the equator through your plumbing.

## The Claim

Here is the assertion, in its most common form: because the Earth rotates, water draining from a sink, bathtub, or toilet spins counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, with the effect fading to zero exactly at the equator. Some versions add that flush toilets prove it, that ships' crews notice it, or that you can "feel" the difference when you travel across the line.

It is a beautifully testable claim. Anyone with a passport and a sink can check it. And that is precisely the problem for the claim, because people have checked it — informally, constantly, for decades — and the results refuse to cooperate. Travelers notice Australian sinks draining counterclockwise. University physics teachers are confronted by students who have seen both directions in the same dormitory. As one Hong Kong University physics course puts it, "one can also find both counterclockwise and clockwise flowing drains in both hemispheres." If the hemisphere dictated the drain, that would be impossible. Yet it happens every day, in every country, in both directions.

## An Idea Older Than Anyone Realizes

What makes this myth fascinating is that it predates most of the people who repeat it, and it has been taken seriously by serious people. The opening line of the famous 1965 Australian paper on the subject begins: "It has long been thought that water draining from a tank would rotate counter-clockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern hemisphere, provided other influences were kept small compared with the influence of the rotation of the Earth." The phrase "long been thought" does a lot of work there — it means the idea was already old enough by 1965 that its exact origin had blurred.

The underlying physics it invokes is genuine, and it was formalized nearly two centuries ago. In 1835, the French engineer and mathematician Gustave-Gaspard Coriolis described the apparent deflection of moving objects on a rotating frame of reference. Stand at the North Pole, fire an artillery shell southward, and by the time it lands, the Earth has rotated beneath it — the shell appears to veer right. In the Southern Hemisphere it veers left. The effect is real and measurable: long-range artillery and snipers account for it, high-speed aircraft navigation compensates for it, and above all, the atmosphere obeys it. The Coriolis deflection is why hurricanes and typhoons spin counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, why ocean currents wheel in enormous gyres, and why the trade winds blow the way they do.

So the myth has a kernel of truth, which is exactly what makes it so persistent. The reasoning goes: if the Earth's rotation can bend a hurricane, why not a bathtub? The answer is a matter of scale — and the scale gap is staggering. Hurricanes are hundreds of kilometers across and last for days, giving the feeble Coriolis deflection enormous distances and times to work with. A bathtub is about a meter and a half long, and it empties in minutes. Asking the Coriolis effect to steer a bathtub is like asking the tide to rock a teaspoon.

## Why Your Bathroom Is Immune

The physicist's objection is quantitative, not philosophical. The Coriolis force at the scale of a bathtub is vanishingly small — so small that the tiniest everyday disturbance swamps it completely. Alistair Fraser, a professor of meteorology at Pennsylvania State University who has spent years debunking bad Coriolis claims, put it this way: "The direction of rotation of a draining sink is determined by the way it was filled, or by vortices introduced while washing. The magnitude of these rotations may be small, but they are nevertheless gargantuan by comparison to the rotation of the Earth."

Read that again: gargantuan by comparison to the rotation of the Earth. When you fill a tub, the stream from the faucet injects rotation. When you climb in and out, you stir currents. The drain itself may be slightly off-center, the tub may be imperfectly level, and temperature differences across the water create slow convection currents. Each of these forces is, in Fraser's word, gargantuan next to the Coriolis nudge. One physics teaching site demonstrates this neatly: the hucksters of the myth need only pour water into a basin at a barely perceptible angle to implant a spin that the water will "remember" for hours — you can stir a basin, walk away for several hours, pull the plug later, and still see the ghost of your earlier swirl. Tiny pits and imperfections in the basin do the same thing, randomly, hemisphere-blind.

Toilets are an even clearer case, because their swirl is engineered. Peek around the rim of a toilet bowl and you can see that the flush water is jetted into the bowl at an angle; that engineered jet, not the planet, decides which way the water spins. Toilet manufacturers in Australia and the United States both design their bowls to swirl however they please, and physics lectures note that flushes are simply too fast — seconds, not hours — for the Coriolis deflection to accumulate into anything.

Here is the bottom line from the scale argument: for the Coriolis effect to show itself in a draining tank, the experiment must eliminate every competing influence. That means a perfectly round vessel, a minuscule centered drain, water settled motionless for many hours, and a room free of air currents and temperature drift. Your bathroom satisfies approximately none of these conditions.

## The Professor Who Watched a Bathtub Drain for Science

In the early 1960s, one man decided to find out what it would actually take. Ascher H. Shapiro was a professor of fluid mechanics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Boston, and he approached the bathtub the way engineers approach everything: by building a very careful one.

For his experiment, Shapiro used a circular, flat-bottomed tank with a centered drain hole just three-eighths of an inch in diameter, attached to a 20-foot length of hose plugged with a stopper at the end. He filled the tank six inches deep with clean, room-temperature water. Then began the war against disturbances. Small variations — a breath of air movement, a slight temperature change, a surface disturbance — create buoyancy currents that overshadow the Coriolis effect, so Shapiro covered the tank with a sheet of plastic to keep out air currents and carefully controlled the room's temperature. He even filled the tank by deliberately swirling the water clockwise, so that any counterclockwise drain he observed could not be blamed on the way he had filled it.

Then he waited. Twenty-four hours, letting every current in the water die down to nothing. When he finally pulled the plug from the end of the hose, he gently placed a tiny float above the drain — two crossed slivers of wood, each an inch long. The tank took about twenty minutes to drain. For the first twelve to fifteen minutes, the float sat motionless. Then it began to rotate, almost imperceptibly, counterclockwise — reaching a peak speed of roughly one revolution every three to four seconds.

It was a remarkable achievement, and it made headlines. But the most important number in the whole story is the one Shapiro's team reported about the strength of what they had detected: at MIT's latitude of 42 degrees, the Coriolis effect on the tank was "only thirty-millionths that of gravity." Thirty-millionths. That is the scale of the force the myth credits with visibly steering your bathtub every single day. As newspapers at the time put it, the effect "will be overcome by filling and even temperature differences and water impurities."

Shapiro published the result in the journal Nature on December 15, 1962, and for more than a decade afterward he received letters and newspaper clippings from all over the world about what became known as the "bathtub vortex" controversy. The experiment was later repeated by colleagues using his technique, including Binnie in Cambridge, England — and, crucially, by a team in the Southern Hemisphere. In September 1965, Lloyd Trefethen and colleagues published "The Bath-Tub Vortex in the Southern Hemisphere" in Nature, reporting clockwise rotation from a carefully controlled tank in Sydney, Australia. They concluded they had "acquired confidence in the hypothesis that carefully performed experiments on liquid drainage from a tank will show clockwise rotation, if done in the Southern Hemisphere."

Note the words "carefully performed experiments." Shapiro, Binnie, and Trefethen did not prove that bathtubs drain the myth's way. They proved the exact opposite of what the myth's believers think they proved: that you need a laboratory-grade tank, a day of stillness, and heroic controls to detect the effect at all — which means your actual bathtub, with its sloshing and splashing, never shows it.

## The Equator Water Trick: A Parlor Scam

If the myth were true, the most dramatic place to witness it would be the equator itself: step a few feet north, and the water spins one way; step a few feet south, and it reverses; stand exactly on the line, and it drains straight down with no spin at all. And indeed, in tourist areas near the equator — most famously around Nanyuki, Kenya, and outside Quito, Ecuador — enterprising demonstrators offer exactly this show. For a small fee, they pull the plug on a basin and let you watch the miracle.

There is only one problem: it is a trick. Physicists who have analyzed the demonstrations note several giveaways. The demonstrator often uses a different bowl for each "hemisphere," pours the water in differently, or steadies the water with a blade or hand that is secretly pushing it in the intended direction. One analysis of a viral TikTok version, posted by a traveler who tagged it with "#itsfake" and "#iknow," found the guide's "steadying" blade was doing the opposite of steadying — carefully driving the water one way or the other so floating flower petals would drift in the advertised direction. A customer who paid for the Uganda version in 2017 reported that the flowers were floating in the opposite direction than the demonstration supposedly required — the physics equivalent of the magician's rabbit escaping mid-trick.

The physics makes the fraud almost mathematical. The Coriolis effect is not merely weak near the equator; it is practically zero there. The deflection depends on the sine of the latitude, and the sine of zero is zero. So the very place where the demonstrators claim the most dramatic reversal is the one place on Earth where the effect they are invoking literally vanishes. As one debunker noted with some relish, the charlatans even get the script backwards in some performances — pouring the "northern hemisphere" basin so it spins the wrong way for the hemisphere they claim to be demonstrating.

A traveler who experienced the demonstration at El Mitad del Mundo outside Quito described the mechanics plainly: the first container of water was allowed to sit motionless before the plug was pulled, while the second was poured in with momentum — which created the vortex. Same water, same equator, different pour, different result. The celebrated "experiment" is not measuring the planet. It is measuring the pour.

Perhaps the most telling detail of all: the Nanyuki demonstrator's act was featured on Michael Palin's PBS travel series *Pole to Pole* — presented, however briefly, as though it were real. Fraser's verdict on that broadcast was unsparing: Palin, he wrote, was "shilling for bad meteorology." Television, it turns out, is not a peer-reviewed journal.

## The Simpsons Broadcast It to Millions

If you learned this myth somewhere specific, there is a good chance it was Springfield. On February 19, 1995, the sixteenth episode of *The Simpsons*' sixth season — "Bart vs. Australia," written by Bill Oakley and Josh Weinstein — built an entire plot around the drain claim. Bart notices his bathroom sink drains counterclockwise; Lisa explains the Coriolis effect; the skeptical Bart embarks on a ruinously expensive campaign of international phone calls to check, eventually reaching a boy named Tobias Drundridge in the rural Australian locality of Squatter's Crag. The six-hour collect call racks up a $900 phone bill, Tobias's father demands payment, Australia indicts Bart for fraud, and the family flies Down Under so Bart can apologize.

The episode's most memorable gag lands at the American embassy in Canberra. Bart asks the ambassador whether the toilets go backward there. The ambassador reassures him: "To combat homesickness, we've installed a device that makes them swirl the correct, American way." He opens the bathroom door to reveal a toilet fitted with a large, clanking contraption. He flushes; the water starts going clockwise; the machine roars to life and forces it counterclockwise instead. Homer, misty-eyed, whispers, "Sweet land of liberty."

It is a perfect joke — and, as physics commentators have noted ever since, a perfect vehicle for the myth. Millions of viewers absorbed the premise that hemisphere determines drain direction as background truth, the same way viewers absorbed that the embassy needed a machine to fix what the planet supposedly got wrong. The writers were doing comedy, not science; but the episode became one of the most efficient myth-delivery systems ever built. People who have never read a word about Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis can quote the premise of his 1835 paper, filtered through a cartoon.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that the Coriolis effect makes sinks, bathtubs, and toilets drain counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere — in the ordinary, observable, everyday sense — is false.

What is true: the Coriolis effect exists, it deflects moving air and water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern, and it is the reason hurricanes spin the way they do. Under extraordinary laboratory conditions — a perfectly circular tank, a tiny centered drain, water settled motionless for 24 hours, a draft-free temperature-controlled room — scientists have detected its whisper in draining water: counterclockwise in Boston, clockwise in Sydney.

What is false: that this whisper determines the direction of any real drain. The evidence is overwhelming and comes from every direction at once. Controlled experiments show the force is thirty-millionths of gravity at mid-latitudes — weaker than the disturbance of filling the tub. Physicists observe drains swirling both ways in both hemispheres. Toilet swirl is set by the angle of the flush jets. And the famous equator demonstration, the single most persuasive piece of "evidence" most people will ever see, is a sleight-of-hand trick performed where the effect is mathematically zero.

The myth survives because it is almost true. It takes a real phenomenon, shrinks the scale by a factor of a few hundred kilometers, and trusts that nobody will notice the arithmetic. Shapiro noticed. Trefethen noticed. And now, the next time someone tells you at a dinner party that your bathtub knows which hemisphere it is in, you can smile and tell them: it does not. It only knows how you filled it.
