Scroll through any fitness corner of TikTok, Reddit, or YouTube and you will eventually meet the same warning, delivered with total confidence: creatine makes your hair fall out. The comments underneath are full of young lifters asking whether the strength gains are worth going bald, and plenty of replies from strangers who swear they watched their hairline retreat within weeks of opening their first tub of white powder. It is one of the most durable scares in sports nutrition — and it rests on an astonishingly thin foundation. The entire claim traces back to a single small study from 2009 that never measured a single hair. Sixteen years later, researchers finally ran the trial that should have been run first: they gave creatine to one group of men and a placebo to another, then actually counted their hair. The result was unambiguous. This is the story of how a hormone reading became a baldness myth, what the science has said ever since, and why the best available evidence says the answer to the question in the headline is no.

## How the Claim Went Viral

Creatine is the most studied sports supplement in the world, and also one of the most widely used. It is a compound the body makes naturally from amino acids and stores mostly in muscle, where it helps regenerate adenosine triphosphate, the cell's quick-access energy currency. A standard daily dose of around 3 to 5 grams of creatine monohydrate is the routine of millions of gym-goers, athletes, and older adults interested in maintaining muscle. A 2024 meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials in adults under 50 found that creatine added to resistance training increased lean body mass by 1.14 kilograms more than training alone, while trimming body fat by 0.88 percentage points. It is not a steroid, not a prohormone, and not testosterone, and it is not on the World Anti-Doping Agency's list of banned substances. By the standards of sports nutrition, it is about as mainstream as a supplement gets.

So why does the hair-loss warning refuse to die? Part of the answer is timing. Hair loss is one of the most emotionally loaded side effects a young man can imagine, and the age when men typically start taking creatine — late teens to early thirties — overlaps almost exactly with the age when male-pattern hair loss first becomes visible. A man who starts lifting at 22, starts creatine at 23, and notices his hairline receding at 24 has a ready-made explanation sitting in his kitchen cupboard, even if the two events are entirely unrelated. Correlation feels like causation when the stakes are personal, and a supplement tub is a much more satisfying villain than genetics or the passage of time.

The other part of the answer is the internet's memory. A single study published in 2009 reported that creatine raised levels of a hormone called dihydrotestosterone, or DHT, in a small group of rugby players. DHT is the hormone most directly involved in male-pattern hair loss, so the leap from "creatine raised DHT" to "creatine causes baldness" took about five minutes — and it has been repeated, screenshotted, and reposted ever since. In May 2024, the science communicator Rhonda Patrick traced the claim back to that same 2009 paper on her FoundMyFitness platform, showing how a narrow hormone finding had been stretched into a sweeping warning. But by then the myth had a life of its own, amplified by forums, fitness influencers, and countless videos with titles that state the conclusion before offering any evidence. The claim survived because it sounds biologically plausible, and because for sixteen years nobody had actually tested the thing people were worried about: the hair itself.

## The 2009 Study That Started It All

To understand the myth, you have to look closely at the study that created it, because its details matter far more than its headline. The study was conducted by van der Merwe, Brooks, and Myburgh and published in 2009 in the Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine. The researchers recruited 20 college-aged male rugby players and ran a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial — a respectable design for its size. For seven days, the creatine group took a loading dose of 25 grams per day, followed by a maintenance dose of 5 grams per day for a further 14 days. Sixteen of the 20 players completed the protocol. Blood samples were taken at baseline, after the loading phase, and after the maintenance phase, and the researchers measured testosterone, free testosterone, and DHT.

The results, as reported, were striking on paper. DHT in the creatine group rose about 56 percent after the seven-day loading phase and remained roughly 40 percent above baseline after the two-week maintenance phase. The ratio of DHT to testosterone rose by about 36 percent, then settled at roughly 22 percent above baseline. Total and free testosterone did not change. If you stopped reading there, the baldness warning would seem almost reasonable: a hormone known to shrink hair follicles had jumped by more than half.

But several details in the same study tell a different story. First, the DHT rise stayed within the normal clinical range the whole time. The creatine group's average DHT went from 0.98 to 1.53 nanomoles per liter, while the normal range for healthy adult men runs up to 3.27 nanomoles per liter. In other words, the group's DHT went from the low end of normal to the middle of normal — a real change, but not a surge into dangerous territory. Second, the creatine group started the study with DHT about 23 percent lower than the placebo group, 0.98 against 1.26 nanomoles per liter, which made any subsequent rise look larger than it would have from a level starting point. Third, and most important of all: the study did not measure hair. Not hair count, not hair density, not scalp DHT, not the ratio of growing to resting hairs, not follicle size. Nothing about hair. The researchers measured a hormone in the blood and stopped there. They did not claim that creatine causes hair loss, because their study could not support that claim — and yet the claim is exactly what the internet attached to their names.

The final weakness is replication. In science, one small study is a starting point, not a verdict. In the sixteen years after 2009, no follow-up study reproduced even the DHT finding, let alone documented actual hair loss. The same research group's own follow-up work in 2010 did not reproduce the result. The 2009 paper stands alone, unconfirmed, and its numbers have never been repeated.

## What DHT Actually Does to Hair

The reason the 2009 finding sounded so alarming is that DHT really is the central hormone in male-pattern hair loss, known medically as androgenetic alopecia. Understanding the mechanism shows why a blood DHT reading, on its own, proves so little.

DHT is produced when the enzyme 5-alpha reductase converts testosterone into a more potent androgen. In the scalp of a genetically susceptible person, DHT binds to receptors in hair follicles and progressively miniaturizes them: each growth cycle produces a thinner, shorter hair until the follicle eventually stops producing visible hair at all. This is why drugs like finasteride, which block 5-alpha reductase and lower DHT, can slow or partly reverse pattern hair loss. The mechanism is real, well documented, and the reason any report of raised DHT gets attention.

But genetics, not supplements, is by far the strongest predictor of androgenetic alopecia. Whether your follicles are sensitive to DHT is written largely in your DNA — the same reason pattern baldness runs in families, and why two men with identical hormone levels can have very different hairlines. A rise in blood DHT matters only if it reaches the scalp in a meaningful amount, meets follicles that are sensitive to it, and stays elevated long enough to change the growth cycle, which plays out over months and years, not days. Serum DHT — the kind measured in a blood test — is also not the same as scalp DHT, the concentration inside the follicle itself, which is what actually drives miniaturization. A blood reading is a rough proxy, not a direct measurement of what the follicle experiences.

That chain of conditions is why a 56 percent rise in blood DHT, even if it were real and lasting, would not automatically mean hair loss. It is a reason to ask a question, not an answer. And for sixteen years, the question went unasked in the form that mattered: nobody measured the hair.

## Fifteen Years of Follow-Up Research

Science did not stand still after 2009, even if the myth did. The first major reckoning came from the International Society of Sports Nutrition. Its 2017 position stand on creatine, authored by Kreider and colleagues, reviewed more than 500 primary studies and concluded that creatine supplementation does not negatively affect testosterone, free testosterone, or hair. Across that enormous safety review, the position stand found no evidence that short-term or long-term creatine monohydrate use causes hair loss or baldness. It remains one of the most-cited reference documents in the field, representing the consensus of 13 leading exercise-science researchers.

Four years later, a team led by Jose Antonio published a review specifically targeting the most persistent creatine myths, including hair loss, in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. They pooled the available studies on creatine, testosterone, and DHT — 12 studies in one analysis, 13 in another count — and found that in 10 of them, neither testosterone nor DHT changed at all. In the remaining studies, only minimal increases appeared, with no practical significance. Their conclusion was plain: "the current body of evidence does not indicate that creatine supplementation increases total testosterone, free testosterone, DHT, or causes hair loss/baldness." An updated version of the review in 2024 reached the same verdict.

The pattern across all of this research is consistent. The 2009 finding is the outlier, not the rule. Twelve other studies investigated creatine's effects on testosterone and DHT, and most found no significant changes. Not one study in the entire literature reported direct hair loss or balding as a consequence of creatine supplementation. The myth's evidence base was, and remained, a single small trial that measured a hormone and never looked at a scalp.

## The 2025 Trial That Finally Measured Hair

The missing piece arrived in April 2025, when a team led by Mohammadyasin Lak of the Sport Sciences Research Institute of Iran published the first randomized controlled trial designed to answer the hair question directly, in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. The paper, titled "Does Creatine Cause Hair Loss? A 12-Week Randomized Controlled Trial," is indexed under DOI 10.1080/15502783.2025.2495229 and PMID 40265319, and it is the study this entire debate had been waiting for.

The design was straightforward and deliberately ordinary — the closest test yet to a normal lifter's routine. Forty-five resistance-trained men aged 18 to 40, each with at least a year of weight training behind them, were randomly assigned to either 5 grams of creatine monohydrate per day or a matching placebo of 5 grams of maltodextrin per day, for 12 weeks. Neither the men nor the researchers knew who was getting what. Thirty-eight participants completed the study. The researchers measured total testosterone, free testosterone, and DHT in the blood at baseline and at week 12. For the hair itself, they used trichogram analysis and the FotoFinder digital imaging system — tools specifically designed to detect changes in hair growth — measuring hair density, total hair count, follicular unit count, cumulative hair thickness, the percentage of growing versus resting hairs, and the balance of terminal versus vellus hairs.

The results were unambiguous. There were no significant differences between the creatine group and the placebo group on any hormone and on any hair measure. DHT did not rise. The DHT-to-testosterone ratio did not change. Hair density, hair count, follicular units, and hair thickness were all statistically indistinguishable between the groups. After 12 weeks on the standard 5-gram daily dose, the men taking creatine looked exactly like the men taking a dummy powder — in their blood and on their scalps. The authors described their work as the first study to directly assess hair follicle health following creatine supplementation, providing strong evidence against the claim that creatine contributes to hair loss in healthy young males.

This is what makes the 2025 trial decisive in a way the earlier literature could not be. The 2009 study asked whether creatine moves a hormone. The 2025 study asked whether creatine harms hair — and measured the hair to find out. When the only trial that counted hair follicles found no difference, the claim lost the last piece of ground it was standing on.

## The Verdict

**False.** There is no good evidence that creatine causes hair loss. The claim rests entirely on one small 2009 study of 20 rugby players that reported a rise in blood DHT — a rise that stayed within the normal range, that started from an unusually low baseline, that was never replicated, and that was never accompanied by any measurement of hair. In the sixteen years since, reviews pooling a dozen or more studies have found no consistent effect on testosterone or DHT, and the International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand found no evidence of hair loss from creatine use. Most decisively, the first randomized trial to measure hair follicles directly, published in 2025, found no difference between creatine and placebo in DHT or in any of eight hair measures after 12 weeks at the standard 5-gram daily dose. The scare is a textbook case of a plausible mechanism outrunning the evidence: DHT matters for hair loss, but nothing in the research shows that creatine meaningfully raises it or harms hair.

That verdict comes with honest limits, and they matter. The 2025 trial was small — 38 men completed it — and short, at 12 weeks, which is too brief to rule out a very slow change playing out over years. It studied healthy young men who lift weights, not women, older adults, or people already experiencing hair loss. It screened nobody's genes, so it cannot say what happens in men with the strongest family history of baldness, and it measured DHT in the blood, not in the scalp, a gap the American Hair Loss Association has pointed out. None of that rescues the claim — a claim needs positive evidence, and after sixteen years and hundreds of studies, there is none — but it does mean the honest statement is that the evidence shows no link, rather than that a link has been proven impossible. If you have a strong family history of pattern hair loss and you are worried, the sensible step is to talk to a doctor or dermatologist before deciding, and to remember that the supplement aisle is the wrong place to look for the cause of a receding hairline. Genetics, age, and hormones you were born with are doing that work, and they were doing it long before the creatine tub arrived.

## What the Rest of the Supplement Shelf Teaches Us

The creatine scare is not unique. It belongs to a familiar pattern in which a single study, a plausible mechanism, and a frightening outcome combine into a warning that outlives the evidence by years. Supplement claims are especially prone to this because the products are cheap, widely used, and discussed constantly online, where a scary headline travels much further than a careful correction. The same pattern shows up across the shelf: multivitamins are sold as longevity insurance, seed oils are blamed for nearly every modern disease, and vitamin D is marketed as a shield against colds and flu — claims that each deserve the same treatment creatine just received, which is to ask what the trials actually measured. Readers who want to see how that method works on other popular products can start with our fact-checks on <a href="/blog/daily-multivitamins-extend-life-fact-check/">whether daily multivitamins really extend your life</a>, <a href="/blog/seed-oils-toxic-claim-fact-check/">whether seed oils are toxic</a>, and <a href="/blog/vitamin-d-prevents-colds-flu-fact-check/">whether vitamin D supplements prevent colds and flu</a>. In each case, the question is the same one that settled the creatine debate: not whether the claim sounds plausible, but whether anyone has measured the outcome people are actually worried about.

There is a broader lesson here about how health myths are built. A study measures a proxy — a hormone level, a biomarker, a lab value — and the proxy gets reported as though it were the outcome itself. DHT goes up, so hair must fall out. A vitamin level rises, so immunity must improve. A calorie count drops, so weight must follow. But proxies are not outcomes, and the distance between them is where most health myths live. The 2009 creatine study measured a proxy. The 2025 trial measured the outcome. That is the whole difference between a scare and an answer, and it is why the 2025 paper matters far beyond the gym: it is a reminder to ask, of any alarming claim, the simplest question in science — did anyone actually check?

## Creatine, In Brief

For readers weighing the supplement on its own merits, the wider evidence is worth a short summary. Creatine monohydrate is the most researched form, and the standard dose in the trials is 3 to 5 grams per day, with some protocols starting with a short loading phase of around 20 to 25 grams per day split across doses. The performance benefit is modest but real: the 2024 meta-analysis found about 1.14 kilograms of extra lean mass when creatine was combined with resistance training, and decades of trials support small improvements in strength and power, particularly in short, high-intensity efforts. The safety record in healthy adults is strong across hundreds of trials — the best-known side effects are water retention in muscle and, at large single doses, stomach discomfort, with gastrointestinal complaints appearing in 9.2 percent of creatine study groups against 4.3 percent of placebo groups in one large 2025 analysis. Claims that creatine damages the kidneys in healthy people are not supported by the evidence, and neither, as this article has shown, is the claim that it causes hair loss. None of this is medical advice, and anyone with a kidney condition, or taking medication, should talk to a clinician first. But for a healthy adult, the supplement's reputation for danger rests far more on internet repetition than on research.

## Why the Myth Will Probably Survive Anyway

Here is the uncomfortable coda: this article will not kill the creatine hair-loss myth. Myths built on a single study and a plausible mechanism are remarkably hard to dislodge, because every new lifter who notices thinning hair will reach for the same explanation, and every forum thread will contain someone who is sure it happened to them. Personal anecdotes are immune to randomized trials in a way that no amount of evidence can fix — a man who lost hair while taking creatine will always trust his own mirror over a trichogram. Add the fact that the 2025 trial, while decisive, is small and short, and the myth's defenders will always have room to say the study was not long enough, or did not test the right people, or measured the wrong thing.

That is precisely why the verdict matters, and why it should be stated plainly rather than hedged into meaninglessness. The claim that creatine causes hair loss has been tested, directly and indirectly, for sixteen years. The indirect tests — a dozen studies of testosterone and DHT — found nothing consistent. The direct test — the first trial to count hair follicles under creatine — found nothing at all. The burden of proof belongs to the claim, and the claim has not met it. A myth that survives because it feels true is still a myth, and the men deciding whether to take a legal, well-studied supplement deserve to know that the warning they keep hearing comes from a hormone reading in 20 rugby players in 2009, not from anyone's actual hair.

## The Bottom Line

So, does creatine cause hair loss? No — not according to any evidence we have. The scare began with a 2009 study of 20 rugby players that found a rise in blood DHT and measured no hair at all. The DHT rise stayed within the normal range, started from an unusually low baseline, and has never been replicated. Reviews of a dozen or more studies found no consistent effect on testosterone or DHT, and the sports nutrition field's own position stand found no evidence of hair loss from creatine. Then, in 2025, the trial the debate had needed for sixteen years finally ran: 45 men, 12 weeks, 5 grams a day, hair follicles counted with a trichogram and digital imaging — and creatine was indistinguishable from placebo on every hormone and every hair measure. Keep lifting, keep the dose standard, and if your hairline moves, look to your family tree first. The science has spoken, and this time it actually looked at the hair.
