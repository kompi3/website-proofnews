It is the objection every solar salesperson hears sooner or later, and it sounds like common sense. You live somewhere with grey skies for weeks at a time. The sun hides behind cloud for most of the winter. Surely panels on your roof would sit there doing nothing for half the year, an expensive decoration waiting for a blue sky that rarely comes. The claim travels in exactly that form on social media and in comment sections: solar panels do not work on cloudy days, so they are pointless anywhere that is not sunny most of the year.

It feels true. It is also wrong. Solar panels do work on cloudy days. They work less well — sometimes much less well — but they do not switch off when the sun disappears behind a cloud. The confusion comes from a misunderstanding of what a solar panel actually harvests. It does not harvest sunshine in the everyday sense of a bright disc in a blue sky. It harvests photons, and on a cloudy day the sky is still full of them, scattered in every direction by the water droplets in the clouds. The real question is not whether panels work under cloud, but how much they produce — and the answer, measured again and again in field data and in the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory's own modelling tools, is a range that depends almost entirely on how thick the cloud is.

## The claim, stated plainly

The viral version of this claim comes in two strengths. The strong version says solar panels produce nothing at all without direct sunlight — that a cloudy day means zero electricity. The softer version says they are so useless under cloud that installing them in a cloudy climate is a waste of money. Both versions rest on the same mental picture: a panel as a kind of sunbather that only functions while the sun shines directly on it.

That picture is wrong in a specific and checkable way. A photovoltaic cell converts light into electricity through the photovoltaic effect, and the light it needs is not the direct beam from the sun. It is any light of the right wavelengths hitting the cell. Direct sunlight is the most intense source, but it is not the only one. Sunlight scattered by clouds, dust, and the atmosphere — what scientists call diffuse irradiance — reaches a panel from the whole dome of the sky at once, and the panel converts it in exactly the same way. A photon is a photon, whether it travelled straight from the sun or bounced off a cloud first.

## How a solar panel actually makes electricity

To understand why clouds reduce output rather than eliminating it, it helps to know what is happening inside the panel. A standard solar panel is made of silicon cells, a semiconductor material. When a photon of light strikes a silicon cell with enough energy, it knocks an electron loose. The cell's internal electric field sweeps that electron in one direction, and the flow of those electrons is electric current. More photons mean more current, which is why output tracks the intensity of the light falling on the panel.

The industry rates panels under Standard Test Conditions, a laboratory benchmark of 1,000 watts of light per square metre falling on the panel, at a cell temperature of 25 degrees Celsius. On a clear day at noon, the total sunlight reaching the ground is close to that figure — roughly 800 to 1,000 watts per square metre of direct beam, plus another 100 to 200 watts per square metre of diffuse light scattered by the atmosphere. The panel's rated wattage, the number printed on the box, is what it produces under those ideal conditions.

On an overcast day, the direct beam is blocked. What remains is the diffuse light, and how much of it gets through depends on the cloud. A thin, high layer of cirrus might still let 750 to 900 watts per square metre through. A bright grey sky, the kind where you cannot see the sun but the whole sky glows evenly, still delivers roughly 300 to 500 watts per square metre. A dark, heavy overcast might deliver only 100 to 250. In a rainstorm, the figure can fall to 50 to 200. Because a panel's output scales roughly with the light falling on it, those numbers translate directly into the output ranges that installers and researchers report.

## The numbers: what panels actually produce under cloud

The honest answer to the cloudy-day question is a table, not a single number, because cloud is not one thing. Different sources report slightly different ranges, because they measure different panels in different places, but they converge on the same picture.

Under light cloud or high haze — thin clouds, a bright sky, shadows still visible on the ground — panels typically produce about 50 to 70 percent of their rated output. One detailed field-based guide puts light overcast at 50 to 70 percent and a bright grey sky at 30 to 50 percent of rated capacity, with a 410-watt panel still producing roughly 125 to 205 watts. A study at the University of Port Harcourt in Nigeria measured the effect directly on a 250-watt monocrystalline module: light cloud cover cut power output by 23.8 percent, meaning the panel kept about 76 percent of its normal output.

Under moderate overcast — a uniform grey sky with no visible sun — output typically falls to around 25 to 50 percent of rated capacity. Under heavy overcast, the kind of dark sky that makes the afternoon feel like evening, output generally drops to about 10 to 25 percent of peak. That 10-to-25-percent figure is the one most often quoted by installers and consumer guides for a heavily overcast day, and it matches modelling from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's PVWatts calculator, which put a typical heavily overcast day at about 14 to 21 percent of a clear day's light across several American cities.

Rain brings thicker, darker cloud, and output on a rainy day is usually around 10 to 20 percent of rated capacity. In a severe storm, with very dark cloud, output can fall to 3 to 10 percent. That is the worst case — and even then it is not zero. The panel is still generating a trickle of power from whatever scattered light makes it through.

Those numbers also explain why the experience of a cloudy day can be misleading. A homeowner watching a monitoring app on a grey morning may see output at a fifth of the midday summer peak and conclude the system has failed. It has not. It is doing exactly what the physics predicts: converting a fifth of the light into a fifth of the electricity.

## Why the sky is not dark just because the sun is hidden

There is a useful analogy for diffuse light. On a cloudy day, you can still read a book outdoors without a lamp. You can still get sunburned on an overcast afternoon — a fact we have already examined in our check of <a href="/blog/can-you-get-sunburn-on-a-cloudy-day-fact-check/">whether you can get sunburn on a cloudy day</a> — because ultraviolet light scatters through cloud too. The light is dimmer, but it is not absent, and it comes from everywhere rather than from one point. A solar panel is, in effect, reading that same scattered light and turning it into current.

The split between direct and diffuse light flips completely under cloud. On a clear day, direct sunlight makes up roughly 85 to 90 percent of the light hitting a panel, with diffuse light contributing only 10 to 15 percent. On an overcast day, that ratio reverses: diffuse light becomes the primary, sometimes the only, source. The Solar Energy Industries Association states plainly that photovoltaic panels can use both direct and diffuse sunlight to generate electricity, though they perform best when direct sunlight hits them unobstructed.

That is also why panel orientation matters less on a cloudy day than on a sunny one. A panel tilted to face the sun's path captures the direct beam efficiently. But diffuse light arrives from the whole sky dome, so a flatter panel that sees more of the sky can do relatively better under cloud. Modelling cited by one recent analysis found that a panel tilted at 35 degrees sees about 91 percent of the sky, while one at 60 degrees sees about 75 percent — a difference that matters on grey days and barely matters on sunny ones.

## The cloud-edge effect: when clouds briefly help

Clouds can even, briefly, make panels produce more than their rated output. This is a real and documented phenomenon called the cloud-edge effect — not to be confused with the separate question of <a href="/blog/can-the-government-control-the-weather-fact-check/">whether the government can control the weather</a>, which is a different claim entirely. When the sun emerges from behind a cumulus cloud, the direct beam adds to the already-elevated diffuse light bouncing off the bright cloud edges around it. For 30 to 90 seconds, the total light hitting the panel can spike to 1,200 to 1,400 watts per square metre — 20 to 40 percent above the 1,000-watt Standard Test Conditions benchmark. Panels can briefly exceed their rated wattage, sometimes reaching 110 to 120 percent of normal output. The effect is short-lived and unpredictable, but it is a vivid reminder that the relationship between cloud and output is not a simple one-way decline. According to material from NOAA's National Environmental Satellite, Data, and Information Service, certain cloud conditions can actually increase the amount of light reaching a solar array, at least briefly.

## Rain, snow, and fog

Rain deserves its own mention, because the claim about cloudy days often gets extended into a claim about bad weather in general. On a rainy day, the thick cloud overhead is doing the damage, not the rain itself. Rain falling on a panel does not hurt it; in fact, rain helps in a second way. It washes dust, pollen, and bird droppings off the panel surface, and soiling alone can cut output by 5 to 15 percent between cleanings. The Solar Energy Industries Association notes that rainfall rinses debris off panels — buildup that would otherwise block incoming light and chip away at efficiency over time. A rainy day costs output in the moment and buys a little back in the days after.

Snow is a different case. While it is snowing, thick cloud cover cuts output to roughly 10 to 20 percent of rated capacity, much like heavy rain. But snow that settles on the panel is the real problem: a layer of snow blocks light entirely, and a snow-covered panel produces nothing until the snow melts or slides off. The good news is that panels are dark, tilted, and often warmer than the air around them, so snow tends to slide off more quickly than it does from a flat roof — and a cold, sunny day after a snowfall can produce excellent output, because cold improves panel efficiency, as the next section explains.

Fog scatters light heavily and can cut output more than a moderate cloud layer, sometimes to 10 to 20 percent of peak, because the tiny water droplets suspended in the air block and diffuse light in a similar way to thin cloud cover. Fog is, in optical terms, a cloud that has come down to ground level, and panels treat it the same way.

## The surprise: cold weather helps panels

One of the most counterintuitive facts in solar power is that panels prefer cold to heat. A solar cell is a semiconductor, and like most semiconductors it becomes less efficient as it heats up. Panels lose roughly 0.3 to 0.5 percent of their efficiency for every degree Celsius above 25 degrees Celsius. On a scorching summer afternoon, a rooftop panel can reach 65 degrees or more, and that heat penalty can cost 12 to 20 percent of output. Cloudy days are often cooler, and that cooler temperature partially offsets the light-loss penalty — one reason cloudy-day output is not as bad as the irradiance numbers alone would suggest.

The US Department of Energy confirms this property, called the temperature coefficient, and notes that cold conditions can actually improve efficiency compared with a scorching summer day. The real winter trade-off is not the cold but the shorter daylight hours, which reduce total daily output regardless of cloud. A panel in a cold, cloudy climate can be surprisingly productive on a bright winter day, because the air is cold, the sun is low but unobstructed, and the panel is operating near its ideal temperature.

This is also why the snowiest American states are not the worst places for solar. Colorado, which gets plenty of snow, receives almost as much sunlight as New Mexico or Arizona on an annual basis, according to the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's solar resource maps. Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Michigan still receive around four peak sun hours a day — a peak sun hour being the equivalent of one hour of full 1,000-watt-per-square-metre sunlight. It is less than the desert southwest, but it is far from nothing.

## The country-scale proof: cloudy nations run on solar

If the claim that solar panels are useless under cloud were true, the global map of solar power would look very different from the one we actually have. Germany is one of the cloudiest countries in Europe, averaging only about three peak sun hours a day, yet it has around 82 gigawatts of installed solar capacity — among the largest fleets in the world. The United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Japan are all major solar producers despite famously grey skies. They do not achieve this by defying physics. They achieve it by sizing systems for their actual conditions rather than for an imaginary sunny climate.

The difference between a sunny climate and a cloudy one is a matter of degree, not of kind. A homeowner in Arizona might need a 5-kilowatt system to cover a given share of annual electricity use; a homeowner in Seattle might need a 7- or 8-kilowatt system to cover the same share. That is a real cost difference, and it is the honest version of the sceptic's objection. But "you need more panels" is a very different claim from "panels do not work."

How often heavy cloud actually happens also varies enormously by place. PVWatts modelling for six American cities found that heavily overcast days — days delivering under 30 percent of that month's clear-day benchmark — made up about 1 percent of days in Phoenix, 6 percent in Denver, 15 percent in Minneapolis and Atlanta, 18 percent in Chicago, and 24 percent in Seattle. Even in Seattle, the cloudiest of the six, three out of four days are not heavily overcast. And December, the hardest month in the north, is when the system earns its keep least — but the annual total, which is what a solar investment is judged on, still adds up.

One homeowner's own figures, reported by a solar guide author living in Slovenia, illustrate the seasonal swing. His 6-kilowatt array produced 180 kilowatt-hours in December 2024 and 980 kilowatt-hours in June — roughly a five-to-one ratio between the darkest and brightest months. The December number is small. It is also not zero, and it is the figure the "panels don't work in winter" claim has to explain away.

## What actually matters if you are buying panels for a cloudy place

The evidence points to a practical conclusion rather than a dramatic one. Solar works under cloud, but a system designed for a cloudy climate should be designed differently from one built for a desert.

First, sizing. Installers in cloudy regions typically oversize arrays by 15 to 25 percent relative to a sunny-climate calculation to hit the same annual output, and off-grid setups in persistently grey climates may oversize by 30 to 50 percent. The extra panels cost more upfront, but they are what turns a marginal system into a productive one.

Second, panel type. Monocrystalline panels, made from a single continuous silicon crystal, have the highest efficiency ratings — typically 21 to 23 percent or more — and the best low-light performance of mainstream panel types. Polycrystalline panels, made from multiple silicon fragments melted together, are cheaper but typically run 15 to 17 percent efficiency and fall off faster in diffuse light. Some guides report that monocrystalline panels generate 20 to 30 percent more power than polycrystalline panels on overcast days. Bifacial panels, which capture light reflected off the roof or ground from behind as well as from the front, can add another 10 to 20 percent in cloudy weather.

Third, electronics. A maximum power point tracking (MPPT) charge controller adjusts the panel's operating voltage continuously to extract the maximum power available at whatever light level is present, and it makes a significant difference in low-light conditions. Microinverters or power optimisers, which let each panel operate independently, also help, because on a partly cloudy day one panel may be in sun while its neighbour is shaded.

Fourth, expectations. The right tool for setting them is the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's PVWatts calculator, which uses the National Solar Radiation Database — decades of real weather data at 30-minute resolution — to project output for a specific location, accounting for that place's actual cloud patterns rather than a generic sunny-day assumption. Any installer who quotes a system based on a single sunny-day figure, rather than on local annual data, is not doing the job properly. This is the same kind of evidence-based reasoning we applied when we checked <a href="/blog/ev-fires-vs-gasoline-fact-check/">whether electric cars really catch fire more often than petrol cars</a> — the aggregate data, not the dramatic anecdote, is what decides the question.

## What about night?

A related question often gets bundled with the cloudy-day claim: if panels struggle under cloud, what happens at night? The answer is simple and total — at night, panels produce nothing, because there is no light at all to harvest. That is not a flaw in the cloudy-day argument; it is a different question, and the solar industry answers it with batteries and with the grid itself. A grid-connected system exports surplus electricity on sunny days and draws from the grid at night; a battery stores the daytime surplus for the evening. The cloudy-day question is about reduced output, not zero output, and it should not be confused with the night-time question, which everyone in the industry already accepts and designs around.

It is worth saying plainly because the two get conflated in online arguments. "Panels produce less on a cloudy day" is true. "Panels produce nothing on a cloudy day" is false. "Panels produce nothing at night" is true, and nobody selling solar pretends otherwise. Keeping those three statements separate is the key to an honest conversation about what solar can and cannot do.

## The Verdict

**False.** The claim that solar panels do not work on cloudy days is false. Solar panels keep generating electricity under cloud, because they harvest diffuse light — sunlight scattered by clouds and the atmosphere — as well as the direct beam. The amount depends on the cloud: light overcast typically leaves panels at 50 to 70 percent of rated output, moderate overcast at roughly 25 to 50 percent, heavy overcast at about 10 to 25 percent, and rain at around 10 to 20 percent. A study at the University of Port Harcourt measured a 23.8 percent output loss under light cloud and a 66.75 percent loss under heavy cloud. Modelling from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's PVWatts tool puts a typical heavily overcast day at about 14 to 21 percent of a clear day's light. Even the worst case — a dark storm sky — still yields a few percent of rated output, not zero, and the cloud-edge effect can briefly push panels above their rated capacity.

The softer version of the claim — that solar is pointless in a cloudy climate — is also wrong, though it contains a grain of truth. Cloudy climates do need larger systems, and winter output is much lower than summer output. But Germany, with about 82 gigawatts of installed solar and only around three peak sun hours a day, is one of the world's largest solar producers, and the UK and the Netherlands run substantial solar fleets under famously grey skies. The honest statement is that clouds reduce output, that the reduction is large under thick cloud, and that a system for a cloudy place should be sized and specified accordingly. What the evidence does not support is the idea that a grey sky switches a panel off.

So the next time someone tells you solar panels are useless where you live because it is always cloudy, the answer is not to argue about the weather. It is to ask which cloud. A thin, bright overcast costs a panel perhaps a third of its output. A dark winter storm costs it most of the day's output, but not all of it, and the rain washes the panel clean for tomorrow. The panel does not care whether the sun is visible. It cares how many photons reach it, and on any ordinary cloudy day, plenty still do.

## Why this one keeps spreading

It is worth asking why a claim this checkable keeps circulating. Part of the answer is that it is half-true, and half-true claims are the hardest to kill. Clouds really do slash output, sometimes by 80 percent or more, and anyone who has watched a solar monitoring app on a stormy afternoon has seen the numbers collapse. The leap from "output collapsed" to "panels don't work" is a small one, and it flatters a natural intuition: no sun, no solar.

Part of the answer is also that the people repeating the claim have rarely seen the numbers. The output ranges in this article are not industry marketing. They come from a Nigerian university study, from the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory's own database, from the Solar Energy Industries Association, and from homeowners who publish their monthly generation figures. They agree with each other, and they agree that the answer is a range, not a zero.

There is a broader lesson here, and it is the same one that runs through every fact-check on this site. A claim about technology that sounds like common sense deserves the same treatment as a claim about health or history: check the mechanism, find the measurements, and see whether the strong version of the claim survives them. In this case it does not. The panel on a cloudy roof is not a decoration. It is a machine doing its job with less light than it would like — and doing it, measurably, all the same.

The sky over your roof will be grey again tomorrow, and the day after that. The panels will still be working. Not at full power, not on their best day, but working — quietly converting the scattered light of a cloudy sky into electricity, the same way they have been doing since the first rooftop systems went up in places far greyer than yours. That is what the evidence says, and it is a much more interesting story than the claim it replaces. The sun does not need to be visible for its light to be useful. It only needs to be there, above the cloud, sending photons down through the grey — and it always is.
