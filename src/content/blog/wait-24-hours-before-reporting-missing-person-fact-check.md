Someone you love is not home. They were supposed to be back hours ago. Their phone goes straight to voicemail. You are shaking as you dial the non-emergency number, and somewhere in the back of your mind, a calm voice tells you what everyone knows: the police will not take a report until twenty-four hours have passed. Come back tomorrow.

That voice is lying. It is the most dangerous piece of television most people have ever memorized, and this fact-check is about unlearning it.

## The Claim

The claim is one of the most universally "known" rules in modern life: before police will accept a missing-person report, you must wait 24 hours — sometimes 48. It is repeated by well-meaning neighbors, passed around parenting groups, embedded in true-crime podcasts, and treated as settled fact in casual conversation. Surveys of police departments suggest it is among the first things officers must un-teach every worried caller.

The consequences are not abstract. Every hour a family waits because they believe they are following the rules is an hour when a search radius widens, when surveillance footage gets recorded over, when witnesses' memories blur, and when a person in danger stays out of reach. The belief that a waiting period exists is, researchers say, one of the single most damaging factors delaying missing-person reports.

## Where the Myth Was Born

Here is the uncomfortable part: the 24-hour rule was essentially invented by television. Researchers who study missing persons say crime dramas — CSI, Criminal Minds, Without a Trace — spent decades staging the same scene: a distraught relative rushes into a precinct, and a desk sergeant recites the line about nothing being possible until a day has passed. It gives the writers a tidy obstacle for the first act. It has no basis in law.

The Missing Persons Research Hub, which studies policing practice internationally, notes that shows like CSI and The First 48 "pushed this rule internationally, and continue to do so," until it became a quietly recognized "fact" about reporting people missing. One detective quoted in the hub's national research on police response confirmed the damage: delay can erase leads, degrade evidence, and send details sideways in witnesses' memories — lost time the police could have spent searching.

The myth had a little help from reality, too. In the past, some departments did quietly discourage reports on adults who had been gone only a few hours — not because of any policy, but because most adults who vanish for an afternoon have a mundane explanation and turn up. It was never written down, never applied to children or vulnerable adults, and never applied to suspicious disappearances. But the informal brush-off, repeated a few times, began to feel like a rule. In Canada, police services may indeed once have observed waiting practices; those have been formally retired precisely because of the harm delays cause.

## What Police Around the World Actually Say

The answer is so unanimous it is almost boring, which is exactly the point. Ask police forces across four continents and you get the same sentence.

Canada's national police, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, put it in a plain-language guide for families of missing persons: "A missing person should be reported as soon as possible. You do not need to wait a certain amount of time before reporting a missing person." On the supposed waiting period, they are blunt: "There is a misconception that there is a 24 or 48 hour waiting period before a missing person can be reported. This is false." Early reporting, they add, can be crucial for gathering video surveillance, interviewing witnesses while memories are fresh, and preparing media releases.

In the United Kingdom, West Yorkshire Police's official advice — supplied by the Police National Legal Database and approved by the National Police Chiefs Council — states: "There is no rule that states you must wait 24 hours to report a missing person." Cleveland and Cambridgeshire constabularies go further, reassuring callers directly: "You do not have to wait 24 hours to report someone missing. It is not wasting police time to report someone missing. We are here to protect you and your loved ones."

Australia's New South Wales Police Force says the same in its missing-person factsheet: "You can make a report as soon you have concerns for the safety of a person who is missing. You do not have to wait 24 hours. There is no waiting period."

Individual detectives say it even more plainly. Richard Coleman, a detective with the Urbana, Illinois, police department's investigative unit, told a local paper: "Call us right away with as much information as you can give us. Don't hesitate." His advice on DIY searching is equally direct: tell the police first, so they can send resources. Joe Gallo, a deputy police chief in nearby Champaign with 27 years on the force, said he could not recall a single waiting-period policy in his career: "I don't know if people are just seeing that on TV, or where that number came from, but certainly there is no waiting period required for reporting a missing person." And Patrick Wade of the University of Illinois police summed up the gap between fiction and reality: "I know in the movies and in TV we hear about some kind of 24-hour, 48-hour rule, that's not what is it in reality."

## For Children, Waiting Is Not Just Bad Practice — It Is Forbidden

For missing children, the myth does not merely fail; it runs headlong into federal law. The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, the US clearinghouse for missing-child cases, states that law enforcement agencies are "prohibited from establishing or maintaining a waiting period before accepting a missing child report." Once a report is accepted, the agency must enter the child into the FBI's National Crime Information Center system within two hours. There is no 24-hour grace period, no "come back tomorrow" — the statute explicitly forbids the delay the TV version demands.

The federal push goes further for children in the most fragile situations. For children in foster care, NCMEC notes, state agencies must report a missing or abducted child to law enforcement and to the national center "immediately, and in no case later than 24 hours" — the same 24 hours the myth offers as a waiting period, converted here into a hard deadline to act. And local departments write the rule down plainly. The Bethlehem, New Hampshire, police department's missing-persons policy reads: "There is no required waiting period for reporting a missing person. A person may be declared 'missing' when his or her whereabouts are unknown and unexplainable for a period of time that is regarded by knowledgeable persons as highly unusual or suspicious in consideration of the subject's behavior patterns, plans, or routines." Suspicion, in other words — not a stopwatch — is the trigger. Illinois enacted the Missing Persons Identification Act, Senate Bill 24 — sponsored by state Sen. Michael Hastings, championed by Cook County Sheriff Tom Dart, and signed by Gov. JB Pritzker — which ends waiting periods outright: police must immediately take a missing-person report and enter it into the statewide Law Enforcement Agencies Data System. The law also requires agencies to collect available fingerprint and dental records, photographs, and other biometrics, to publish the information in the federal National Missing and Unidentified Persons System if the person remains missing for 60 days, and to keep missing-person cases active until the person returns or is located. As Hastings put it, the law ends "outdated" practices and takes advantage of modern databases — technology, he noted, has outpaced some policies.

## Who Counts as an Emergency

Police everywhere draw the same line: the more vulnerable the missing person, the less room there is for hesitation. The RCMP lists the cases it considers emergencies requiring immediate contact with police: a child, an elderly person, anyone with mental or developmental disabilities, anyone with a disability or cognitive disorder such as autism, ADHD, or fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, anyone suicidal or with serious mental health issues, anyone with medical conditions that require medication, anyone who has recently experienced a traumatic incident, and anyone with physical limitations.

The logic is plain. A person with dementia who walks out the door cannot retrace their steps. A diabetic without insulin, a teenager in crisis, a child who cannot navigate home — for all of them, the first hours are not bureaucratic; they are medical. The UK's West Yorkshire Police makes the same distinction: a missing child, elderly person, or vulnerable person must be reported to police "straight away." There is no version of the guidance in which a worried family member is told to wait a day.

## Every Hour Counts

Why do investigators care so much about the first hours? Because the evidence decays on a schedule that does not care about the myth.

Security camera footage is overwritten on loops, often within days. Witnesses forget details or contaminate each other's recollections. Digital trails — phone pings, transit card taps, app logins — get harder to reconstruct as data ages. A person on foot covers distance: every hour of delay widens the search area, and a wider search area means thinner resources.

The stakes are highest for children. The US Office of Juvenile Justice and Delinquency Prevention describes the first 24 to 48 hours after a child's disappearance as the most crucial window for bringing the child home. That is the same window the myth would have families spend waiting. Police Magazine, the law enforcement trade publication, put the verdict on the myth in one sentence: "There is no truth nor logic to the widely held belief that one must wait 24 or 48 hours to report a missing person."

## What To Do Instead of Waiting

So the myth is dead. What replaces it is a short, practical checklist — and it starts before you ever pick up the phone.

First, do the quick obvious checks: call the person's phone, text friends and family, check the places they usually go, look in the places they were last seen. That is the "reasonable" step every police force describes, and it is measured in minutes, not hours. The moment their absence feels wrong — out of character, unexplained, or carrying any hint of danger — stop checking and start reporting.

When you report, police will ask a lot of questions, and the RCMP is candid about why: the questions are "necessary to assess risk and assist in locating the person," even when they feel personal. Have ready what you can: the person's full name and any nicknames, age and date of birth, a detailed physical description, what they were wearing, distinguishing features like tattoos or scars, a recent clear photograph, the contact details of people they might reach out to, and their email and social media accounts. New South Wales police specifically ask for all of this, and they add one thing many people overlook: if you cannot get to a station, call and ask — police may be able to come to you.

While you wait for officers, preserve what you can: do not clean or disturb the room or vehicle the person last used, keep their devices charged and accessible, and write down the timeline of when you last heard from them while it is fresh. Early reports let police do three things immediately, in the RCMP's words — secure video surveillance before it is overwritten, interview witnesses before memories blur, and prepare media releases if a public appeal will help. None of that can start until you call.

## The Myth's Echo Chamber

The television origin of the myth deserves a second look, because the myth did not stay on television. It migrated. True-crime podcasts — Serial, Crime Junkie, Up and Vanished, and a shelf of regional shows — routinely repeat the waiting-period line as established fact, usually in the form of "police said they had to wait 24 to 48 hours." Parenting forums pass it on. Well-meaning friends pass it on. Each retelling borrows authority from the last, and the result is a belief that feels folk-tested even though no one can name the law.

Researchers describe it as one of the most recognized myths in the entire missing-persons field — so recognized that debunking campaigns are launched specifically against it. Think about what that means: the false rule is so entrenched that professionals must budget time to un-teach it during the worst moments of a family's life. The crime dramas invented an obstacle for their first acts, and reality inherited it.

There is a lesson in that for the next viral claim you hear. The 24-hour rule never came from a statute, a manual, or a training bulletin. It came from dialogue, and dialogue is written to entertain. A claim that feels true because you have heard it a hundred times is not a claim with evidence — it is a claim with distribution.

## The Honest Nuance: Adults, Worry, and Common Sense

There is one grain of truth inside the myth, and honesty requires naming it. Adults have the right to go missing. An adult who has not broken contact in a suspicious way, who simply stayed out late or turned off their phone, is not automatically a police case. Departments will often suggest, for an adult whose absence is not alarming, that you first try calling the person, check with friends, family, and the last place they were — the "reasonable amount of time" phrasing used by some UK forces is advice for the worried-but-not-alarmed, not a rule.

That distinction matters, but it does not rescue the myth. A suggestion to check obvious places first is not a waiting period, and it evaporates the moment the absence feels wrong. If the circumstances are unusual, if the person is vulnerable, or if something simply feels off, the guidance everywhere is identical: report it now. As Minneapolis police guidance puts it, call the moment you have a concern — you do not need proof of danger, and framing it as "I have a concern" rather than a formal declaration removes the psychological barrier the myth builds.

And there is a second, quieter myth riding alongside the first: that reporting too soon will annoy the police or waste their time. Officers consistently say the opposite. Sorting out uncertain situations is the job; a report that ends with the person found safe is a good outcome, not an embarrassment. The person who hesitates because they fear looking foolish is trading their pride against someone else's safety.

## The Verdict

The claim that you must wait 24 hours (or 48) before reporting a missing person is **False**. There is no federal law, no state statute, and no standing police policy requiring a waiting period anywhere it has been checked — not in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, or Australia. For children, federal law in the US affirmatively prohibits agencies from imposing one. Illinois passed a law specifically to stamp out the practice. And every police force asked has said the same thing in its own words: call now; do not wait.

The 24-hour rule was not carved into any stone tablet in a filing cabinet. It was written in a writers' room, rehearsed by actors, and repeated so often that it started to feel true — a piece of set dressing that escaped the screen and started costing real families the only hours that matter. Television can keep the line for drama. Real life cannot afford it.

So forget what the detective on the show said. If someone is missing and their absence feels wrong, the rule is simpler than anything on television: call the police immediately. Not in 24 hours. Now. The clock is the one thing nobody in the story gets back.
