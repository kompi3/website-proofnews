It usually starts with a photo of a warning label. Someone unboxes a brand-new air fryer, spots a small tag mentioning cancer, photographs it, and posts it online with a caption along the lines of: we have all been poisoning ourselves for years. Within days, the post has been shared thousands of times, the comments fill up with people swearing off their air fryers, and a kitchen gadget that millions of households use every week suddenly looks like a health hazard.

The claim is simple and alarming: air fryers cause cancer. The reality, as with so many viral health scares, is more complicated than the headline — and more interesting. No air fryer has ever been shown to cause cancer. But the claim did not come out of nowhere. It grew out of two genuine scientific topics that got flattened into a single scary sentence: a chemical called acrylamide that forms in browned starchy foods, and the nonstick coatings used on many air fryer baskets. Both deserve honest attention. Neither turns your air fryer into a cancer machine.

## What an air fryer actually is

The first thing to clear up is what the machine itself does, because a surprising amount of the fear rests on a misunderstanding of the technology. An air fryer is not a microwave, it does not use radiation to cook food, and it does not irradiate anything. At heart, it is a small, powerful convection oven: a heating element warms the air inside a compact chamber, and a fan circulates that hot air rapidly around the food. That fast-moving hot air is what makes the outside of a potato wedge crisp without submerging it in a vat of oil.

This matters because the cancer claim sometimes travels together with a vague idea that the machine itself emits something dangerous. It does not. There is no mechanism by which a heating element and a fan could cause cancer. If there is any risk associated with air frying, it comes from the same places it comes from with every other form of high-heat cooking: the chemistry of the food as it browns, and the materials the food touches while it cooks. Keeping those two things separate is the key to the whole story.

## Where the scare came from

Air fryers became one of the best-selling kitchen appliances of the last decade, which means they also became a perfect subject for viral content. Two things fed the scare in particular. First, some air fryers sold in the United States carry a warning label required by California's Proposition 65, a law that requires businesses to warn consumers about products that can expose them to chemicals the state lists as known to cause cancer or reproductive harm. A label like that, photographed out of context, looks like a confession.

Second, the two real chemicals at the centre of the story — acrylamide and the PFAS family — are genuinely the subject of serious scientific research and genuine regulatory attention. That is what makes this claim different from a pure hoax. It is not invented from nothing. It takes two real concerns, strips away the doses, the context, and the comparisons with other cooking methods, and presents the worst possible reading as settled fact. That is the textbook definition of a misleading claim, and it is why this one deserves a careful answer rather than a dismissive one.

## Suspect number one: acrylamide

Acrylamide is the more important of the two suspects, because it is the one that actually ends up in your food. It forms naturally when starchy foods — potatoes, bread, breakfast cereals, crackers, root vegetables — are cooked at high temperatures, generally above about 120 degrees Celsius (250 degrees Fahrenheit), in low-moisture conditions. The reaction, part of the Maillard reaction that gives browned food its flavour and golden colour, happens between natural sugars in the food and an amino acid called asparagine. It happens in an oven, in a frying pan, on a barbecue, and in an air fryer. It is not a feature of one appliance.

Acrylamide first grabbed public attention in 2002, when Swedish researchers discovered unexpectedly high levels of it in fried and baked starchy foods, including potato products and coffee. Laboratory studies in animals found that high doses of acrylamide caused tumours, and on that basis the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies acrylamide as a Group 2A agent, meaning probably carcinogenic to humans. That classification is real and should not be waved away. But it is also important to understand what it is based on: animal studies at doses far above what people normally get from food, not proven cancer cases in humans traced to dietary acrylamide.

That gap between animal evidence and human evidence is where the claim starts to wobble. The US National Cancer Institute states that there is no consistent evidence that dietary acrylamide exposure is associated with the risk of any type of cancer in humans. A 2023 meta-analysis reviewing 17 major cohort studies found no statistically significant association between dietary acrylamide and cancers of the colon, breast, prostate, or bladder. The European Food Safety Authority treats acrylamide as a contaminant worth reducing as a precaution, and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) advises consumers to cut down on it, but the FDA has not set a maximum recommended level in food. In short, scientists consider acrylamide worth minimising, but the human evidence that the amounts in a normal diet cause cancer is inconclusive — and that applies to acrylamide from every cooking method, not just air frying.

## Does air frying make more acrylamide, or less?

Here the evidence is genuinely mixed, which is itself an important finding, because the viral version of the story usually presents one side only. Several studies have found that air frying produces significantly less acrylamide than deep frying. A study published in the Journal of Food Science in 2020 found that air frying reduced acrylamide formation compared with deep frying, and other research has reported reductions of up to 90 percent, crediting the smaller amount of oil and shorter cooking times. That is the basis for the common claim that air fryers are the safer way to make fries.

But more recent comparisons complicate the picture. A study published in Frontiers in Nutrition compared homemade French fries cooked three ways and found the highest acrylamide levels in the air-fried potatoes, at 12.19 micrograms per kilogram, versus 8.94 for deep-fried and 7.43 for oven-fried potatoes. The researchers noted that the difference was not statistically significant, and offered a plausible explanation: the oil in deep frying is capped at around 180 to 190 degrees, while the air inside the air fryer spiked to 229 degrees during the test. Higher surface temperature means more acrylamide. A separate review paper cited the European Food Safety Authority as noting that air fryers can generate 30 to 40 percent more acrylamide than traditional deep fryers under some conditions. Other controlled trials have gone the other way, finding air-fried fries with far less acrylamide than deep-fried ones.

What should a home cook take from this disagreement? The honest summary is that air frying is not acrylamide-proof, and it is not uniquely dangerous either. The amount of acrylamide depends far more on temperature, time, and how brown the food gets than on which appliance you used. The same study that found the highest acrylamide in air-fried potatoes also produced the most useful practical finding of all: soaking the cut potatoes in room-temperature water for ten minutes before cooking reduced acrylamide in every method — air frying, deep frying, and oven baking alike. Food safety authorities give the same advice for all high-heat cooking: cook starchy foods to a golden yellow, not dark brown or black, because browning is where acrylamide lives.

None of this means air-fried food is unhealthy in general. Air frying typically uses a fraction of the oil of deep frying, and studies consistently find air-fried foods carry far less fat and fewer calories than their deep-fried equivalents — reductions of 70 to 80 percent are commonly reported. That is a real health benefit, and it is one reason dietitians often recommend air fryers over deep fryers. The acrylamide question is about one chemical in browned starch, not about the whole nutritional profile of the meal. It is also worth remembering that air frying is only one branch of a much older family of kitchen scares. We previously examined whether [microwaves destroy the nutrients in your food](/blog/microwaves-destroy-nutrients-fact-check/), and whether [it is safe to microwave food in plastic containers](/blog/is-it-safe-to-microwave-food-in-plastic-containers-fact-check/) — both cases where a familiar appliance attracted a claim much bigger than the evidence.

## Suspect number two: nonstick coatings and PFAS

The second suspect has nothing to do with the food itself and everything to do with what the food sits in. Many air fryer baskets and trays are coated with polytetrafluoroethylene, better known by the brand name Teflon. PTFE belongs to the PFAS family — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, nicknamed forever chemicals because they persist in the environment and in the body for a long time. PFAS exposure has been linked in research to a range of health effects, including immune and thyroid effects, and PFOA, one PFAS chemical historically used in manufacturing PTFE, is classified by IARC as carcinogenic to humans, its highest category, Group 1, with strong evidence linking exposure to testicular and kidney cancer.

That sounds alarming, and it is the reason some air fryer boxes carry a Proposition 65 warning. But the details matter enormously, and this is where the viral claim again skips the fine print. First, PFOA has been phased out of PTFE production by major manufacturers, so a modern nonstick coating is not the same product that the older studies examined. Second, intact PTFE is stable at normal cooking temperatures. It begins to break down and release fumes only when overheated, typically above about 260 degrees Celsius (500 degrees Fahrenheit). Most air fryers max out around 200 to 205 degrees Celsius (about 400 degrees Fahrenheit), well below that threshold. The risk scenario is a damaged, scratched, or severely overheated coating, not a basket used as directed.

Third, the human studies linking PFAS to cancer mostly involve people with high occupational or environmental exposure — factory workers, or communities with contaminated drinking water — not people eating food cooked in a nonstick basket. The FDA notes that some PFAS have been authorised for food-contact uses, including certain nonstick coatings, and that migration depends on the intended use and manufacturing conditions. That is not a clean bill of health for the whole PFAS family, which regulators worldwide are now restricting. It is a statement about scale: the dose and route of exposure from a properly used air fryer basket are far smaller than the exposures studied in the cancer research.

There is still a sensible precaution here, and it is a cheap one. If your air fryer basket's coating is scratched, flaking, or peeling, replace the basket or the appliance. Do not preheat an empty air fryer for long periods, do not run it above the manufacturer's recommended temperature, and if the coating question bothers you, choose a model with a stainless steel basket, which has no coating at all. These are reasonable steps. They are not evidence that everyone with a nonstick air fryer is at risk of cancer.

## Why the warning label is not a confession

California's Proposition 65 requires warnings for products that can expose consumers to any of a long list of chemicals, even at very low levels, and the law sets its warning thresholds far below the levels at which harm has been demonstrated. That is why the same style of warning appears on coffee shops, car parks, hotels, and hundreds of ordinary products. A Proposition 65 label on an air fryer is a legal notice about the possible presence of a listed chemical, not a finding by any health agency that the product causes cancer.

This is a pattern worth recognising, because it drives many viral health scares. A real regulation, a real chemical, and a real classification get compressed into a photo and a caption, and the compression removes exactly the information a consumer would need to judge the risk: the dose, the route of exposure, and the comparison with the alternatives. Eating deep-fried food every day is worse for your health than eating air-fried food, on the evidence we have, even though both involve acrylamide. Throwing out an air fryer and going back to a deep fryer would, on the available evidence, be a step in the wrong direction.

## What about the other claims that travel with this one?

The air fryer cancer scare rarely travels alone. It often arrives bundled with claims that air fryers emit harmful radiation, that they destroy nutrients, or that the food they produce is somehow toxic in a way that oven-roasted food is not. None of these hold up. Air fryers do not use radiation. Nutrient retention in air-fried food is generally comparable to or better than deep frying, according to a 2019 study in Food Chemistry, because cooking times are shorter and less oil is involved. And the chemicals of concern — acrylamide above all — form in ovens and frying pans just as readily. If acrylamide in browned potatoes were a reason to throw out an air fryer, it would equally be a reason to throw out the oven, the toaster, and the frying pan. Nobody making the viral claim suggests that, which tells you something about how selectively the evidence is being applied.

There is also a broader lesson about how food scares work. The foods most associated with acrylamide — French fries, potato chips, crackers, breakfast cereals, cookies, coffee — are foods that many people eat in large quantities, and the overall dietary pattern matters far more than any single cooking method. A diet heavy in deep-fried, heavily browned, salty snack foods carries health risks that have nothing to do with acrylamide, including excess calories, fat, and sodium. An air fryer used to cook fresh vegetables, chicken, or fish with little oil is, by any reasonable nutritional measure, a healthier choice than a deep fryer used for the same meal. The cooking fat debate follows the same pattern: we previously looked at whether [seed oils are toxic](/blog/seed-oils-toxic-claim-fact-check/), and found another case where a real scientific discussion about processing and oxidation got flattened into a blanket ban.

## The Verdict

Do air fryers cause cancer? No. The claim is **Misleading**. No study has shown that an air fryer, used as directed, causes cancer, and the machine itself has no mechanism for doing so. The two real issues behind the scare are both worth knowing about, but neither supports the headline. Acrylamide is a probable human carcinogen that forms in all high-heat cooking of starchy foods, and the human evidence linking dietary acrylamide to cancer remains inconclusive, according to the National Cancer Institute. Air frying is not acrylamide-free, and some studies even find more acrylamide in air-fried potatoes than in deep-fried ones, but the decisive factors are temperature and browning, not the brand of appliance on your counter. The PFAS coating concern is real as a chemical family, but modern PFOA-free coatings are stable at normal air fryer temperatures, and the cancer evidence for PFAS comes from far higher exposures than a kitchen basket provides.

In other words, the viral claim takes two genuine scientific topics, removes the doses, the comparisons, and the context, and reassembles them into a verdict the evidence does not support. That is precisely why it spreads: it feels scientific, because its ingredients are real, while its conclusion is not.

## How to use an air fryer more safely

If you own an air fryer, you do not need to throw it away. You can, however, use it in ways that reduce the real, modest risks that do exist, and all of them are simple. Cook starchy foods to a light golden colour rather than dark brown. Soak cut potatoes in water for ten minutes before cooking, which reduces acrylamide in every cooking method. Keep temperatures at or below the manufacturer's recommendation, and do not preheat an empty fryer for extended periods. Replace baskets whose nonstick coating is scratched or flaking, and consider a stainless steel basket if you want to remove the coating question entirely. Vary your cooking methods: steam or boil potatoes sometimes, roast vegetables in the oven, and do not eat heavily browned fried food at every meal. These steps reduce acrylamide exposure from all sources, not just the air fryer, which is the point — the risk was never specific to one gadget.

It is also worth keeping a sense of proportion. The largest, best-established dietary risks to health are not exotic chemicals in a single appliance. They are the familiar ones: too much salt, too much added sugar, too much processed meat, too little fibre, too many calories overall. An air fryer that helps someone cook fresh food at home instead of buying deep-fried takeaway is, on balance, doing their health a favour. The viral post that tells them to throw it away, based on a photographed warning label, is not.

So keep the air fryer. Cook the fries golden, not burnt. Retire the scratched basket. And the next time a photo of a warning label tells you that an ordinary household object causes cancer, ask the question the post never answers: compared with what, at what dose, and according to whom? That is the question that separates a real health warning from a viral scare — and in the case of the air fryer, it is the question that dissolves the scare entirely.
