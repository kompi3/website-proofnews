You are sitting in a meeting, or on a train, or on the sofa next to a friend, when somebody yawns. A wide, slow, jaw-stretching yawn. And before you can think about it, your own mouth is opening too. You did not decide to yawn. You did not feel tired a second ago. And yet there it is — the yawn, arriving uninvited, as if it had been handed to you.

Most of us have lived this moment so many times that we treat it as a fact of life: yawning is contagious. It is the kind of thing people say at parties, in classrooms, and in comment sections, usually with total confidence. But is it actually true? And if it is, what does it mean? The popular version of the claim goes further than mere contagion. It says that catching a yawn is a sign of empathy — that warm, caring people catch yawns and cold, unfeeling people do not. That version has been repeated in magazine features, television segments, and countless social media posts. It has also been used, more than once, as a kind of parlour test of character: did you yawn back? Then you must be a good person.

The science, as it turns out, supports the first half of the claim and quietly dismantles the second. Yawning really is contagious, and the evidence for that is strong, replicated, and surprisingly precise. But the idea that a caught yawn measures your empathy is, at best, unproven — and the largest, most careful study ever conducted on the subject found no connection at all. This is a fact-check of a claim that is half true in the most interesting way: the phenomenon is real, the explanation is not.

## The Claim

The claim, in its simplest form, is this: seeing, hearing, or even thinking about someone else yawning makes you yawn too. It is often bundled with two extra claims. The first is that this happens to most people — that contagious yawning is the normal human response, not a quirk of a suggestible few. The second is that it happens because of empathy: that your brain mirrors the other person's state, and that people with more empathy catch more yawns.

You can find all three versions of the claim everywhere. Parenting blogs tell new parents that babies do not catch yawns until they develop empathy, usually around the age of four or five. Personality quizzes on social media have suggested, only half-jokingly, that failing to yawn back means you might be a psychopath. News articles have reported, with straight faces, that dogs catch yawns from their owners because of the emotional bond between them. The claim has become one of those pieces of folk neuroscience that everybody knows — which is exactly why it deserves a careful check.

The stakes here are low in one sense and oddly high in another. Nobody is going to be harmed by believing that yawning is contagious. But the empathy version of the claim has been used to draw conclusions about real people — about children with autism, about people with schizophrenia, about anyone who sits stony-faced through a yawn-filled room. If the empathy link is weaker than the headlines suggest, then those conclusions are unfair. And the story of how scientists actually tested this claim is a neat illustration of how a catchy idea can outrun its evidence, and how careful research can reel it back in.

## The First Experiments: Watching People Yawn on Purpose

The modern science of contagious yawning begins, fittingly enough, with a psychologist who spent the 1980s making college students watch videos of strangers yawning. Robert Provine, a psychologist at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County, showed his volunteers footage of people yawning, laughing, and staring blankly — and then counted what happened next. The results were striking. A large share of the students yawned back after watching the yawning videos, while almost nobody yawned after the control footage. Reading about yawning, or even thinking about it, had a similar effect. Provine had demonstrated, under controlled conditions, that the yawn is not just a private reflex. It is a social one.

Later researchers gave the phenomenon a formal name: contagious yawning, an example of what scientists call an echophenomenon — an automatic, involuntary imitation of another person's behaviour, in the same family as contagious laughter and the itch you feel when someone nearby starts scratching. It is distinct from spontaneous yawning, the kind that arrives when you are tired, waking up, or shifting between states of alertness. Spontaneous yawning is ancient. According to researchers studying the behaviour, it appears in bony fish, a lineage more than 200 million years old, and it is present in human fetuses long before birth. Contagious yawning, by contrast, is a much newer and rarer trick. Among animals, it has been convincingly demonstrated only in humans, chimpanzees, and gelada baboons — a small club, and one that has kept scientists arguing about why it exists at all.

The laboratory numbers have been remarkably consistent across four decades of replication. In study after study, roughly half to two-thirds of adults yawn after watching, hearing, or reading about someone else yawning. The exact figure depends on the setup — how long the video lasts, whether the sound is on, how the researchers count a yawn — but the range is stable. Around 60 to 70 percent of people catch a yawn in a typical experiment. The rest do not, and that stubborn minority is itself part of the mystery: why do some people catch every yawn in the room while others sit immune?

One of the most consistent findings is about age. Contagious yawning does not appear in infancy. Babies yawn spontaneously from very early on, but they do not catch yawns from others until roughly the age of four or five. That timing matters, because it coincides with the age at which children begin to develop the social and emotional skills that let them read other people's states of mind — a coincidence that, as we will see, launched the empathy theory and has kept it alive ever since. The age pattern continues into adulthood in the other direction. Younger adults catch yawns more readily than older adults: in figures reported from the research literature, about 82 percent of people under 25 catch a yawn in laboratory tests, compared with about 60 percent of people aged 25 to 49, with the rate falling further as age rises. Whatever makes a yawn contagious, it weakens with time.

## The Largest Study: 328 Volunteers and a Surprising Result

If the early experiments established that contagious yawning is real, the most important single study of the subject tested what it actually depends on. In 2014, researchers at the Duke Center for Human Genome Variation recruited 328 healthy adult volunteers, showed them a three-minute video of a person yawning, and measured everything they could think of: the volunteers' sleepiness, their energy levels, their intelligence, their age, and — crucially — their empathy, measured with a standardised survey that asked how they thought and felt in various social situations.

Two-thirds of the participants yawned at least once during the three-minute video. Some yawned up to 15 times. Contagious yawning, in other words, was common — the phenomenon itself was confirmed yet again, in the largest sample anyone had tested. But when the researchers looked for what predicted who would yawn and who would not, the results were not what the popular story would have predicted. There was no strong connection between contagious yawning and empathy. There was no connection with intelligence, or with time of day, or with how tired or energetic people said they felt. The only factor that significantly influenced contagious yawning was age: as age increased, participants were less likely to yawn. And even age explained only 8 percent of the variability in the response — meaning that more than 90 percent of the difference between people who catch yawns and people who do not remains unexplained.

"The lack of association in our study between contagious yawning and empathy suggests that contagious yawning is not simply a product of one's capacity for empathy," said the study's author, Elizabeth Cirulli, an assistant professor of medicine at Duke. The study was published in the journal PLOS ONE and was, at the time, the most comprehensive look at the factors influencing contagious yawning ever conducted. Its message was awkward for the popular version of the claim: the yawn is contagious, but catching one tells you almost nothing about how empathic the catcher is.

The Duke finding has held up in the decade since. Later research, including a 2023 study of more than a hundred adults that tested whether yawns from pet cats and dogs would trigger contagious yawning in humans, found that self-reported empathic concern was, if anything, a negative predictor of the response — people who scored higher on empathic concern were slightly less likely to report catching a yawn from an animal. The study's authors concluded plainly that their findings did not support a strong connection between interspecific contagious yawning and empathy or emotional contagion. The largest studies keep pointing in the same direction: contagion is real, and empathy is not the engine driving it.

## The Other Side: Why the Empathy Idea Refuses to Die

It would be wrong, however, to present the empathy theory as simply debunked. The research record is genuinely split, and the studies that support a social or emotional link are real studies, conducted carefully, and they deserve a fair hearing. If the Duke study is the strongest evidence against the empathy link, the strongest evidence for it comes from Italy, and it is elegant work.

In 2011, researchers Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi of the University of Pisa spent more than a year observing over a hundred adults in everyday life — not in a laboratory, but in natural social settings — recording more than 400 yawns and tracking who caught them. What they found was a clear gradient. Contagious yawning was most frequent between family members, then friends, then acquaintances, and least frequent between strangers. And the response was faster, too: the gap between one person's yawn and the other's was shorter among friends, kin, and mates than among strangers. The pattern held regardless of the social context or whether the yawn was seen or only heard. The researchers concluded that yawn contagion tracks the quality of the emotional bond between two people — that a yawn can be a sign of empathy, not merely of boredom.

Other findings point in the same direction. A well-known 2007 study found that autistic children yawned less often in response to yawning videos than their non-autistic peers, a result that was read at the time as evidence that contagious yawning depends on the social and emotional circuits that develop differently in autism. A 2015 study of college students reported that students who scored lower on empathy measures were less likely to catch yawns from video clips of faces. Brain imaging work has shown that some of the areas activated when people perceive a yawn overlap with areas involved in emotional processing. Each of these findings, on its own, is modest. Taken together, they form a case that is hard to dismiss entirely.

The debate has continued into the 2020s, and it has become more nuanced rather than more settled. Critics of the empathy link, notably the researcher Andrew Gallup, have argued that much of the apparent social gradient could be explained by attention: you simply notice the yawns of people you are close to more than the yawns of strangers, because you look at their faces more. A 2021 review of the literature pushed back, noting that out of the studies that tested whether familiarity increases contagious yawning, nine out of ten found that it did — a level of agreement the authors described as rare in social and behavioural science. Both sides, in other words, can point to real evidence. The honest summary is that the empathy explanation is contested, not settled, and that the biggest and best-controlled study found no link at all. That is a long way from the confident version of the claim that circulates online, where catching a yawn is treated as proof of a warm heart and missing one as a red flag.

## Yawning Across Species: Dogs, Chimps, and Baboons

One of the most charming corners of this research is the discovery that humans are not the only animals that catch yawns. Chimpanzees do it: in a study at Kyoto University's Primate Research Institute, chimpanzees yawned contagiously in response to videos of other chimpanzees yawning. Gelada baboons do it. And dogs do it — from humans. A University of London study found that dogs could catch yawns from people, and later work showed the effect was stronger when the yawning human was familiar to the dog than when it was a stranger. Anyone who has ever yawned in front of their dog and watched the dog yawn back has, in a sense, already replicated this experiment in their living room.

The cross-species findings matter for two reasons. First, they show that contagious yawning is not a quirk of human culture or language. It does not require understanding a joke or sharing a mood in words. It happens in animals that cannot talk about it at all, which suggests the mechanism is old and deep in the brain — something about imitation and social coordination rather than conscious thought. Second, the animal studies sharpen the puzzle about why contagion exists. If dogs catch our yawns, and chimps catch each other's, then whatever contagious yawning is for, it is not uniquely human, and it is probably not about the sophisticated, reflective kind of empathy that humans pride themselves on. A dog is not pondering your inner life when it yawns back at you. Something simpler is going on.

That something simpler may have to do with synchronisation. One influential proposal, developed by Gallup and colleagues, is that contagious yawning evolved to synchronise the arousal states of a group — a way for a band of primates, or a pack of social animals, to shift together between alertness and rest. A yawn, on this view, is a signal that travels through a group the way a wave travels through a stadium: one animal's yawn tells the others that the group's state is changing, and their brains respond in kind. It is a social signal, but not necessarily an emotional one — more like a flock of birds turning in unison than a friend feeling your pain. The distinction matters, because it explains how contagion could be stronger between friends and family (whose brains are more attuned to each other's signals) without being a measure of empathy in the moral sense at all.

## Why Do We Yawn at All? The Brain-Cooling Clue

The contagion question sits on top of a deeper mystery: scientists still do not fully agree on why we yawn in the first place. The old explanation — that yawning tops up the blood with oxygen — was tested and discarded decades ago; breathing extra oxygen does not reduce yawning, and people yawn in situations where oxygen is plentiful. The current leading theory is stranger and more interesting: yawning cools the brain.

The key experiment, conducted by Andrew Gallup at the University at Albany in 2007, was disarmingly simple. Volunteers watched videos of people yawning while holding a hot pack or a cold pack against their forehead. Those with the warm pack — whose brains were, in effect, being kept warm — yawned back 41 percent of the time. Those with the cold pack yawned only 9 percent of the time. Cooling the brain, it seemed, removed the need to yawn. The result has been replicated in various forms, and it fits with other observations: people yawn more when they are hot, when they are transitioning between sleep and waking (when brain temperature is shifting), and before important events, when mental arousal is rising. The yawn, on this account, is a kind of radiator — a deep inhalation of cool air, a stretch of the jaw muscles that increases blood flow, a brief reset of the brain's temperature.

If yawning is, at least in part, a cooling mechanism, then contagious yawning may be a side effect of a brain that is constantly monitoring the social environment for cues about what to do next. Your brain sees a yawn, registers the signal that a state change may be coming, and triggers the same cooling reflex — just in case. That is a long way from the poetic version of the story, in which your soul reaches out to another person's soul. But it has the virtue of fitting the data: it explains why contagion is automatic, why it is stronger among people who pay attention to each other, why it fades with age, and why it does not track empathy scores. It also explains why the most reliable way to stop catching yawns is, apparently, to cool your forehead — though we would not recommend walking around with an ice pack for the sake of a dinner party.

## The 2026 Update: Yawns Beat Laughter and Winces

The science has not stood still. In February 2026, researchers in Turin, Italy, published a study in the journal iScience that measured contagious reactions with unusual precision. They filmed 32 adults as they watched footage of other people laughing, yawning, and wincing in pain, then tracked their facial muscles frame by frame, isolating the exact movements each reaction produced. The counts were blunt. Yawning spread to 23 of the 32 participants — about 72 percent. Laughter caught 20, about 63 percent. The involuntary grimace people make when watching someone else get hurt — the reaction most often framed as empathy — caught only 15, or roughly 47 percent.

The study is small, and its authors are careful about its limits. Percentages calculated on a base of 32 people move by roughly three points with every single person, so the numbers should be read as description rather than as a precise estimate of how contagious these behaviours are in the general population. The study does not test why yawning spreads, and its authors make no claim about anyone's capacity for compassion. What it does show, with unusual clarity, is that yawning is the most transmissible of the three reactions tested — more contagious than laughter, and far more contagious than the wince that is most closely tied to empathy. If contagious yawning were simply a readout of empathy, you might expect the empathy reaction — the wince — to travel furthest. It travelled least. The finding does not settle the debate, but it tilts it: the thing that spreads most easily between people is not the thing we most associate with feeling for others.

There is also a practical warning embedded in this research that regular readers of fact-checks will appreciate. Simply reading about yawning can trigger it. Several of the researchers and writers who have covered this subject note, with varying degrees of apology, that the effect does not require a video or a live human being. Words on a page are enough. If you have yawned while reading this article — and the statistics say there is a roughly two-in-three chance that you have — you have just participated in the phenomenon under discussion. Consider it a live demonstration, and consider, too, how strange it is: a behaviour triggered by a description of a behaviour, leaping from a stranger's face on a screen, or a sentence in an article, into your own jaw, without your permission and without your awareness until it is already happening.

## Claim vs Evidence: What the Research Actually Shows

The confusion around this claim comes from three different statements being bundled into one. Separated out, they score very differently against the evidence — which is why the claim as a whole earns a "True" verdict with a large asterisk attached to its most popular interpretation.

| What people claim | What the evidence shows | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Seeing someone yawn makes you yawn | Replicated for 40 years; 50–70% of adults catch a yawn in lab tests, and about two-thirds yawned in the largest study (328 volunteers) | Confirmed |
| It happens to most people | Roughly 60–70% catch a yawn; 82% of under-25s, about 60% of 25- to 49-year-olds; it emerges around age 4–5 and fades with age | Confirmed, with an age gradient |
| Catching a yawn proves empathy | The largest study (328 adults, PLOS ONE 2014) found no correlation with empathy; a 2023 study found empathic concern was a negative predictor; smaller studies and observations of family bonds point the other way | Contested — not established |
| It happens because of mirror neurons | Mirror-neuron involvement is a popular hypothesis, but direct evidence in humans is thin and brain scans of people catching yawns have not reliably lit up the classic mirror-neuron regions | Unproven |
| Animals catch yawns too | Dogs catch yawns from familiar humans; chimpanzees and gelada baboons show contagious yawning; no aquatic mammal has shown it | Confirmed, in a few species |
| Yawning cools the brain | A 2007 experiment: 41% caught a yawn with a warm pack on the forehead vs 9% with a cold pack; the theory fits the data but is not the full story | Supported, not settled |

The table makes the shape of the verdict clear. The core phenomenon — contagion — is one of the more solid findings in social neuroscience. The folk theory built on top of it — that a yawn is a little empathy test — is the part that the evidence does not support. The two ideas have travelled together for so long that most people have never been asked to separate them. This fact-check is, in large part, an exercise in pulling them apart.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: True.** Yawning is contagious. That is the claim as most people type it into a search bar — <a href="/blog/do-we-only-use-10-percent-of-our-brain-fact-check/">do we only use 10 percent of our brain</a> is the kind of myth that turns out to be false, but "is yawning contagious" turns out to be one of the folk beliefs that science confirms. Roughly half to two-thirds of adults yawn after seeing, hearing, or reading about someone else yawning. The effect has been replicated since the 1980s, confirmed in the largest study ever conducted on the subject, and measured again as recently as February 2026, when yawning spread to 23 of 32 volunteers in a Turin laboratory — more readily than laughter, and far more readily than the wince of shared pain.

But the verdict comes with a correction that matters more than the verdict itself. The popular explanation — that you catch yawns because you are empathic, and that people who do not catch them are cold — is not established by the evidence. The largest and most careful study found no correlation between contagious yawning and empathy at all. A later study found that empathic concern, if anything, predicted slightly less contagious yawning from animals. Smaller studies and naturalistic observations do show a social gradient — yawns pass more easily between family and friends than between strangers — but that gradient can be explained by attention as readily as by empathy, and researchers are still arguing about which explanation is right. So if you yawned while reading this article, it proves that your brain responds to social signals. It does not prove that you are a kinder person than the reader who did not. And if you did not yawn, it proves nothing about your character either. The yawn is a reflex with a social trigger, not a moral test.

There is something reassuring about that, once you sit with it. The internet loves tests that sort people into the warm and the cold, the empathic and the unfeeling, on the basis of a single behaviour. Contagious yawning looked like one of those tests — quick, visible, and flattering to anyone who passed it. The research says it is not. What it is, instead, is stranger and more interesting: a reflex older than humanity in its spontaneous form, and a social signal, shared with a handful of other species, that still resists a tidy explanation after forty years of study. Scientists know that it happens. They know roughly how often, and to whom, and at what ages. What they do not know, yet, is exactly why. And that is a perfectly good place for a scientific question to be — unanswered, but asked properly.

For the rest of us, the practical advice is simple. If you are trying not to yawn in a meeting, looking away helps, and — if the 2007 experiment is any guide — so does keeping cool. If someone near you yawns and you yawn back, enjoy the small proof that your brain is tuned to the people around you. And if someone tells you that your yawn, or your failure to yawn, reveals the contents of your heart, you can tell them what the evidence actually says: the yawn is contagious, the character test is not.

The next time a yawn crosses a room and lands in your jaw, you will know what is happening: a reflex, a signal, and a forty-year-old scientific argument, all arriving at once. You will also know what it is not: a verdict on who you are. Some claims deserve to be believed. This one deserves to be believed precisely — the contagion is real, and the empathy test is not. That distinction is the whole point of checking.
