It happens in a single, sickening second. A phone slips from a wet hand beside a swimming pool, or tips off the edge of a bathtub, or takes an unplanned swim in a kitchen sink. The screen is still glowing. Someone nearby — a friend, a relative, a stranger in the comments of a video you will watch later — delivers the advice with total confidence: put it in rice. Bury it overnight. The rice will suck the water right out.

The advice feels ancient, almost folksy, and that is part of its power. It costs nothing, it uses something already in the kitchen, and it gives a panicking person something to do. But does putting your phone in rice work? The short answer is that rice is not useless, and that is precisely why the claim is so stubborn. Rice does absorb moisture. What it does not do, reliably or quickly, is rescue a wet phone — and in some cases it adds new problems while the clock that matters most is ticking.

## Where the rice trick came from

Long before smartphones, people used dry rice and other grain to keep things dry. A few grains of rice in a salt shaker keep salt from clumping in humid weather, because rice grains absorb a little moisture from the air around them. Silica gel packets tucked into shoe boxes and electronics packaging do a similar job on purpose: they are desiccants, materials designed to pull water vapour out of the air.

It was a short leap from those everyday observations to the idea of burying a soaked phone in a bowl of uncooked rice. If rice dries the air in a salt shaker, the thinking went, surely a whole bag of rice can dry the inside of a phone. The logic sounds tidy. The trouble is that a phone is not a salt shaker. Its water is not sitting in open air waiting to be absorbed. It is trapped in a charging port, behind a speaker grille, under a screen, and around seals and circuit boards that were never designed to be opened by a bag of grain.

The trick also arrived in an era when phones were far more vulnerable to water than they are now. Early mobile phones had removable batteries, exposed internals, and little or no sealing. A dunk often meant instant death, and any folk remedy that coincided with a phone switching back on earned a reputation it did not deserve. That reputation then travelled through forums, chain emails, and later social media, gathering confidence with every retelling.

## What the phone makers actually say

Apple's advice is unusually blunt for a company support page. In its guidance on liquid-detection alerts, Apple tells iPhone owners not to dry a phone with an external heat source or compressed air, not to insert a foreign object such as a cotton swab or a paper towel into the connector, and then adds this line: "Don't put your iPhone in a bag of rice. Doing so could allow small particles of rice to damage your iPhone."

Instead, Apple recommends tapping the phone gently against a hand with the connector facing down to remove excess liquid, wiping it with a soft, lint-free cloth, and leaving it in a dry area with some airflow. A fan blowing cool air toward the connector may help. Charging should wait until the phone is fully dry — Apple says at least five hours after it appears dry, and up to 24 hours if a liquid-detection alert is showing.

Samsung's guidance points in the same direction, even though it does not single out rice in the same sentence. Samsung says water-resistant phones should be dried thoroughly with a clean, soft cloth, that charging must wait until the device is fully dry, and that its phones deliberately refuse to charge when moisture is detected in the USB port, precisely to prevent corrosion. Samsung also warns against heat sources such as hair dryers, chemical cleaners, abrasive materials, and compressed air. In other words, the two biggest phone makers agree on the core message: get the water out, let air do the drying, do not rush to charge, and do not stuff things into the ports.

That consensus matters because it is not marketing language. A manufacturer has little incentive to tell customers that a free kitchen remedy does not work, unless its repair centres keep seeing the consequences.

## What the tests actually found

The experimental record is messier than either side of the internet argument admits, and that mess is worth understanding, because it explains why honest people disagree about rice.

One of the most quoted tests came from TekDry, a company that dries wet phones professionally. Its co-founder told USA Today in 2017 that rice was slower than simply leaving a phone out on the counter, and that neither method worked fast enough on its own to guarantee a rescue. He put a number on it: after about 48 hours in rice, only 13 percent of the water had come out of the phone. That is a damning figure if the goal is to save a device before corrosion sets in.

A test by the resale company Gazelle reached a similar conclusion from a different angle. Gazelle compared how much water different household materials pulled from a wet sponge over 24 hours, testing cat litter, couscous, oatmeal, instant rice, silica gel, and conventional uncooked rice. Conventional rice came last. More strikingly, a sponge left in open air performed better than any of the drying agents. Gazelle's conclusion was that shaking, blowing, or vacuuming as much water out as possible matters far more than the drying agent, and that open air with a fan works just as well as rice, without the dust.

Then there is a study that rice fans often cite, from the University of Huddersfield in England. Researcher Claire Armstrong simulated a wet phone with a sponge on a petri dish, then tested the methods on a real phone as well. She found that a standard 500-gram bag of basmati rice absorbed 80 percent of the water after 12 hours, 95 percent after 24 hours, and all of it after 48 hours, while flaked rice reached 78 percent in 24 hours and brown rice only 44 percent. Silica gel did just as well with only five grams. Her advice was pragmatic: keep silica gel packs handy, and in their absence, basmati or flaked rice in an airtight container was better than nothing.

How can both sets of results be true? The difference is what was being dried. A sponge sitting in a container exposes a large, wet surface to the drying agent. A sealed modern phone does not. Its water is hidden in crevices, and a bag of rice outside the case cannot reach inside it. Rice absorbs moisture from the air immediately around it; it does not vacuum water out of a charging port or from beneath a battery. The Huddersfield work also involved removing the battery first, something almost no modern phone owner can do, since batteries are now sealed inside.

Put together, the honest summary is this: rice is a weak desiccant. It will absorb some moisture from the air in a closed container, and over a day or two it may help dry the surface of a device. But in head-to-head tests on real phones, it performs no better than open air — and often worse, because burying a phone cuts off the airflow that actually drives evaporation. A remedy that is slower than doing nothing is not a rescue technique. It is a delay dressed up as a treatment.

## Why rice seems to work, even when it does not

If rice is so unimpressive, why do so many people swear by it? The answer is a mix of psychology and modern phone design, and it is worth spelling out because it explains many tech myths at once.

First, most phones sold in the past several years are water-resistant. A flagship phone with an IP68 rating is tested in a laboratory in fresh water, typically at a depth of about 1.5 metres for up to 30 minutes. That does not make it waterproof — Samsung itself stresses that water resistance is not the same as waterproof, and that salt water, chlorinated water, soapy water, and high-pressure water are all outside the rating — but it does mean that a brief splash or a quick dunk in a sink often ends with no damage at all, whatever the owner does next. If the phone survives a night in rice, the rice gets the credit for what the seals already did.

Second, time itself is a treatment. A phone left alone for 24 hours will dry substantially on its own, especially if it is powered off and sitting in a room with moving air. The rice is present during that drying, so it looks like the cause. This is the classic trap of folk remedies: the remedy and the recovery happen together, and the human brain links them.

Third, survivorship bias hides the failures. People whose phones recovered after the rice treatment tell the story at parties and post it online. People whose phones died quietly in the rice bowl usually blame the water, not the rice, and rarely post a correction. The internet therefore collects far more rice success stories than rice failures, even if the real success rate is no better than air drying.

Fourth, the phones that rice "saves" are often the ones that were never in serious danger. A phone splashed by rain, or dropped in a puddle for two seconds and fished out immediately, was likely to survive anyway. A phone that spent a minute at the bottom of a chlorinated pool, or that was switched on and charged while still wet, is in a different category altogether, and rice will not reverse corrosion that has already begun.

## How rice can make things worse

The case against rice is not only that it is slow. It is that it introduces risks of its own, and those risks are the reason Apple warns against it explicitly.

Uncooked rice sheds fine starch dust, and broken grains are small enough to lodge in a charging port or a speaker grille. Mixed with water, that dust can form a sticky residue. A cable that no longer seats properly, a speaker that sounds muffled, or a port that keeps triggering a moisture warning can all follow from a well-meaning bowl of rice. Repair technicians also report finding grains and dust inside devices that owners believed they were helping.

Burying a phone also reduces airflow, which is the one thing that genuinely speeds evaporation. A phone sealed in a bag of rice sits in still, humid air inside the bag, while a phone on a shelf near a fan benefits from a constant stream of dry air. In that sense, rice can actively slow the drying it is supposed to accelerate.

The most serious risk, though, is false confidence. A phone that spent the night in rice feels "treated," so its owner is more likely to switch it on, plug it in, and declare it fixed. Charging a wet phone is exactly what causes the most damage: electricity flowing through water drives corrosion on circuit boards and connectors, turning a recoverable dunk into permanent damage. The phone makers' moisture warnings exist to stop this, and Samsung's phones will block charging outright when the port is wet. But a phone that appears dry on the outside can still be wet inside, and a night in rice does not guarantee otherwise.

There is also the problem of what kind of water the phone met. Fresh water is the kindest case. Salt water leaves corrosive residue as it dries. Chlorinated pool water and soapy water attack seals and coatings. For those exposures, both Apple and Samsung recommend rinsing the outside of a water-resistant phone with fresh tap water and wiping it dry — a counterintuitive step that removes the corrosive salts before they can do their work. Rice does none of this. It cannot rinse anything, and it cannot neutralise salt.

## What actually kills a wet phone

Understanding the enemy helps explain why the right response is about speed and electricity, not about grain. Water itself, in pure form, is not instantly fatal to electronics. The damage comes from what water enables: short circuits while the device is powered, and electrochemical corrosion afterwards, as minerals and salts in the water attack metal contacts and circuit traces.

That is why the first minutes matter more than the first day. Every second a wet phone stays powered on, current can travel through water to places it was never meant to go. Every hour it stays powered on and damp, corrosion spreads a little further. Drying agents that work over 24 to 48 hours are therefore fighting the wrong battle. By the time rice has absorbed its share of moisture, the corrosion clock has already been running for a day or two.

Heat is another enemy, which is why the hair dryer and the oven belong in the same rejected pile as rice. High heat can warp seals, damage batteries, and crack screens, and it can drive water deeper into the device before it evaporates. Compressed air can do similar damage by forcing water further inside. Vigorous shaking can spread water to dry compartments. The theme is consistent: the gentler and faster the removal of free water, the better the odds.

## What to do instead, step by step

If a phone gets wet, the evidence points to a short, boring sequence that is far less satisfying than a folk remedy, but far more effective.

First, get it out of the water and switch it off immediately, if it is still on. Remove the case, and remove the SIM tray if you can do so without tools or force. Do not press buttons repeatedly to "check" whether it still works; each press is another chance for water to meet electricity.

Second, wipe the outside with a soft, lint-free cloth. Hold the phone with the charging port facing down and tap it gently against your hand, as Apple recommends, to coax water out of the port and speaker cavities. If the phone met salt water, pool water, or anything other than fresh water, and it is a water-resistant model, rinse the outside briefly with fresh tap water first, then wipe it dry. Do not use a running tap at high pressure, and do not insert cotton swabs, paper towels, or any object into the port.

Third, leave the phone powered off, port facing down, in a dry place with good airflow. A fan blowing cool air across it helps. Do not put it in a sealed bag, do not bury it in anything, and do not place it on a radiator or in direct sunlight to "bake" it dry. Patience is the treatment now: Apple advises waiting at least five hours after the phone appears dry before charging, and up to a full day if a liquid alert is showing. Samsung notes that water in a charging port typically evaporates within one to two hours, but that is the port, not necessarily the whole device, so waiting longer before powering on is the safer choice.

Fourth, if you happen to have silica gel packets, they are a better desiccant than rice and they shed no starch dust. Sealing the phone in a container with a generous quantity of silica gel, after the free water has been shaken and wiped away, is a reasonable optional step. It is not magic either — silica gel still cannot reach water trapped deep inside a sealed phone — but it absorbs moisture faster and more thoroughly than rice, and it does not leave grains behind. Keep the packets away from children and pets, and do not confuse them with a substitute for airflow and time.

Finally, watch for warning signs after the phone is dry and switched back on: fog under the camera lens, a screen that flickers, a battery that drains unusually fast, muffled speakers, or a charging port that keeps warning about moisture. Any of those means the phone should go to a professional repair service rather than back into daily use. If the phone will not switch on at all, or if it was submerged in salt water for more than a few seconds, professional inspection is warranted even sooner. Repairers can open the device, clean corrosion from the board with proper solvents, and assess damage that no pantry item can reach.

## The water-resistance trap

Part of the rice myth's staying power is a misunderstanding of what water resistance means. An IP68 rating is a laboratory result, not a promise. It is tested in fresh water, under controlled conditions, with a brand-new device whose seals are intact. It does not cover steam, soap, sunscreen, salt, or chlorine. It does not survive a cracked screen or a dented frame. And it degrades over time as seals age and adhesives weaken.

Manufacturers are careful about this for a reason: water damage is generally not covered by standard warranties, even on water-resistant phones. The liquid-damage indicators inside phones — small stickers that change colour permanently when wet — exist precisely so that repair centres can tell whether water got inside. A phone can therefore be "water-resistant" and still be ruined by water, and a phone can survive a dunk and still carry hidden corrosion that shows up weeks later as a dead speaker or a swollen battery.

This is why the advice to visit a service centre after a serious dunk is not upselling. Samsung recommends inspection even when a phone appears to work normally after water exposure, because moisture may remain inside. A professional can check for corrosion before it spreads, and can advise whether a battery or a board needs attention. Rice, however long the phone sits in it, cannot perform that inspection.

## The Verdict

So, does putting your phone in rice work? As a rescue technique, no — not in the way the viral claim promises. Rice absorbs some moisture from the surrounding air, and in a sealed container over one or two days it can help dry a device's surface. But tests on real phones find it slower than open air, and no better at removing the water trapped inside a sealed handset. Apple explicitly warns against it because rice particles can damage the phone, and Samsung's guidance points the same way: remove the water, use airflow, wait, and do not charge until dry.

The verdict is therefore **Misleading**. The grain of truth is real — rice is a mild desiccant — but the claim built on it, that burying a wet phone in rice will save it, overstates the effect, ignores the faster and safer alternative of open air, and hides real risks from dust, blocked ports, and delayed repair. The phones that "survive rice" usually survive despite it: they were water-resistant, the dunk was brief, and time and air did the actual drying.

The next time a phone takes a swim, skip the pantry. Switch it off, shake the water out gently, wipe it down, set it in moving air, and wait. It is less photogenic than a bowl of rice, and it will not make a satisfying video. But it gives the phone what it actually needs: stillness, airflow, and time — and it does not charge the rescue with a handful of starch dust.
