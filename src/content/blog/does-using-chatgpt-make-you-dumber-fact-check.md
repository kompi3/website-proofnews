In the summer of 2025, a frightening headline raced across social media: a study from MIT had supposedly proven that ChatGPT makes you dumber. Some posts claimed the research showed a 47 percent drop in brain power. Others said the chatbot was causing "brain rot" or even brain damage. The posts were shared by teachers worried about their students, parents worried about their children, and millions of ordinary users who had quietly started letting an AI assistant do more and more of their thinking.

There is a real study behind the panic, and it is worth taking seriously. Researchers at the MIT Media Lab really did wire up volunteers with EEG headsets, watch their brains while they wrote essays with and without ChatGPT, and find that the ChatGPT users showed weaker brain connectivity and could barely remember what they had just written. But the viral version of the story goes far beyond what the study actually found — and the researchers themselves have publicly pushed back against the way their work was described. The truth is narrower, stranger, and more useful than the headline.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, is simple and absolute: using ChatGPT makes you dumber. Depending on which post you saw, the supposed proof was that ChatGPT collapses your brain's neural connectivity, destroys your memory, or cuts your cognitive function nearly in half. One widely shared figure put the damage at 47 percent. The implication was that every essay drafted, every email polished, and every homework question answered by a chatbot was quietly degrading the user's mind — and that the effect might be permanent.

That is a big claim, and it deserves a careful answer, because it sits at the intersection of two things people genuinely care about: their own minds, and a technology that hundreds of millions of people now use every day. If the claim were true as stated, it would be one of the most important findings in the history of psychology. If it is exaggerated, the exaggeration itself matters, because it tells us how easily a preliminary scientific result can be turned into a scare story.

The verdict, in short, is that the claim is misleading. A real experiment found real differences in how people's brains behaved while they wrote with ChatGPT — but it did not measure intelligence, did not find brain damage, and did not show that ordinary ChatGPT use makes anyone dumber in any general or lasting sense. What it did show is narrower and, in some ways, more interesting: when you hand the thinking in a task to an AI, your brain does less of that thinking, you remember less of the result, and the habit can linger even after you put the tool down. That is a genuine reason to change how you use AI. It is not proof that AI is rotting your brain.

## How One Preprint Became "Proof That AI Rots Your Brain"

The story begins on 10 June 2025, when a team at the MIT Media Lab led by Nataliya Kosmyna posted a preprint titled "Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task" on arXiv, the open server where researchers share papers before peer review. The paper ran to more than 200 pages in its revised form and described an experiment in which 54 people wrote essays while wearing EEG headsets. It was, by the authors' own description, preliminary work — a first exploration of a new question, not a settled conclusion.

Eleven days later, on 21 June 2025, author Brad Stulberg published a post titled "A New Study Shows ChatGPT is Dramatically Weakening Our Brains." The post caught fire. From there the study's findings were compressed, simplified, and in some cases distorted as they travelled through newsletters, social media accounts, and news aggregators. The careful language of the paper — connectivity, engagement, ownership, cognitive debt — was replaced with punchier words: dumb, brain rot, damage, collapse.

Somewhere along the way, a figure of 47 percent began circulating as the supposed size of the brain-power loss. No such figure exists in the study. The paper never measured a percentage decline in overall cognition, never tested IQ, and never claimed that ChatGPT users lost nearly half of their mental capacity. The number appears to have been invented somewhere in the chain of re-posting, then repeated until it looked like a fact.

The distortion became so bad that the researchers took the unusual step of publishing a public FAQ asking journalists and commentators to stop describing their results with words such as "dumb," "brain rot," or "damage." They stressed that their experiment measured brain activity during one specific kind of task — writing short essays — and that it did not show that ChatGPT harms the brain. MIT itself continued to describe the work as preliminary. In December 2025 the authors posted a substantially revised version of the preprint, but the revision did not change the study's fundamental limits: a small sample, a single task, and no peer review at the time it went viral.

The timeline matters because it shows how the claim was built. A real experiment produced a real, interesting result. A dramatic retelling stripped away the caveats. And then the stripped-down version, not the study, became what millions of people believed. To judge the claim fairly, we have to go back to the experiment itself.

## What the MIT Researchers Actually Did

The experiment, known formally as Kosmyna and colleagues (2025), recruited 54 adults aged 18 to 39, mostly from universities in the Boston area, and split them randomly into three groups of 18. One group wrote essays using ChatGPT. A second group wrote using an ordinary search engine but no AI. A third group, called the brain-only group, wrote with no tools at all. The essays were short, timed pieces of about 20 minutes each, written to the kind of open prompts used in standardised tests such as the SAT. Each participant wrote three essays in three separate sessions spread over several months, while wearing a 32-channel EEG headset that recorded the electrical activity across their brains.

The researchers measured several things. The EEG data showed how different regions of the brain communicated with each other while the participants wrote — a measure called connectivity. They also analysed the essays themselves with language-processing tools and had them scored by human teachers and by an AI judge. And after each essay, they interviewed the participants, including asking them to quote a sentence from the essay they had just finished, without looking at it.

In a fourth session, 18 of the participants switched conditions: people who had been writing with ChatGPT now had to write with no tools, and people who had been writing with no tools now got to use ChatGPT. This crossover was designed to test whether the effects of using the AI lingered after the tool was taken away — the question at the heart of the paper's title, "cognitive debt."

It is worth pausing on what this design can and cannot tell us. It is a genuine experiment, with random assignment and a control group, which is much stronger than a survey or a collection of anecdotes. But it is also small — 54 people from one metropolitan area — and it studies one task, essay writing, over a few months. It does not test memory in general, attention in general, or intelligence in general. And when it went viral in June 2025, it had not been peer-reviewed: no independent experts had yet checked the methods and the analysis. Those limits do not make the study worthless. They do mean its results should be described as early evidence about a specific kind of writing with AI, not as a verdict on all ChatGPT use.

## What They Found

The results were striking, and they are the reason the study deserves attention rather than dismissal. Across the three sessions, brain connectivity scaled down with the amount of outside help. The brain-only group showed the strongest and most widely distributed networks of coordinated brain activity while writing. The search-engine group sat in the middle. The ChatGPT group showed the weakest connectivity overall, across the alpha, beta, theta, and delta frequency bands the researchers examined. In the alpha band, the researchers counted 79 statistically significant directional connections favouring the brain-only group, against 42 favouring the LLM group. One analysis reported connectivity up to 55 percent lower in the AI group than in the brain-only group. The paper's abstract summarises the pattern plainly: "Cognitive activity scaled down in relation to external tool use."

The second finding was about memory — and it was the one that shocked most readers. Right after finishing an essay, participants were asked to quote a single sentence from it. In the first session, 15 of the 18 ChatGPT users could not quote anything at all, and none of the 18 could quote a sentence correctly. That is the origin of the widely repeated figure that 83 percent of the ChatGPT group could not reproduce a line of their own essay. In the other two groups, only two or three people out of 18 had the same difficulty. The gap narrowed in later sessions — in session two, four ChatGPT users could not quote correctly, against two in each other group; in session three, six against two — and the authors themselves noted that the question may have caught people off guard the first time. But the first-session result is real, and it is easy to understand why it alarmed people: the participants had just "written" an essay they could not remember writing.

The third finding was the one the authors considered most important. In the fourth session, when the groups switched, the people who had spent three sessions leaning on ChatGPT and now had to write unaided still showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity — what the paper describes as under-engagement. The debt, in other words, lingered. By contrast, people who had started in the brain-only group and then used ChatGPT showed higher memory recall and brain activation patterns similar to the search-engine users. The authors also found that self-reported ownership of the essays — the feeling that the essay was truly yours — was lowest in the LLM group and highest in the brain-only group, and that the essays produced with ChatGPT were more similar to one another within the group, as if the model had flattened out individual voices.

Taken together, the paper's own summary is sobering: "Over four months, LLM users consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels." That sentence is genuine, and it is the seed of truth inside the viral claim. But it is also a sentence about a specific task, measured in specific ways, in a small group of people. The leap from that sentence to "ChatGPT makes you dumber" is where the claim goes wrong.

## What the Study Did Not Show

Here is the part the viral posts left out. The study did not measure intelligence. Nobody took an IQ test. Nobody was tested on memory, attention, or problem-solving outside the essay task. The EEG results show less coordinated brain activity while writing with ChatGPT — which is, on reflection, close to what you would expect when a machine is doing much of the writing. Lower connectivity during a task you have largely handed to a machine is not the same as a damaged brain, any more than a quieter engine means a broken car when you are coasting downhill.

The study also did not show permanent change. Four sessions over a few months is not a measure of long-term effects, whatever the word "accumulation" in the title suggests. No structural brain imaging was performed, so nothing was shown about the physical structure of anyone's brain. And the crossover result, while suggestive, involved only 18 people in the fourth session — a very small group on which to hang a claim about lasting harm.

The sample itself was narrow: 54 people, aged 18 to 39, drawn mostly from universities around Boston. That is a reasonable group for a first experiment, but it cannot support sweeping claims about hundreds of millions of ChatGPT users of all ages, in all countries, using the tool for every imaginable purpose. The authors themselves listed the small number of participants from a few nearby universities as a limitation of the work.

And the study was a preprint. As of late 2026 it had been revised — the December 2025 version runs to 216 pages — but it had not gone through peer review when it went viral, and independent experts had not yet had the chance to test whether the effects hold up. Preprints are a normal and valuable part of science, but a single preprint is the beginning of a conversation, not the end of one.

None of this means the study should be ignored. It means it should be described accurately: a small, preliminary experiment found that writing essays with ChatGPT reduced brain engagement during the task, weakened memory of the finished text, and left a residue of under-engagement when the tool was taken away. That is a real finding about a real risk. It is not evidence that ChatGPT causes brain damage, lowers IQ, or makes its users dumber in general. The researchers said so themselves, in public, in writing — and the fact that their correction was far less widely shared than the original scare is itself part of the story.

## The Classroom Test: Answers Versus Hints

If the MIT study is the most famous evidence in this debate, it is not the strongest. That distinction belongs to a field experiment published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2025, by Bastani and colleagues, which tested ChatGPT in real classrooms with about 1,000 high school mathematics students in Turkey.

Each class was given one tool for its practice sessions: textbooks only, GPT-4 behind an interface like standard ChatGPT (called GPT Base), or the same GPT-4 instructed to guide students step by step with hints, without giving away the answer (called GPT Tutor). After the practice sessions, every student took an exam on similar problems with no tools allowed.

During practice, the AI helped a lot. On a 100-point scale, the textbook-only classes averaged 28.4, the GPT Base classes 42.1, and the GPT Tutor classes 64.5 — improvements of 48 percent and 127 percent over textbooks alone. But on the exam without tools, the picture flipped. The textbook-only classes averaged 32.1. The GPT Base classes scored 26.7 — 17 percent worse than the students who had never had AI at all. The GPT Tutor classes scored 31.7, statistically no different from the textbook group. In other words, the same AI produced harm or no harm depending entirely on how it was used: as an answer machine, it cost students learning; as a tutor that made them work, it cost them nothing.

The researchers also read the messages students sent to the AI, and the pattern was revealing. The single most common first message in the GPT Base classes was "What is the answer?" In the first session, 56 percent of first messages to GPT Base simply restated the question or asked for the answer, and across all problems in that session the share rose to 67 percent. With the tutor version, the share started at 42 percent and fell to 37 percent. Faced with a tutor that would not hand over the answer, students moved toward trying the problem and asking for help instead. The researchers checked whether GPT Base's frequent maths mistakes explained the exam drop, and concluded that being misled by wrong answers mattered less than not engaging with the problem: students had used the AI as a crutch.

The students' own sense of the experience was also misleading. The GPT Base students did worse on the exam but did not feel they had learned less. The GPT Tutor students scored the same as the textbook group yet felt they had done better. How easy studying felt said little about how much was learned — a warning that applies well beyond this one experiment. Fluency is not learning, and a smooth study session can be an empty one.

This experiment matters for the claim about ChatGPT making people dumber because it is peer-reviewed, randomised, large, and conducted in real classrooms rather than a lab — and because its message is more precise than the viral headline. It is not the AI that costs you learning. It is handing the AI the thinking.

## What the Wider Research Says

The MIT and Bastani studies are the two most cited pieces of evidence, but they are not the only ones, and the wider research points in the same direction: the risk is real, specific, and tied to how the tool is used.

A 2025 survey by Lee and colleagues at Microsoft Research, presented at the CHI conference, asked 319 knowledge workers who used generative AI at least weekly to describe 936 real examples of using it at work. The pattern was that higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence was associated with more. AI shifted people's thinking toward verifying, integrating, and overseeing answers rather than generating them — a shift that is fine when the oversight is real, and dangerous when it becomes a rubber stamp.

A 2025 survey study by Gerlich, published in the journal Societies, found among 666 participants that frequent AI tool use was significantly and negatively correlated with critical-thinking scores, with the link running through cognitive offloading — the habit of handing mental work to an external tool. Younger participants depended on AI more and scored lower. Like all surveys, this one shows correlation rather than proof of cause, and self-reported use is a rough measure. But it points the same way as the experiments.

A randomised study by Fan and colleagues, published in the British Journal of Educational Technology in 2025, assigned 117 university students to a writing task with ChatGPT, a human expert, writing analytics software, or no additional tool — a design that lets researchers compare AI help against human help directly, rather than against nothing at all.

And the mechanism behind all of this is one of the oldest findings in memory research. Psychologists call it the generation effect: what you produce yourself is remembered better than what you only read. Bertsch and colleagues pooled 445 effect sizes from 86 studies in 2007 and found that generating beat reading by about 0.40 of a standard deviation. In experiments by Kornell, Hays, and Bjork in 2009, people who tried to answer a question before seeing the answer later remembered more than people who read the question and answer together — even though their attempts failed. Trying, and failing, is part of learning. Getting the answer first takes away the chance to try.

That is also why the concept of cognitive offloading, defined by Risko and Gilbert in 2016 as using the outside world to reduce the mental demand of a task, is not itself a villain. We offload all the time — setting a phone reminder, tilting our heads to read a rotated image, writing a shopping list — and it is usually sensible. The trouble starts when the work you hand over is the very thing you meant to learn. A calculator does not make you worse at arithmetic if you already know arithmetic; it makes you worse at learning arithmetic if you use it instead of learning. ChatGPT is the same kind of bargain, at a much larger scale.

It is worth noting, too, that the anxiety about AI and the mind is not limited to ChatGPT. The same questions are being asked about every AI system that now sits between people and their thinking — which is why the debate over whether AI will replace human work, covered in our earlier fact-check <a href="/blog/ai-replace-programmers-claim-fact-check/">Will AI Replace All Programmers?</a>, keeps returning to the same theme: the tools are powerful, the effects depend on how they are used, and the boldest claims about them usually outrun the evidence. The same caution applies to fears about devices listening in, examined in <a href="/blog/is-your-phone-listening-to-you-fact-check/">Is Your Phone Secretly Listening to You for Ads?</a>, and to the gap between what a device claims to measure and what it actually measures, examined in <a href="/blog/do-smartwatches-measure-blood-pressure-accurately-fact-check/">Do Smartwatches Measure Blood Pressure Accurately?</a>. In each case, the technology is real, the anxiety is understandable, and the evidence is more complicated than the viral version.

## The Verdict

The claim that using ChatGPT makes you dumber is misleading. It takes a genuine finding and stretches it into something the evidence does not support.

What is true: in a small MIT experiment, people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker brain connectivity while writing than people who wrote with a search engine or with no tools, and most of them could not quote a sentence from the essay they had just produced. In a large classroom experiment, students who used ChatGPT as an answer machine scored 17 percent lower on an exam taken without it. Surveys link heavy AI reliance with less critical thinking. The mechanism is well understood: when a tool does the thinking, you stop practising it, and practice is what builds memory and skill.

What is not true: that the MIT study proved a 47 percent loss of brain power, brain damage, or a general decline in intelligence. No such figure exists in the paper. No IQ test was given. No structural brain change was found. The study was a small preprint about one task, and its own authors publicly asked journalists not to describe it with words like "dumb" and "brain rot." The strongest experiment in this area shows that the harm disappears when the AI is set up to give hints instead of answers — which is the opposite of what you would expect if ChatGPT itself were simply making people dumber.

The honest summary is this: ChatGPT does not make you dumber. Using it as a substitute for thinking can make you worse at the thinking you stop doing. That distinction is the whole story — and it is also the useful part, because it tells you exactly what to change.

## How to Use ChatGPT Without Dulling Your Thinking

The research does not point to abandoning ChatGPT. It points to using it in a different order. Three habits follow directly from the studies above, and none of them requires using the tool less — only using it differently.

First, try before you ask. Spend a minute answering on your own before opening the chatbot — write what you know on a blank page, or just the first line of a solution, even if you are wrong. The generation effect means that the attempt itself helps the eventual answer stick, and the Bastani experiment showed that students who engaged first learned more. A wrong first try is not wasted effort; it is the effort that makes the correction memorable.

Second, ask for a hint, not the answer. Tell the chatbot what you are stuck on and ask it to question you, or to explain one step, rather than to produce the finished product. The GPT Tutor in the classroom experiment did exactly this, and its students lost nothing on the exam. If you are writing, ask for feedback on a draft you wrote yourself rather than asking for a draft from scratch — the MIT study's ownership finding suggests that text you did not produce will not feel, or stick, like yours.

Third, close the window and recall. After you have read an answer, close it and write down three lines: what you asked, the core of the answer, and what you did not know before. Then come back to it a few days later and try to recall it again. The MIT participants who could not quote their own essays had never really engaged with the words; a short act of recall is the cheapest way to make sure you have.

One more habit is worth adding, and it is the simplest of all: be suspicious when studying feels easy. The students in the Bastani experiment who learned the least were the ones who felt they had learned just as much. Ease is a feature of a good tool, but it is a poor measure of learning. If an answer arrives without any friction at all, ask yourself whether you have actually understood it — or merely received it.

## The Verdict, Restated

So, does using ChatGPT make you dumber? No — not in the way the viral claim says, and not on the evidence we have. What the evidence shows is that letting an AI do your thinking for you means you practise that thinking less, remember less of what it produces, and can carry that under-engagement into the next task. That is a real cost, and it is one that shows up in brain scans, in exam scores, and in the simple test of whether you can repeat a sentence you supposedly wrote five minutes ago.

But it is a cost with a fix that is entirely in your hands. The same research that documents the risk also shows the way around it: try first, take hints instead of answers, and recall what you learned after the screen is closed. The tool does not decide whether you get dumber. You do — one prompt at a time.
