Somewhere in the summer of 2025, the planet you are standing on finished its daily spin a little early. Not early enough for you to notice — not early enough for your alarm clock, your commute, or your dinner to care — but early enough for the people who guard the world's time to sit up and take notice. On 9 July 2025, Earth completed one full rotation roughly 1.3 milliseconds faster than the standard 24 hours. Two weeks later it did it again. And on 5 August it did it a third time. None of those days quite matched the record set on 5 July 2024, when the planet shaved 1.66 milliseconds off the day — the shortest day measured since atomic clocks began keeping score in the 1960s.

The headlines that followed were, as headlines tend to be, louder than the facts. "Shortest day in history!" some declared. "Earth is spinning out of control!" others implied. Social media did what social media does, and within days the story had acquired the usual barnacles: claims that the days are collapsing, that time itself is speeding up, that something is deeply wrong with the planet. So let us slow down — by about a millisecond and a half — and check what is actually happening, why it is happening, and whether any of it matters to anyone who is not a timekeeper.

The short version is this: yes, Earth really is spinning faster than it has in decades. The measurements are real, the trend since 2020 is real, and the scientists who watch the planet's rotation are genuinely puzzled by part of it. But the effect is measured in thousandths of a second, the long-term direction of the planet's spin is still a slowdown, and the most dramatic consequence so far has not been felt by any human being at all — it has been felt by the world's timekeeping system, which in October 2026 voted to abolish the leap second rather than face the prospect of deleting one for the first time in history.

## The shortest days ever measured

To understand the story, start with what a day actually is. A mean solar day — the time it takes Earth to rotate once relative to the Sun — is defined as exactly 86,400 seconds. That is the day your watch assumes. But Earth is not a metronome. Its rotation wobbles and drifts by tiny amounts, so any given real day can be a fraction of a millisecond longer or shorter than 86,400 seconds. The body that keeps score is the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service, or IERS, which measures the planet's spin against distant quasars and folds in data from more than 450 cesium and hydrogen maser atomic clocks. The difference between the ideal day and the real one is called the length of day, and it is usually measured in milliseconds.

For most of the era of precise measurement, the length of day ran slightly long. Earth was slowing down, and timekeepers periodically added a leap second to the world's clocks to keep atomic time in step with the turning planet. Then, around 2020, something flipped. The planet started turning faster, and it has been setting short-day records ever since. On 19 July 2020, Earth completed its rotation about 1.47 milliseconds early — a record at the time. The summer of 2024 broke it: on 5 July 2024, the shortfall reached 1.66 milliseconds, the shortest day since precise atomic measurements began.

The summer of 2025 produced a cluster of near-records. According to IERS data reported by timeanddate.com, the BBC's Sky at Night magazine, and others, 9 July 2025 came in about 1.3 milliseconds short, 10 July about 1.36 milliseconds short, 22 July about 1.34 milliseconds short, and 5 August about 1.25 milliseconds short. The exact figures vary slightly between sources, because some numbers were predictions issued before the day and others were measurements finalised afterwards — but every version of the story agrees on the shape of it: a run of unusually short days, none quite beating the 2024 record, all of them far outside the range anyone expected a decade ago.

A word of caution about the phrase "shortest day in history," which appeared in more than one headline. The record covers only the era of atomic timekeeping, which began in the 1960s. Geologically speaking, the opposite is true: Earth used to spin considerably faster, and days were much shorter in the distant past. Even within the atomic era, the ranking comes with a footnote. National Geographic noted that IERS data also show very short days in June and August 2022 and in June 2024, so the precise ordering of the fastest days depends on which measurements you count. The 1.66-millisecond figure for 5 July 2024 is the widely reported record, but the honest version of the headline is "shortest day since we started measuring days this precisely" — which is still a remarkable thing to be able to say.

## How do you measure a millisecond?

It is worth pausing on how anyone knows any of this, because the claim rests entirely on measurement, and the measurement is extraordinary. The IERS tracks Earth's rotation with a technique called very-long-baseline interferometry, in which radio telescopes on different continents observe the same distant quasars — objects billions of light-years away that are effectively fixed points in the sky — and compare the tiny differences in when the signals arrive. Combined with satellite laser ranging, GPS tracking, and the network of more than 450 atomic clocks that feed into the IERS Bulletin A, the system can pin down the length of a day to a fraction of a millisecond.

That precision matters because almost nothing in modern life runs on the Sun's time any more. Your phone, your bank, the power grid, air-traffic control, and the GPS satellites overhead all run on atomic time — Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC — which ticks at a perfectly steady rate. Earth's rotation, by contrast, is kept as a separate quantity called UT1, astronomical time. The two are held within 0.9 seconds of each other. When the gap approaches that limit, the IERS announces a leap second: an extra second inserted at the end of June or December, in which the clock reads 23:59:60 before rolling over to midnight.

Since the system began in 1972, leap seconds have been added 27 times. The most recent was on 31 December 2016. No leap second has been added since — not because the system was fixed, but because the planet stopped needing one. Earth started running fast, and a planet that runs fast does not need extra seconds added to its clocks. It needs the opposite.

That opposite has a name — a negative leap second — and it has never happened. If Earth runs fast, UT1 pulls ahead of UTC, and the only way to bring them back together is to remove a second: a minute that jumps from 23:59:58 straight to 00:00:00, deleting the 59th second entirely. No computer system has ever had to handle one. Timekeeping experts have warned for years that deleting a second would be far more dangerous than adding one, because software is built on the assumption that every minute has 60 seconds. A deleted second could, in principle, crash systems that have never been tested against it.

## Why is the Moon speeding the planet up?

Part of the speed-up has a known cause, and it involves the Moon. The Moon's gravity tugs on Earth, raising tides in the oceans and deforming the solid planet itself. That deformation acts as a brake: it slowly leaches momentum from Earth's rotation, which is why the long-term trend is a slowdown of roughly two milliseconds per century. But the braking is not constant. When the Moon sits far north or south of the equator, its pull on Earth's equatorial bulge is weaker, the braking eases, and the planet spins a little faster.

That is exactly the geometry behind the short days of 2024 and 2025. As Graham Jones of timeanddate.com explained, the record-short days clustered on dates when the Moon was at its greatest distance from the equator. The Moon's position is predictable, so the short days were predictable too — timeanddate.com flagged 9 July, 22 July, and 5 August 2025 in advance, and the planet delivered on schedule.

But the Moon's position explains the timing of the short days, not the trend. The summer clusters of 2024 and 2025 sit on top of a deeper shift that began around 2020, and the short days now are roughly half a millisecond shorter than comparable days before 2020. Something else has been pushing the planet faster, and scientists are still arguing about what it is. That is the genuinely mysterious part of the story, and it is where the honest answer becomes "we don't fully know."

## The core mystery

The leading suspect for the deeper speed-up lives 2,900 kilometres beneath your feet. Earth's liquid outer core has been decelerating for about 50 years, and because angular momentum has to go somewhere, the solid Earth above it has been spinning a little faster to compensate. That exchange of spin between the core and the mantle is the mechanism behind the most widely cited study of the phenomenon: a 2024 paper in the journal Nature by Duncan Agnew, a geophysicist at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, who modelled the planet's rotation and projected that, on current trends, the first negative leap second would be needed around 2029.

Agnew is candid about how much remains unknown. "We don't know why, or what the core might do in the future," he told National Geographic. The core's behaviour is inferred, not observed directly, and its future is anyone's guess. The speed-up could continue for years, or the core could reverse and hand the momentum back, and the planet would slow down again. Nobody can say which it will be, because nobody has ever watched this particular exchange play out in real time before.

There is also a counterweight in the other direction, and it comes from climate change. As polar ice melts and groundwater is pumped and redistributed, mass moves toward the equator, and a planet with more mass near its equator spins more slowly — the same physics as a figure skater extending her arms. NASA-funded studies have found that melting ice and shifting groundwater have been slowing Earth's rotation by about 1.33 milliseconds per century since 2000. Agnew's study calculated that without that braking effect from melted ice, the negative leap second would already have been needed by 2026 — three years earlier than his 2029 projection. In other words, climate change is currently holding back the clock on the clock problem, delaying by a few years a reckoning that the core's slowdown would otherwise have brought sooner.

That tangle of causes — the Moon's position, the core's deceleration, the redistribution of water — is why scientists describe the acceleration as real but not fully explained. The measurements are solid. The mechanism is partly understood. The forecast is not.

## The long view: Earth has been slowing down for eons

Here is the part the viral versions of this story almost always leave out. The long-term direction of Earth's rotation is not a speed-up. It is a slowdown, and it has been going on for a very long time. The Moon's tides have been braking the planet for billions of years, lengthening the day by roughly two milliseconds per century in the recent era. The days of the distant past were far shorter than 24 hours, and the days of the distant future will be longer. Against that background, the acceleration since 2020 is a ripple, not a reversal. It is a decades-long wobble superimposed on a billion-year slowdown.

That perspective matters because it is the difference between a curiosity and a crisis. A planet that was permanently and rapidly speeding up would be a genuine mystery with unknown consequences. A planet whose rotation wobbles by a millisecond or two as its core, oceans, atmosphere, and ice sheets trade momentum is a planet behaving exactly like the complicated, fluid, layered object it is. The surprise is not that Earth's rotation varies. The surprise is that it varied so little for so long that we built a civilisation on the assumption that a day is exactly 86,400 seconds, and then discovered that it is not.

The wobble is not new, either. Earth's rotation has always fluctuated by milliseconds — with the seasons, with El Niño, with earthquakes that redistribute mass, with the slow rebound of continents still rising after the last ice age. What is new is the direction and the persistence: six years of running fast, when the previous half-century ran slow. That is what has the timekeepers' attention, and it is what makes this a real story rather than a measurement artefact.

## The leap second's strange afterlife

The most consequential part of this story has nothing to do with astronomy and everything to do with bureaucracy. For half a century, the leap second was the humble mechanism that kept atomic time and Earth time in step: 27 seconds added between 1972 and 2016, each one announced six months in advance by the IERS, each one inserted at the end of June or December. The system worked, but it was unloved. Positive leap seconds have caused real outages — computer systems that assume every minute has 60 seconds have crashed when handed a 61-second minute — and the prospect of a negative leap second, a minute with only 59 seconds, alarmed engineers far more. A 2025 workshop held by the BIPM and the IERS estimated the probability of needing a negative leap second at 30 per cent by 2035, and Judah Levine, a physicist at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, put the likelihood at roughly 30 per cent within the next decade.

Rather than attempt a subtraction nobody had ever tested, the world's timekeepers chose a different path. In 2022, the General Conference on Weights and Measures had already voted to phase out leap seconds by 2035. Then, in October 2026, its 28th meeting in Versailles went further: the delegates voted to retire the leap second altogether. From 20 May 2027, UTC will run continuously, with no more leap seconds added or subtracted, and the allowed gap between atomic time and Earth's rotation will be widened to one hour. In practice, that means the clocks will be allowed to drift slowly away from the Sun's position, and the difference will be somebody else's problem in the distant future — by some estimates, centuries from now.

The IERS confirmed the immediate consequence in Bulletin C 72, issued in July 2026: no leap second would be added at the end of December 2026. The era of the leap second is ending not with a bang but with a vote, and the reason it ended when it did is sitting in the data above. The planet's unexpected acceleration is what turned a slow, scheduled phase-out into an urgent one. The fear was not another slow year. It was the opposite: a planet spinning fast enough to need a second removed from the clock, something no system has ever had to survive.

## Would you notice?

No. That is the honest answer, and it is worth stating plainly, because the gap between the headlines and the lived experience is the whole story here. A day that is 1.66 milliseconds short is a day that is 0.0000019 per cent shorter than usual. Your body cannot detect it. Your watch cannot detect it. You would need about 600 such days in a row — under two years of them — to lose a single second of sleep. The reason this story matters is not that your days are getting shorter in any sense you could feel. It is that the systems you depend on can feel it, and they care enormously.

GPS is the clearest example. A GPS receiver works out its position by timing radio signals from satellites, and a timing error of a single millisecond translates into a position error of about 300 kilometres. The system corrects for Earth's rotation continuously, using the IERS data, and it has to: the satellites and the ground stations must agree on where the planet is pointing at any given instant, to the millisecond. Financial markets are another. High-frequency trading timestamps transactions to the microsecond, and the exchanges depend on a shared, stable time standard. Power grids, mobile networks, and air-traffic control all lean on the same foundation. None of these systems would fail because Earth ran 1.66 milliseconds fast on a Tuesday in July. But all of them are built on the assumption that the difference between atomic time and Earth time stays small and predictable, and the acceleration since 2020 has made that assumption slightly less safe than it was.

There is a second, quieter reason the story matters: it is a reminder that the most stable-seeming facts about the world are measurements, not certainties. A day is 24 hours because we defined it that way, and then we built instruments precise enough to discover that the planet disagrees with our definition by a thousandth of a second. That is not a failure of the definition. It is a triumph of the instruments. The correct response to a planet that will not sit still is not alarm but better measurement — and, as the October 2026 vote showed, a willingness to redesign the system when the measurements say the old design no longer fits.

## The Verdict

So, is Earth spinning faster? The evidence says yes — with qualifications that matter more than the yes itself.

The claim is **Mostly True**. The planet has been spinning measurably faster since about 2020, and the IERS data behind that statement is among the most precise measurement in all of science: the shortest day on record, 5 July 2024, came in 1.66 milliseconds under 24 hours, and the summer of 2025 produced a cluster of days between 1.25 and 1.36 milliseconds short. The Moon's position explains the timing of those short days, the core's deceleration is the leading explanation for the deeper trend, and the acceleration was real enough to push the world's timekeepers to abolish the leap second in October 2026 rather than risk the first negative leap second in history.

But the viral framing of the claim overreaches in three ways. First, the effect is about a millisecond and a half — utterly imperceptible to any human being, and far too small to change anything about daily life. Second, "shortest day in history" is only true if "history" means "since atomic clocks started measuring," and even within that era the exact ranking is debated. Third, and most importantly, the long-term trend is still a slowdown: the Moon's tides are braking Earth by roughly two milliseconds per century, and the current speed-up is a wobble on top of a billion-year deceleration, not a reversal of it. Climate change is even pushing in the other direction, with melting ice slowing the planet by about 1.33 milliseconds per century since 2000.

The honest summary is this: Earth is spinning faster than it has in decades, scientists know some of the reasons and not others, the consequences are real but confined to the machinery of timekeeping, and the planet will, in all likelihood, slow down again. The days are getting shorter by a millisecond. Your life is not. The clocks, though, will never be quite the same.
