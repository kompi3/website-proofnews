It sounds like a riddle a child would ask on a long car ride: can fish drown? The animal spends its entire life underwater, breathing the stuff, so the question feels almost like a trick — the way asking whether a bird can fall out of the sky feels like a trick. And yet the question keeps coming back, year after year, typed into search engines millions of times, argued about in comment sections, and answered with total confidence by people who have never looked into it. The confident answers split neatly in two. One camp says no, obviously not, because fish breathe water. The other says yes, obviously, because everything that needs oxygen can run out of it.

Both camps are half right, and the half they get wrong is the interesting part. Fish do not drown the way a human drowns. They have no lungs to fill with water, no airway to choke, no cough reflex that fails at the worst moment. But fish absolutely can die underwater because they cannot get enough oxygen — and scientists who study them will tell you, in careful language, that this is close enough to drowning that the distinction is mostly a matter of terminology. A fish surrounded by water, in the middle of the ocean, can suffocate. It happens in warming lakes every summer. It happens in fishing nets. It happens to some of the ocean's fastest and most powerful predators if they simply stop swimming. And every year, it happens across thousands of square miles of sea where the water itself has run out of air.

This is the story of how that happens — what fish actually breathe, why water can betray them, and which fish are one stopped fin-beat away from suffocating in the only home they have ever known.

## The Short Answer

Yes — but with a caveat that matters. A fish can die of oxygen deprivation while fully submerged, which is the outcome the question is really asking about. What it cannot do is drown in the strict, textbook sense, because drowning means water entering lungs and blocking the intake of air, and fish have neither lungs nor air to block. The precise word for what kills a fish underwater is suffocation, or hypoxia: the water around it holds too little dissolved oxygen, or something stops that oxygen from reaching its blood.

That is not a pedantic footnote. It is the whole story. Fish do not breathe water. They breathe the oxygen dissolved in water, and that is a much more fragile arrangement than it sounds. Air is about 21 percent oxygen. Water, even at its coldest and most saturated, holds only a tiny fraction of that — a few milligrams of oxygen per liter, a concentration so low that fish had to evolve one of the most efficient gas-exchange organs in the animal kingdom just to survive on it. Take away a little of that oxygen, or damage the organ that harvests it, or stop the water flowing across it, and the fish is in exactly the same trouble as a swimmer who cannot reach the surface: surrounded by the stuff it needs, and unable to use it.

## What "Drowning" Actually Means

To settle the question honestly, it helps to start with the dictionary rather than with the fish. Drowning, in the ordinary sense, is dying because liquid prevents the body from getting oxygen. For a human, the mechanism is specific: water gets into the lungs, the tiny air sacs called alveoli collapse or fill, oxygen stops crossing into the blood, carbon dioxide builds up, and the organs fail. The water is the obstacle. It is in the way of the oxygen.

Apply that definition to a fish and the logic wobbles. A fish has no alveoli and no lungs to flood. Water is not an obstacle to its breathing; water is the medium its breathing depends on. As the Collins dictionary definition puts it, drowning is "to die or kill by immersion in liquid" — and by that strict wording, a fish cannot drown, because it is already immersed in liquid by design. Researchers who are asked this question tend to give the same careful answer: no, not technically, but also yes, in every way that matters. Marco Vindas, a researcher at the Norwegian University of Life Sciences, put it plainly when the question was put to him: fish can suffocate if there is too little oxygen in the water, and "it is more correct to say that fish suffocate, but to some extent it is more or less the same thing."

Frances Withrow, a marine scientist at Oceana, gave the same answer from the other side of the Atlantic. "Marine animals also need oxygen to live," she told Live Science. "It's just that they live off of dissolved oxygen, while we get oxygen from the air." The difference between a drowning human and a suffocating fish is the plumbing, not the outcome. Both die because oxygen stops reaching their tissues. One dies because water gets in the way; the other dies because the oxygen runs out.

That is why the verdict on this claim is Mostly True rather than a clean yes or no. The question, as people actually mean it, is whether a fish can die underwater from a failure to breathe. The answer to that is an unambiguous yes. The only thing wrong is the word "drown" — and scientists themselves use it loosely, because the alternative requires a paragraph of explanation every time.

## How Fish Breathe: The Gill Machine

Understanding why a fish can suffocate underwater means understanding what its gills actually do, and the organ is far more remarkable than the simple picture most of us carry from school. A fish does not just open its mouth and let water wash over some feathery bits. It runs a precision gas-exchange machine, one that extracts oxygen from a medium that holds roughly thirty times less of it than air does, and it does so continuously, for its entire life, without ever taking a break.

The process starts at the mouth. The fish draws water in, then pumps it across the gills — two sets of arches on either side of the head, each carrying rows of filaments, and each filament carrying thousands of microscopic plates called lamellae. The lamellae are the business end of the organ: wafer-thin, packed with capillaries, and arranged so that together they offer an enormous surface area, many times the surface area of the fish's whole body. As water flows across them, dissolved oxygen diffuses through the lamellar membrane into the blood, while waste carbon dioxide diffuses out into the passing water. The spent water exits through the gill openings, and the cycle repeats — thousands of times a day, every day, for as long as the fish lives.

The clever part is the direction of flow. Blood inside the lamellae flows in the opposite direction to the water passing over them, a arrangement called countercurrent exchange. Because the two streams move against each other, the concentration gradient that drives oxygen into the blood stays favorable along the whole length of the lamella, instead of equalizing halfway and stalling. It is the same engineering trick used in industrial heat exchangers, and it is why gills can pull oxygen out of water that holds only a few milligrams per liter. Without it, fish would need gills the size of sails.

But the efficiency of the machine is also its vulnerability. It only works if three things are true at the same time: the water must contain enough dissolved oxygen, the water must keep flowing across the gills, and the gills themselves must be healthy. Break any one of those three conditions and the machine stops, even though the fish is still underwater. That is the whole story of how fish "drown" — it is a story of a machine with three failure points, and nature, fishing gear, and warming seas keep finding all three.

It is worth pausing here to note how strange fish senses and inner lives are in general, because the breathing question is not the only one where intuition fails. Researchers have spent two decades arguing over whether fish even experience pain the way we do — a question we examined in our fact-check, <a href="/blog/do-fish-feel-pain-fact-check/">Do Fish Feel Pain? What 20 Years of Science Actually Found</a> — and the answer there, too, turned out to be more complicated than either side of the argument wanted. Fish are not simple machines. They are animals with sophisticated biology, and their breathing is a good example of just how sophisticated — and how fragile — that biology can be.

## Five Ways a Fish Can Suffocate Underwater

If the gill machine has three requirements — oxygen in the water, water flowing, healthy gills — then there are five common ways those requirements fail in the real world. None of them involves a fish inhaling water into lungs it does not have. All of them end the same way.

**1. The water runs out of oxygen.** This is the most common cause, and the most consequential. Dissolved oxygen is not a permanent property of water; it rises and falls with temperature, sunlight, and biology. Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water — a lot more, as the table later in this article shows — so summer is the dangerous season. When the water warms, its oxygen ceiling drops, while at the same time every living thing in it — fish, bacteria, algae — breathes faster and consumes more. The U.S. Geological Survey describes exactly this squeeze: in stagnant water with a lot of rotting organic material, especially in summer, dissolved-oxygen levels fall to a seasonal low, and "conditions may become especially serious during a period of hot, calm weather, resulting in the loss of many fish." Anyone who has read a local news story about a summertime fish kill in a lake has read about this failure mode. The fish did not get sick. The water ran out of air.

**2. Algal blooms strip the oxygen away.** The most dramatic version of the first failure is eutrophication: excess nutrients, usually nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer runoff and sewage, feed an explosion of algae. The bloom itself is not the killer. The killing happens when the algae die and sink, and the bacteria that decompose them consume enormous quantities of oxygen in the process. Plankton blooms can use up the oxygen in an area so fast that the water cannot replenish it, and the fish in the area suffocate. When this happens on a large scale in coastal waters, the result is a "dead zone" — a stretch of sea where the bottom water holds too little oxygen to support most life. The largest dead zone in the United States forms every summer in the northern Gulf of Mexico, west of the Mississippi Delta. We will come back to it, because its size tells the story better than any single fish kill.

**3. The gills get damaged or blocked.** Even in water full of oxygen, a fish still needs working gills to harvest it. Fishing gear is a common culprit: hooks can tear gill tissue, and nets can abrade it. Disease is another. Pathogens, mainly bacteria, can attach to the gills and block them so they cannot filter oxygen from the water, or degrade them to the point where they no longer work. After oil spills, fish populations often crash not only because of toxicity, but because oil coats and compromises gill function. A fish with ruined gills is in the same position as a person breathing through a clogged filter: the air is there, and the body cannot use it.

**4. The water stops moving.** Many fish actively pump water over their gills with muscles in their mouth and gill covers, a method called buccal pumping, and they can do it while sitting perfectly still on the bottom. But a large and important group of fish cannot. They rely on ram ventilation: they swim forward with their mouths open, and the motion itself forces water across the gills. Stop swimming, stop breathing. If such a fish gets trapped — in a net, in a shrinking pool, against a barrier — it suffocates even though the water around it is perfectly well oxygenated. This is why some sharks die so quickly when they are caught and held still, a subject we return to below.

**5. The fish gets stuck where the water is wrong.** A fish can also suffocate if it ends up trapped in a river or a pool with too little water to reach the oxygenated flow it needs, or in a drying lake where the remaining water is warm, shallow, and stagnant. Every spring and autumn, for example, Lake Cerknica in Slovenia dries up, and fishermen have to transport the fish — including pike gasping at the surface — to a nearby pool of water. The fish are not out of the water. They are in water that has stopped being breathable.

## The Sharks That Must Never Stop Swimming

No animal illustrates the fragility of fish breathing better than the shark — and no group of sharks illustrates it better than the obligate ram ventilators, the species that cannot pump water over their own gills at all. For them, forward motion is not a choice. It is respiration.

The list is longer than most people expect. It includes the great white shark, the whale shark, mako sharks, hammerheads, thresher sharks, the porbeagle, the salmon shark, and the sandbar shark. The entire family Lamnidae — great whites, makos, porbeagles, salmon sharks — are obligate ram ventilators. So are Atlantic bluefin tuna and Atlantic mackerel, which switch to ram ventilation once they reach about two body lengths per second. For all of them, stopping means suffocating. A great white that stops swimming does not rest. It starts to die.

That is why the shark-fin trade is lethal in a way that goes beyond the obvious cruelty. A shark thrown back into the sea with its fins removed cannot swim, and a shark that cannot swim cannot breathe. As one wildlife guide puts it plainly, a finless shark is unable to swim and will therefore suffocate. The same vulnerability explains why hammerhead sharks die at such high rates when they are caught on fishing lines: they are exquisitely sensitive to any interruption of water flow over their gills, and even a short period of restraint can be fatal.

Scientists have measured just how hard these animals work to recover from an interruption. In one study of 29 ram-ventilating sharks from 10 species, every single animal swam at roughly twice its normal cruising speed immediately after being caught and released, and only gradually settled back to baseline over about six hours. That post-capture burst is not panic. It is a survival response — the shark is forcing water over its gills as hard as it can to pay back an oxygen debt.

Not all sharks are trapped by this design. Nurse sharks, bull sharks, tiger sharks, lemon sharks, whitetip reef sharks, and carpet sharks can all breathe while stationary, because they retained the ability to pump water over their gills. A nurse shark can lie on the bottom and breathe perfectly well. A great white cannot. Evolution made a trade: ram ventilation is extremely efficient at speed, which is why the fastest sharks in the ocean use it, but it comes with a condition that can never be broken. The shark that must never stop swimming is a fish that can drown in water simply by standing still.

It is a strange thought that the ocean's most feared predator shares a vulnerability with the most delicate aquarium fish. But it is also a useful reminder that "can fish drown" is not an abstract riddle. For these animals, the answer is not just yes — it is yes, constantly, held off only by motion. And sharks, as we showed in our investigation of a very different myth — <a href="/blog/sharks-dont-get-cancer-myth-fact-check/">No, Sharks Do Get Cancer — the Myth That Built a $30 Million Supplement Industry</a> — have been the subject of more confident false claims than almost any other animal. The claim that they are invulnerable is one of them. The truth is that they are, in this one specific way, more vulnerable than almost anything else in the sea.

## The Air-Breathers Next Door

Fish are not the only animals that can drown in the sea, and the contrast with their neighbors makes the fish story clearer. Whales, dolphins, and sea turtles all breathe air, exactly the way we do. They have lungs, they must surface to fill them, and if something prevents them from surfacing, they drown in the ordinary, textbook sense of the word. A sea turtle tangled in a fishing net drowns. A dolphin trapped in a drift gill net drowns. The mechanism is identical to a human drowning: water where air should be, lungs that cannot function, oxygen cut off.

The scale of that problem is staggering. Entanglement in fishing gear kills an estimated 300,000 whales, dolphins, and porpoises every year, according to estimates from the International Whaling Commission cited by Live Science — and a large share of those deaths are drownings, animals that could not reach the surface in time. Drift gill nets, enormous walls of netting that float in the water and are not designed to target any particular species, are a major culprit. They catch whatever swims into them, and for an air-breathing animal, being held underwater is a death sentence measured in minutes.

The comparison sharpens the fish question nicely. A dolphin and a tuna can be caught in the same net, in the same water, on the same day, and die of the same ultimate cause — oxygen deprivation — by opposite mechanisms. The dolphin dies because water keeps it from the air. The tuna dies because the net keeps it from swimming, and swimming is its breathing. One drowns in the strict sense. The other suffocates. Both are dead because they could not get oxygen, and both deaths are preventable, and both are happening at industrial scale in the world's oceans right now.

There is a third category of animal that complicates the picture even further: the fish that breathe air. Not all fish rely on gills alone. Some species have evolved a second way of taking in oxygen, using organs in their mouths or in parts of their intestines, in addition to what their gills extract from the water. The most famous of them are the lungfish — six species still alive today, in rivers and lakes in Africa, Australia, and South America, survivors of a lineage that first appeared around 400 million years ago. Lungfish come to the surface to breathe, drawing air into primitive lungs that are a modified swim bladder, and the same anatomy lets them survive droughts by burrowing into mud and waiting for the water to return.

The arapaima of South America, the largest freshwater fish in the world at up to three meters long, has very small gills and a modified swim bladder that lets it take oxygen from the air. It can survive out of water for up to 24 hours. These fish blur every neat line in the argument. They are fish, they live in water, and they breathe air — which means they, too, can drown in the strict sense if they are held underwater and cannot reach the surface. The neat categories — fish breathe water, mammals breathe air — were never as neat as the school textbook made them look.

That untidiness extends to the sea itself. The immortal jellyfish, a creature so strange it can reverse its own life cycle, reminds us how little our intuitions about marine life can be trusted — a point we explored in <a href="/blog/does-the-immortal-jellyfish-live-forever-fact-check/">Does the Immortal Jellyfish Live Forever? The Tiny Animal That Can Reverse Its Own Life Cycle</a>. If an animal can be biologically immortal, it should not surprise us that another animal can suffocate in the middle of the ocean.

## The Dead Zones: When Whole Seas Run Out of Air

If the fish-kill story sounds like a local problem — a warm lake here, a trapped shark there — the dead zones show that it is a planetary one. A dead zone is a stretch of sea where the bottom water holds so little dissolved oxygen that most animal life cannot survive in it. Fish that can swim away do. Everything that cannot — shellfish, crabs, worms, the small creatures that anchor the food web — dies where it sits.

The largest dead zone in the United States forms every year in the northern Gulf of Mexico, west of the Mississippi Delta, and it is fed by the entire American Midwest. Nitrogen and phosphorus wash off farm fields and flow down the Mississippi and Atchafalaya rivers into the Gulf, where they fertilize enormous algal blooms. The algae die, sink, and decompose, and the bacteria doing the decomposing strip the oxygen from the bottom water. The depletion begins in late spring, increases through the summer, and ends in the fall. It has been measured every year since 1985, and in that time the zone has grown: in 2002 it reached more than 20,000 square kilometers, about the size of New Jersey.

The numbers in recent years tell a story of partial progress and stubborn scale. In 2025, the Gulf dead zone grew to cover 4,402 square miles of sea floor. In 2026, it measured 1,332 square miles — the second smallest ever recorded in the forty-year record, measured between July 24 and July 30 — a welcome result, but still more than a thousand square miles of sea where the water could not support most life. The zone sits in the middle of one of the most productive and valuable fisheries in the country, a fishery worth about $2.8 billion a year. The fish that feed that industry do not die in the dead zone because they are weak or sick. They die, or flee, because the water itself has become unbreathable.

The Gulf is not unique. Dead zones form wherever nutrient pollution meets warm, stratified water, and they are one of the clearest demonstrations that "can fish drown" is not a riddle but a real environmental question. The oxygen in the sea is not infinite, and it is not evenly distributed, and human activity is very good at removing it. Fertilizer runoff, sewage, warming water — all three push in the same direction, and the direction is toward less oxygen. The U.S. Geological Survey puts the mechanism in plain language: warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, and in a stratified lake the warm surface layer is too hot for cold-water fish while the deep layer holds too little oxygen for anything. The fish are squeezed from above and below, in water that looks perfectly fine from the shore.

There is a second, quieter squeeze that rarely makes the news. Growth in marine organisms is inhibited when dissolved oxygen falls below about 5 milligrams per liter. Mobile animals start leaving when it drops below 2. Burrowing animals emerge from the sediment and die if oxygen stays near 0.5 for long. Below 0.2, the sediment turns black, mats of sulfur-oxidizing bacteria spread across the sea floor, and toxic hydrogen sulfide can appear where the bottom water has gone completely anoxic. The fish do not have to die for the damage to be real. Long before the kills, there is slower growth, fewer young, and a food web quietly coming apart — all because the water holds a little less oxygen than it used to.

## What About Fish Out of Water?

The question usually runs in one direction — can a fish die in water? — but the reverse is worth a moment, because it shows how specific the gill machine is to its medium. A fish out of water does not die because it is "out of its element" in some vague sense. It dies because its breathing organ stops working the moment it leaves the water.

On land, a fish's feathery gills stick together. The lamellae, which need to be separated by flowing water to expose their enormous surface area, collapse into a clump, and the space available for gas exchange shrinks dramatically. The fish is surrounded by air that is 21 percent oxygen — vastly richer than anything it ever breathed in water — and it cannot use any of it, because its gills are built for water and water only. It suffocates in the open air, slowly, which is why researchers describe it as an unpleasant way for a fish to die and why the most humane way to kill one is a quick blow to the head.

Some fish last longer than others. The arapaima, with its air-breathing swim bladder, can survive out of water for up to 24 hours. Lungfish can survive droughts buried in mud. But for the ordinary fish — the trout, the pike, the carp — the clock runs in minutes. The gills that make life in water possible make life out of it impossible. It is the same machine, the same bargain: exquisite efficiency in one medium, total failure in the other.

That symmetry is the real answer to the riddle. A human drowns in water because human lungs are built for air. A fish suffocates in water when the water fails it, and suffocates in air because its gills are built for water. Each animal is a breathing machine tuned to one medium, and each dies when its medium stops delivering oxygen. The fish is not exempt from drowning because it lives in water. It is exposed to the same risk in a different form — and, unlike us, it cannot hold its breath, climb out, or call for help. It can only keep swimming, and hope the water keeps its side of the bargain.

## How Much Oxygen Is in the Water, Anyway?

The numbers behind this story are worth seeing side by side, because they make the fragility concrete. Fresh water in equilibrium with air holds less and less oxygen as it warms — the maximum falls by about a third between 10°C and 30°C — and the thresholds at which aquatic life starts to suffer are uncomfortably close to the levels found in real lakes and coastal seas every summer. The table below combines the saturation values from the U.S. Geological Survey's solubility tables with the biological thresholds documented by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

| Water temperature | Maximum dissolved oxygen the water can hold |
| --- | --- |
| 0 °C | 14.6 mg/L |
| 5 °C | 12.8 mg/L |
| 10 °C | 11.3 mg/L |
| 15 °C | 10.1 mg/L |
| 20 °C | 9.1 mg/L |
| 25 °C | 8.3 mg/L |
| 30 °C | 7.6 mg/L |
| 35 °C | 7.0 mg/L |

| Dissolved oxygen level | What it means for aquatic life |
| --- | --- |
| Above 8 mg/L | Typical of healthy, well-oxygenated surface water in summer |
| Below about 5 mg/L | Growth of marine organisms is inhibited |
| Below 2 mg/L | Defined as hypoxic; mobile animals leave the area |
| Near 0.5 mg/L for a prolonged period | Burrowing organisms emerge from the sediment and die |
| Below 0.2 mg/L | Sediment turns black; toxic hydrogen sulfide may appear |
| Below 1 mg/L | Described by the EPA as hypoxic and usually unable to support much life |

Read the two halves of that table together and the summer fish kill stops looking mysterious. A lake at 30°C can hold, at most, about 7.6 milligrams of oxygen per liter — and that is before a single fish has breathed, a single bacterium has decomposed anything, or a single night has passed without photosynthesis to top the oxygen back up. Algae and plants produce oxygen during the day, but they consume it at night, and bacteria consume it around the clock. In a warm, still, nutrient-rich lake, the pre-dawn low can drop below what fish tolerate, and the fish that cannot swim away die where they are. The water did not poison them. It simply stopped being air.

## The Verdict

**Mostly True.** Fish can die underwater from a failure to breathe, and that is what the question "can fish drown" is really asking. They suffocate when the water holds too little dissolved oxygen, when their gills are damaged or blocked, when they are trapped and cannot keep water flowing across their gills, or when they are ram ventilators that stop swimming. Scientists who study fish say it directly: it is more correct to call it suffocation than drowning, but "to some extent it is more or less the same thing."

The only thing that keeps this from being a clean "True" is the word itself. Drowning, strictly defined, means water entering lungs and blocking air — and fish have no lungs. A fish does not inhale water the way a drowning swimmer does. It fails to extract oxygen from water that has stopped providing it. That is a real distinction, and it is worth keeping, because it points to the real causes: warming water, nutrient pollution, damaged gills, and nets that hold a swimming animal still. But it is a distinction about the mechanism, not about the outcome. The fish is dead either way, and it died because it could not breathe.

So the next time someone asks whether fish can drown, the honest answer is not a yes or a no. It is a story: about gills that are among the most efficient organs in nature and also among the most fragile, about sharks that must swim or die, about dead zones the size of states, and about a simple fact that is easy to forget on a hot summer day beside a lake that looks perfectly fine. The water is not the oxygen. The oxygen is a guest in the water, present in tiny amounts, easily driven out — and when it goes, the fish go with it.
