It usually starts the night before something important. A pimple appears, red and unmistakable, right where everyone will look. Someone — a friend, a sibling, a commenter on a video with millions of views — offers the same confident advice that has circulated for decades: just dab a little toothpaste on it before bed, and by morning it will be gone. The tube is already in the bathroom cabinet. It costs nothing extra. It feels like exactly the kind of clever, thrifty trick that folk wisdom is supposed to be.

It is also, according to dermatologists, a bad idea. Toothpaste can make a pimple look smaller for a few hours, because several of its ingredients are drying agents. But drying a spot is not the same as treating acne, and the difference matters. A pimple is not a smear on a surface that can be scrubbed away. It is an inflamed, blocked pore in living skin, and the paste designed to scour plaque off tooth enamel is far too harsh, far too alkaline in some formulations, and far too full of flavourings, foaming agents, and abrasives to belong on a face. The most likely overnight result is not clear skin. It is a drier, redder, possibly peeling patch of skin sitting exactly where the pimple was, sometimes with the pimple still underneath it.

That distinction — between something that shrinks a spot temporarily and something that actually clears acne — is the whole story of this claim. Toothpaste fails the test dermatologists actually apply: does it address what causes the pimple, does it do so without damaging the surrounding skin, and is there any evidence it works better than doing nothing? On all three counts, the answer is no.

## The claim, in its usual form

The version of the advice that travels online is remarkably consistent. Squeeze a small dot of plain white toothpaste — never the gel kind, the lore insists — onto the pimple, leave it on overnight, and wash it off in the morning. Some versions add a warning to avoid whitening toothpaste, others recommend covering the spot with a bandage, and a few suggest repeating the treatment for several nights if the first one does not work. The promise is always speed: this is presented as an overnight cure for an individual spot, not as a long-term acne treatment.

The advice survives because it contains a grain of observable truth. Many people who try it do wake up to a spot that looks flatter and drier. That observation is real, and it is worth taking seriously, because it explains why the trick has lasted so long. But an observation is not an explanation, and a flatter spot in the morning is not evidence that the underlying problem has been solved. A sunburn also looks less red after a cold compress, and nobody would call a cold compress a cure for sun damage. The question is what the toothpaste is actually doing to the skin, and whether that effect helps or harms over the following days.

Part of the claim's staying power is its simplicity. Acne treatment can feel complicated and expensive: cleansers, spot treatments, retinoids, dermatologist appointments, prescriptions. Toothpaste, by contrast, is already there, already paid for, and already associated in people's minds with cleanliness. If a product cleans teeth, the reasoning goes, surely it can clean a pore. That reasoning feels intuitive, but it ignores a basic fact about how products are formulated. A toothpaste is engineered for a hard, non-living surface — tooth enamel — that is rinsed within minutes and protected by saliva. Facial skin is living tissue, much thinner and more reactive, and it is expected to tolerate a paste left on it for hours. What is gentle enough for enamel is not automatically gentle enough for a face.

## Why it sounds plausible: the drying ingredients

Toothpaste does contain ingredients that dry things out, and this is the kernel of truth at the centre of the myth. Baking soda, alcohol, and hydrogen peroxide — the last of these mainly in whitening formulas — all remove moisture and oil. Sodium lauryl sulfate, the foaming agent that makes toothpaste lather, strips oil as well. Applied to a pimple, this cocktail can dehydrate the top of the spot, making it look smaller and less shiny by morning. Cleveland Clinic dermatologist Shilpi Khetarpal has acknowledged exactly this part of the story, noting that modern toothpaste contains ingredients like alcohol or baking soda that may help dry out a pimple, before adding the crucial qualification that those same tubes contain other ingredients never intended for facial skin.

The problem is that drying is a blunt instrument. The pimple is not simply wet; it is a pore blocked by dead skin cells and excess oil, colonised by bacteria, and inflamed by the immune system's response. Drying the surface does nothing to unblock the pore, nothing to normalise the shedding of skin cells inside it, and nothing to calm the inflammation in a controlled way. At best it masks the spot for a day. At worst it damages the skin barrier around the spot, which is the thin protective layer that keeps moisture in and irritants out. Once that barrier is compromised, the skin becomes red, tight, and flaky, and it is more vulnerable to further irritation from everything else a person puts on their face afterwards, from cleanser to sunscreen to makeup.

Dermatologists describe a further trap. When skin is overdried, it often compensates by producing more oil. That rebound oiliness can then feed the very conditions that create new pimples, leaving a person in a cycle: dry out a spot aggressively, irritate the skin, watch oil production rise, and develop another spot nearby. This is why the advice to repeat the toothpaste treatment for several nights is particularly unhelpful. Each application adds irritation without addressing the cause, and the cumulative damage can outlast the original pimple by days.

There is also a simple mismatch of targets. The bacteria most associated with acne, Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes), live deep inside the oil-rich, low-oxygen environment of a blocked follicle. Toothpaste's antibacterial ingredients, where they exist at all, were chosen for the bacteria of the mouth, not for the bacteria of a blocked pore, and a paste sitting on the skin's surface cannot reliably reach the site of the problem. As dermatologist Dennis Gross has put it, the short answer is that toothpaste should not be used as a pimple treatment, precisely because a product formulated to clean teeth is not formulated to treat skin.

## What a pimple actually is

To understand why toothpaste fails, it helps to understand what it is supposed to be fixing. Acne develops through four interlocking processes, and every effective treatment targets at least one of them. First, the skin produces excess sebum, the oil made by sebaceous glands. Second, dead skin cells fail to shed normally and instead clump together, plugging the hair follicle. Third, Cutibacterium acnes, a bacterium that normally lives harmlessly on skin, multiplies in the blocked, oxygen-poor follicle. Fourth, the immune system responds to that overgrowth with inflammation, producing the redness, swelling, and tenderness of a visible pimple.

A whitehead is a closed, blocked pore. A blackhead is an open one, darkened by oxidation rather than dirt. When the blockage and bacterial overgrowth provoke a stronger inflammatory response, the result is a papule, a pustule, or, in more severe cases, a painful nodule or cyst beneath the skin. None of these stages is a surface stain. They are all happening inside the follicle, below the surface, which is why surface drying is such a poor substitute for treatment. Effective acne ingredients work by unblocking the pore, reducing bacterial numbers inside it, normalising cell shedding, or calming inflammation — and they do so at concentrations and in formulations tested on skin, not on teeth.

This is also why squeezing a pimple is discouraged alongside the toothpaste trick: both approaches attack the visible tip of a deeper problem and risk making the inflammation worse. Pressing on an inflamed follicle can rupture it beneath the surface, spreading its contents into surrounding tissue and increasing the chance of scarring. Toothpaste does not carry quite the same mechanical risk, but it shares the same flaw — it treats the spot as something to be punished into submission, rather than as an inflamed pore that needs gentle, targeted care.

## What dermatologists actually say

The professional consensus on this claim is unusually clear, and it is worth stating plainly because the online advice is so confident in the opposite direction. Cleveland Clinic dermatology resident Taylor Bullock has said that toothpaste is made for the mouth and should stay there, and that it will likely do more harm than good for skin, leaving a person with a redder, more irritated pimple than they started with. Khetarpal, a Cleveland Clinic dermatologist, has cautioned that using toothpaste to treat a pimple could possibly irritate the skin. Marie Hayag, a board-certified dermatologist in New York, has acknowledged that toothpaste can dry out a pimple because of its drying ingredients, while warning that those same ingredients — alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, baking soda, sodium lauryl sulfate, herbal extracts and oils, and propylene glycol — can irritate skin by causing redness and peeling.

Notice the pattern in these assessments. Nobody claims toothpaste does literally nothing. The claim is not that the paste sits inert on the skin. The claim is that what it does is the wrong thing: it dries and irritates, rather than treating. That is why the verdict on the viral claim is False rather than merely unproven. The promise is that toothpaste gets rid of pimples. The evidence says it does not clear acne, and that the apparent overnight improvement is a drying illusion that can cost more in irritation than it gains in appearance.

It is also worth noting what dermatologists recommend instead, because the contrast is instructive. The recommended spot treatments are boring by comparison — a dab of benzoyl peroxide, a salicylic acid product, a hydrocolloid patch — and they are boring for a reason. They were developed for skin, tested on skin, and dosed for skin. Benzoyl peroxide kills acne bacteria inside the pore by releasing oxygen into an environment where those bacteria cannot thrive. Salicylic acid, a beta hydroxy acid, dissolves the bonds between dead skin cells and helps unblock the pore. Hydrocolloid patches absorb fluid and protect the spot from picking. None of these promises an overnight miracle, and none of them comes with a tube of flavourings, abrasives, and foaming agents designed for a different part of the body entirely.

## The ingredients problem, one by one

A typical toothpaste label reads like a list of things that should never spend a night on an inflamed pore. The abrasives — hydrated silica or calcium carbonate — are there to polish plaque off enamel. On skin they act as a physical irritant, especially on a spot that is already tender. The foaming agent sodium lauryl sulfate is a strong cleanser that strips the skin's natural oils and is a known irritant for many people; it is one of the ingredients most often implicated when a skincare product causes stinging or redness. Menthol and flavouring oils such as peppermint, spearmint, cinnamon, and eucalyptus create the fresh sensation in the mouth, but on facial skin they can provoke burning, redness, and contact reactions, particularly in people with sensitive skin or conditions such as rosacea.

Whitening toothpastes are worse again, because they add hydrogen peroxide or other bleaching agents to the mix. Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidiser; on an open or irritated spot it can damage surrounding skin cells and delay healing. Gel toothpastes, despite the folk rule that only white paste works, are no safer — they simply contain a different blend of the same kinds of detergents, humectants, and flavourings. And the fluoride that makes toothpaste valuable for teeth has no acne-fighting role at all; it is there to remineralise enamel, not to unblock pores.

The risk is not theoretical. Dermatology sources consistently list the same outcomes from toothpaste on skin: redness, peeling, dryness, contact dermatitis, and in more sensitive cases something resembling a mild chemical burn. The irritation often looks worse than the original pimple, which is a cruel irony for a treatment chosen for an important morning. A flat, red, peeling patch is more noticeable, harder to cover with makeup, and slower to heal than a small spot would have been. In people with darker skin tones there is an additional concern: inflammation can trigger post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, dark marks that linger for weeks or months after the redness fades. A remedy that trades a one-day pimple for a months-long mark is a bad bargain by any measure.

It is important to be fair about the range of outcomes. Not everyone who tries toothpaste will be harmed. Some people will apply it once, see a drier spot, experience no obvious irritation, and conclude that it worked. Skin varies, formulations vary, and a single brief application is less risky than repeated nightly use. But a fact-check is not about whether a remedy sometimes gets away with it. It is about whether the claim holds up as general advice, and whether the expected benefit outweighs the expected harm. On that standard, toothpaste fails. The benefit is cosmetic and temporary; the harm is real, common, and entirely avoidable by using a product actually made for skin.

## The triclosan twist: where the myth may have started

Every durable myth usually has an origin story, and this one has a plausible candidate. Older toothpaste formulas sometimes contained triclosan, an antibacterial agent that was also used in soaps and other products. The reasoning, such as it was, ran like this: pimples involve bacteria, triclosan kills bacteria, therefore toothpaste containing triclosan might kill the bacteria in a pimple. It was never a strong argument — triclosan was chosen for oral bacteria and for use in a product rinsed away within minutes — but it gave the trick a veneer of scientific logic, and it may explain why the advice took hold in the first place.

That veneer has since peeled away. The United States Food and Drug Administration banned triclosan in over-the-counter antibacterial hand and body washes effective September 2017, after manufacturers failed to demonstrate that those products were both safe for long-term daily use and more effective than plain soap and water. The agency did not ban triclosan in toothpaste at that time, because there was evidence it helped reduce plaque and gingivitis, but the direction of travel was clear. Cleveland Clinic has since reported that, as of 2019, no toothpaste brands sold in the United States use triclosan. The one ingredient that ever gave the toothpaste trick even a thin antibacterial rationale is therefore gone from the tubes most people actually own. What remains is a drying, irritating paste whose original justification no longer applies.

This history also illustrates a broader lesson about folk remedies. Advice can outlive the conditions that made it seem reasonable. A tip passed from person to person, and later from video to video, rarely carries its own expiry date. Nobody updates the sleepover advice when a manufacturer reformulates, or when a regulator acts, or when dermatologists publish clearer guidance. The result is a claim frozen in time, repeated with the confidence of settled wisdom long after its foundations have been removed. Fact-checking is, in part, the work of putting an expiry date back on such advice.

## Why the morning-after test misleads

There is a psychological reason the toothpaste trick feels convincing, beyond the visible drying. When a person applies toothpaste the night before an important event, they are highly motivated to see improvement in the morning, and pimples themselves are variable. A single spot naturally changes over twenty-four hours: inflammation peaks and subsides, fluid shifts, and the spot may look different for reasons that have nothing to do with what was applied to it. Without a comparison — the same spot, at the same stage, left alone — it is impossible to know from one morning's mirror whether the toothpaste helped, did nothing, or merely coincided with the spot's natural course.

This is the same trap that sustains many skincare myths. A remedy is tried at the worst moment, the spot improves on its own timeline, and the remedy takes the credit. The irritation that follows, if it appears a day or two later, is often blamed on something else — a new moisturiser, the weather, stress — rather than on the paste that damaged the barrier two nights earlier. The result is a self-confirming loop in which successes are remembered, harms are misattributed, and the advice is passed on with genuine sincerity by people who believe it worked for them.

Controlled evidence points the other way. There are no clinical trials showing that toothpaste clears acne, and there is a reason for that absence: no researcher or manufacturer has a plausible basis for testing a dental product as an acne treatment, and dermatology guidelines consistently recommend the proven alternatives instead. The absence of trials is not, on its own, proof of ineffectiveness — many folk remedies have simply never been studied — but combined with a clear mechanism of harm and a unanimous professional consensus, it leaves the claim with nowhere to stand. The burden of proof rests on the remedy, and toothpaste cannot meet it.

## Related claims worth untangling

The toothpaste myth does not travel alone. It belongs to a family of bathroom-cabinet acne advice that shares the same flaw: a household product is repurposed for skin on the basis of one superficial property. Crushed aspirin is sometimes recommended because its active ingredient resembles salicylic acid, but dermatology guidance notes limited clinical evidence that it works for acne, and it can dry skin without much benefit. Lemon juice is suggested because it is acidic and astringent, but it can cause irritation and increase sun sensitivity. Rubbing alcohol is used to dry spots, with the same barrier-damaging consequences as toothpaste. In each case, the logic is the same — if it dries, cleans, or stings, it must be doing something — and in each case the logic fails for the same reason: skin is not a countertop.

It is also worth separating the toothpaste claim from neighbouring claims about acne that do have some evidence behind them. The question of whether diet affects acne, for instance, is genuinely more nuanced than the toothpaste question. Research has examined high-glycaemic foods and dairy, and our earlier fact-check on <a href="/blog/chocolate-causes-acne-fact-check/">Does Chocolate Cause Acne?</a> found a grain of truth in the chocolate claim, with small trials suggesting cocoa can worsen breakouts in acne-prone people. That nuance does not rescue toothpaste. A claim can be partly true when it concerns a complex dietary relationship, and flatly false when it concerns smearing a dental product on an inflamed pore. Each claim must be judged on its own evidence, not on the reputation of its neighbours.

The same applies to the wider world of skincare gadgets and trends. Our fact-check on <a href="/blog/do-led-face-masks-work-fact-check/">Do LED Face Masks Work?</a> found a real technology wrapped in exaggerated marketing — a kernel of science, oversold. Toothpaste is the reverse image: a household product with no relevant science behind its acne use, sustained by anecdote. And our look at <a href="/blog/sunscreen-prevents-aging-fact-check/">Does Sunscreen Really Prevent Skin Cancer and Premature Aging?</a> showed what strong evidence actually looks like — randomised trials, consistent results, and professional consensus pointing the same way. On toothpaste, the professional consensus points the other way, and there are no trials pointing back.

## The Verdict

Does toothpaste get rid of pimples? No. The claim is False.

Toothpaste can dry the surface of a spot overnight, which is why the trick feels as though it works. But it does not unblock the pore, does not treat the bacterial overgrowth and inflammation that cause the pimple, and does not clear acne in any lasting sense. Its ingredients — abrasives, foaming detergents, menthol and flavourings, and in whitening formulas hydrogen peroxide — are formulated for tooth enamel, not for living facial skin, and they commonly cause redness, peeling, dryness, and contact irritation that can look worse than the original spot and take longer to heal. The one ingredient that once gave the trick a thin antibacterial rationale, triclosan, is no longer used in toothpaste sold in the United States. Dermatologists are unanimous: keep toothpaste for teeth, and treat pimples with products made for skin.

That verdict comes with a fair qualification. A single application will not ruin most people's skin, and some people will escape obvious harm. But "sometimes gets away with it" is not the standard for a remedy recommended to millions of people, including teenagers with sensitive skin and adults with conditions such as rosacea or eczema, for whom the risk is higher. A treatment that offers only a temporary cosmetic illusion while carrying a real risk of irritation is not a treatment at all. It is a gamble with worse odds than doing nothing.

## What actually works instead

The good news is that the alternatives are cheap, widely available, and genuinely effective for the occasional spot. Start with the basics: wash the face gently, twice a day, with a mild cleanser and lukewarm water, and avoid scrubbing the spot. Harsh scrubbing, like toothpaste, irritates inflamed skin and can worsen redness. Pat the skin dry rather than rubbing it, and follow with a simple, fragrance-free moisturiser. Healthy skin heals faster than stripped skin, and a moisturiser is not the enemy of acne treatment — it is part of it.

For the spot itself, benzoyl peroxide is the closest thing to a direct replacement for the toothpaste trick, and it works for the reasons toothpaste does not. Available over the counter in creams, gels, and patches, benzoyl peroxide kills acne bacteria inside the pore and helps clear blockages, at concentrations formulated and tested for skin. Start with a lower strength, apply a thin layer only to the spot, and expect some mild dryness at first — that dryness is a known, manageable side effect of a product designed for the job, rather than an accident of a product designed for something else. If benzoyl peroxide is too drying, use it every other day, or apply moisturiser first as a buffer, a technique dermatologists often recommend.

Salicylic acid is the second mainstay. As a beta hydroxy acid, it penetrates oil and dissolves the bonds holding dead skin cells together, helping to unblock the pore from the inside. It is available in cleansers, toners, and targeted spot treatments, and it suits people whose main problem is clogged pores and blackheads. Adapalene, a topical retinoid available over the counter in many countries, normalises skin cell turnover and prevents new blockages from forming; it works more slowly than a spot treatment, over weeks rather than days, but it addresses the process that creates pimples in the first place. For a single, already-formed whitehead, a hydrocolloid patch can absorb fluid, protect the spot from fingers and friction, and prevent picking — which, unlike toothpaste, is a genuine overnight benefit.

None of these options promises a miracle by morning, and that honesty is part of their value. A pimple takes time to resolve because inflammation takes time to subside, even with the right treatment. The realistic goal of a spot treatment is to shorten that timeline by a day or two, reduce redness, and prevent the spot from becoming worse — not to erase it overnight. Anyone selling an overnight erasure, whether a toothpaste tube or a miracle cream, is selling the fantasy that makes the toothpaste myth so persistent.

## If you have already tried it

If toothpaste is already on your face, or was last night, there is no need to panic. Wash it off gently with lukewarm water and a mild, fragrance-free cleanser, and do not scrub the area or apply further home remedies on top of it. Apply a simple moisturiser, and plain petroleum jelly can help protect a raw or irritated patch while the barrier recovers. Avoid other strong active ingredients — retinoids, exfoliating acids, vitamin C serums — on the irritated area until the redness and peeling settle, and be extra careful with sun exposure, since damaged skin is more vulnerable to ultraviolet light.

Most irritation from a single application settles within a few days. Seek medical advice sooner if the area blisters, swells significantly, weeps, shows signs of infection such as spreading redness and warmth, or does not improve after several days of gentle care. The same advice applies if toothpaste gets into the eyes, which can cause significant stinging and should be rinsed thoroughly with water. And whatever happens, resist the urge to reapply. If the first application irritated the skin, a second one will not fix the problem it created.

## When to see a dermatologist

An occasional pimple needs no professional attention. But several situations do warrant a dermatologist's advice rather than another round of home experiments. If breakouts are frequent, widespread, painful, or cystic — deep, tender lumps beneath the skin — over-the-counter treatments may not be enough, and prescription options such as stronger retinoids, hormonal treatments, or oral medications can make a real difference. If acne is leaving scars or dark marks, early professional treatment can prevent permanent damage that no home remedy can undo. If a skin problem keeps returning in the same place, or looks different from ordinary acne, it may not be acne at all: conditions such as rosacea, perioral dermatitis, and folliculitis can resemble acne and require different treatment entirely.

A dermatologist can also build a maintenance plan, which is something no overnight trick can offer. Acne is a chronic tendency of the skin, not a series of isolated emergencies, and the most effective approach is usually a consistent, gentle routine that prevents blockages from forming, rather than a frantic response to each new spot. That routine will almost certainly not include toothpaste. It will include a cleanser, a moisturiser, sun protection, and one or two active ingredients chosen for the individual's skin type and acne severity. It is less exciting than a secret trick, and it works.

## The bottom line

The toothpaste trick endures because it is free, because it is already in the bathroom, and because it produces a visible change by morning. But a visible change is not a cure, and the change it produces — a dried, irritated patch of skin — is the opposite of healing. Dermatologists do not disagree about this in any meaningful way. Toothpaste belongs on teeth. Pimples belong to products made for skin, to patience, and, when they are frequent or severe, to a dermatologist.

So the next time a pimple appears the night before something important, leave the toothpaste tube where it is. Wash your face gently, dab on a real spot treatment if you have one, put on a hydrocolloid patch if the spot tempts you to touch it, and go to bed. The spot will still be there in the morning — that is how inflammation works — but it will be a spot on its way to healing, on skin that has not been punished for the crime of breaking out. That is a far better deal than a red, peeling patch and a pimple underneath it, and it costs no more than the tube you were about to misuse. Some folk wisdom deserves to survive. This piece of it has had a long enough run, and it is time to put it back in the cabinet — on the toothbrush, where it belongs.
