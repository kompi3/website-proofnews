In September 2025, headlines around the world announced that Earth had gained a second moon. A small asteroid, freshly spotted by a telescope in Hawaii, was said to be sharing our planet's orbit — and some reports presented it as a brand-new companion in the night sky, a second moon to rival the one we have known since the dawn of humanity. The story spread fast, carried by the same social media currents that turn every astronomical oddity into a viral sensation. It was dramatic, it was exciting, and it was, in the way it was framed, wrong.

Earth has exactly one true natural satellite: the Moon. No second moon has ever been confirmed, and no astronomer has found one hiding in the dark. But the story behind the headlines is real, and it is stranger than the version that went viral. Earth does have a small, shifting entourage of rocky companions — temporary captives snatched by our gravity for a few months, and distant lookalikes that orbit the Sun in lockstep with our planet, appearing to loop around us without ever actually doing so. Scientists call them mini-moons and quasi-moons. They are genuine discoveries, they are scientifically fascinating, and they are not moons. This is the story of what they really are, how they were found, and why the "second moon" label keeps misleading the public.

## The Headline That Started It All

The asteroid at the centre of the 2025 headlines is called 2025 PN7. It was first detected on 2 August 2025 by the Pan-STARRS 1 telescope on Haleakala, in Hawaii — one of the survey telescopes that scan the sky every clear night looking for moving objects. At an estimated 19 metres (about 62 feet) across, it is roughly the size of a large house, and at magnitude 26 it is more than a hundred million times fainter than the limit of unaided human vision. It had, in other words, been quietly travelling beside us for decades before anyone noticed it was there.

A paper by Carlos de la Fuente Marcos and colleagues, published in the journal *Research Notes of the American Astronomical Society* on 2 September 2025, identified 2025 PN7 as the newest member of a rare class of objects: Earth's quasi-satellites. The researchers calculated that it had been in its current orbital arrangement since around the 1950s or 1960s — roughly 60 years — and that it would probably stay there for another 60 years or so, until about 2083 or 2084, before drifting away. They described it as the smallest and least stable quasi-satellite of Earth yet known, and the seventh member of the family.

That is a real and interesting discovery. What happened next is a familiar story in science communication: the nuance fell away. "Quasi-satellite" became "second moon" in headline after headline. Some reports stated plainly that Earth had acquired a second moon. Others hedged, but the impression left on millions of readers was the same: look up, and there are now two moons in the sky. There are not. Nobody with a telescope, a camera, or even a pair of good eyes has ever seen a second moon, because 2025 PN7 is far too small and faint to see, and because it does not orbit the Earth at all.

## What 2025 PN7 Actually Is

To understand why 2025 PN7 is not a moon, it helps to start with what a moon actually is. A true moon is a natural object that is gravitationally bound to a planet and orbits that planet directly. Our Moon circles the Earth at an average distance of about 384,400 kilometres, held in place by Earth's gravity, and it has done so for billions of years. It raises our tides, stabilises the tilt of our planet's axis, and is visible to every human being who has ever looked up at a clear night sky.

2025 PN7 does none of those things. It orbits the Sun, not the Earth. Its path around the Sun is almost identical to Earth's — its semi-major axis is 1.003 astronomical units, barely outside our own — so it takes about one year to complete a circuit, the same time Earth takes. That 1:1 orbital resonance is what creates the illusion. From our point of view, the little asteroid sometimes drifts ahead of Earth and sometimes trails behind it, tracing a looping path that looks, in diagrams, as though it is circling our planet. In reality the Sun is its anchor, and Earth's gravity has never captured it.

The paper's own language is careful on this point: 2025 PN7 is a quasi-satellite, and quasi-satellites "are in a resonant orbit but are not gravitationally bound to Earth." Its closest approach to our planet is about 299,337 kilometres — closer than the Moon — but its average distance is around 384,400 kilometres, roughly the Moon's own distance. It is a travelling companion, not a satellite. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the difference between an object that belongs to Earth and an object that merely happens to be going the same way, at the same speed, around the same star.

2025 PN7 is also temporary on a cosmic timescale. Its time in the quasi-satellite state is expected to last about 128 years in total, far shorter than the roughly 381 years estimated for its larger cousin Kamoʻoalewa. And in October 2026, new spectroscopic research examined its physical characteristics, offering further evidence relevant to its possible origins in the asteroid belt — a reminder that even a year after its discovery, scientists are still working out what this small rock is made of and where it came from.

## Mini-Moons: Earth's Temporary Captives

Quasi-moons are only half of the story. Earth also experiences a different, rarer phenomenon: genuine temporary captures, in which a small asteroid wanders close enough for Earth's gravity to snatch it into orbit around our planet for a while — weeks, months, or occasionally a year or two — before it slips away again. Astronomers call these objects mini-moons, or temporary satellites, and they are real moons in a limited sense: while they last, they genuinely orbit the Earth.

But the record shows just how rare and how small they are. According to research presented by Grigori Fedorets of the University of Helsinki at the Europlanet Science Congress in September 2026, only two mini-moons have ever been confirmed to have completed a full orbit around Earth: 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3, both discovered by the Catalina Sky Survey in Arizona. Two more objects, 2022 NX1 and 2024 PT5, were captured only briefly, without completing a full orbit, and sit at the edge of the definition. Fedorets' modelling suggests that the largest mini-moon around Earth at any given moment is typically only about 75 centimetres — 30 inches — across. Most of Earth's temporary moons are the size of a beach ball or smaller, and they are gone before most people ever hear about them.

The most famous recent case is 2024 PT5, and it illustrates both why these objects matter and why the headlines oversell them. The asteroid was first detected on 7 August 2024 by the ATLAS survey telescope in Sutherland, South Africa, a NASA-funded system designed to spot objects that might one day threaten Earth. At about 10 metres (33 feet) wide, it was small but large enough to track. It was temporarily captured by Earth's gravity from 29 September to 25 November 2024, looping around our planet for about two months without ever completing a full orbit, and then it drifted back into its orbit around the Sun.

What made 2024 PT5 special was not its visit but its origin. A team led by Theodore Kareta of Lowell Observatory in Arizona obtained visible and near-infrared spectra of the object using the Lowell Discovery Telescope and NASA's Infrared Telescope Facility in Hawaii. The light reflected from its surface matched lunar rock samples — it was rich in pyroxene minerals, unlike asteroids of comparable redness, which tend to be rich in olivine — and it matched no known asteroid type. The team's conclusion, published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* in January 2025, was that 2024 PT5 is ejecta blasted off the surface of the Moon by an ancient impact, making it only the second near-Earth asteroid suggested to have come from the Moon.

"We had a general idea that this asteroid may have come from the Moon, but the smoking gun was when we found out that it was rich in silicate minerals — not the kind that are seen on asteroids but those that have been found in lunar rock samples," Kareta said. He added that the object appears to have spent only a short time in space: "It looks like it hasn't been in space for very long, maybe just a few thousand years or so, as there is a lack of space weathering that would have caused its spectrum to redden."

The team also had to rule out a more mundane explanation. Anything on an Earth-like orbit could, in principle, be a piece of discarded rocket hardware rather than a natural rock — and in 2020, a supposed mini-moon called 2020 SO turned out to be exactly that, a spent Centaur rocket stage from NASA's 1966 Surveyor 2 mission. Scientists at NASA's Center for Near Earth Object Studies analysed 2024 PT5's motion and found that the effect of solar radiation pressure on its trajectory was orders of magnitude smaller than would be expected for a hollow, artificial object. It was natural, and it was lunar. The discovery, in effect, doubled the number of known asteroids thought to originate from the Moon — and the little rock is expected to swing back past Earth again in 2084, for about 43 days.

And the captures keep happening. The Minor Planet Center, the official clearinghouse for asteroid sightings, announced on 29 October 2026 that a tiny object called 2026 QQ8 had been looping around Earth since late April 2026. At only about 3 feet wide, it is far smaller than 2024 PT5, and it was expected to leave Earth's grasp in December 2026. Fedorets also noted a potential third confirmed mini-moon, the recently detected Sar2901, which could equally turn out to be an artificial object; it has faded from view but should be observable again around mid-to-late November 2026. The census of Earth's temporary companions is being written in real time.

## Quasi-Moons: The Companions That Never Orbit Us

If mini-moons are brief guests, quasi-moons are long-term neighbours. They are asteroids in a 1:1 resonance with Earth — travelling around the Sun in about the same time we do, on almost the same path — and they can keep pace with us for decades or centuries. The most famous is Kamoʻoalewa, formally designated 469219 Kamoʻoalewa, discovered in 2016. At 40 to 100 metres across, it is the largest of Earth's known quasi-moons and by far the most stable, expected to keep pace with Earth for roughly 300 more years.

Kamoʻoalewa is also at the centre of one of the most intriguing origin stories in modern planetary science. A study published in *Nature Astronomy* by a team from Tsinghua University in China analysed the spectrum of light reflected from the asteroid and found silicate minerals more common in lunar samples than in ordinary asteroids. Computer simulations suggested its shape and orbit fit a fragment chipped off the Moon in a massive impact that carved a crater 10 to 20 kilometres wide on the lunar far side, probably within the last few million years — young, by geological standards. In other words, our "second moon" may literally be a piece of our first one.

The idea is not settled. A study published in *Nature Communications* in 2026 argued that Kamoʻoalewa's tell-tale reddish colour — long treated as a sign of Moon-like material — could instead be explained by space weathering, the slow alteration of a surface by sunlight and cosmic radiation. The debate is genuinely unresolved, which is exactly why getting a physical sample matters so much. China's Tianwen-2 mission rendezvoused with Kamoʻoalewa in 2026 and aims to collect roughly 100 grams of surface material and return it to Earth, an experiment that could settle the question of whether this quasi-moon is a chip off the old lunar block or simply an ordinary asteroid in disguise.

The family is larger than these two names. Alongside Kamoʻoalewa and 2025 PN7, the known quasi-satellites include Cardea, 277810, 2013 LX28, 2014 OL339 and 2023 FW13 — seven in total as of the 2025 PN7 paper. And the very first object to earn the popular "second moon" nickname was none of these. That honour belongs to 3753 Cruithne, discovered in 1986, a 5-kilometre-wide asteroid that follows a sprawling horseshoe-shaped orbit around the Sun, periodically overtaking Earth and then falling behind. For a time in the 1990s, popular articles breathlessly described Cruithne as Earth's second moon. It is not, and it never was — it orbits the Sun, just like all the rest.

The pattern is worth noticing. Every generation of astronomy seems to produce a new "second moon", and every time the object turns out to be something else: a quasi-satellite, a temporary captive, or a piece of space junk. The label survives because it is vivid and because it is almost true. These objects really do accompany Earth. They really do share our orbit. They simply do not orbit us, and that single fact is what separates a moon from a fellow traveller.

## Why the "Second Moon" Label Is Wrong

The confusion is understandable, because the words astronomers use invite it. "Mini-moon" sounds like a small moon. "Quasi-moon" sounds like a nearly-moon. And "temporary satellite" sounds like a moon with an expiry date. But each term describes a different relationship with Earth, and only one of them involves actually orbiting our planet — and even then, only briefly.

A true moon is gravitationally bound to its planet. The Moon is bound to Earth: if you switched off the Sun tomorrow, the Moon would keep circling us. A quasi-moon is not bound to Earth at all. Switch off the Sun and it would fly away in a straight line, because the Sun is what holds it. A mini-moon is bound to Earth — but only for a visit. Its capture is a gravitational accident, a close encounter that traps a passing rock for a few months before the same forces that caught it set it free again.

Scale is the other half of the story. The Moon is a world thousands of kilometres across, massive enough to raise ocean tides and steady the tilt of our planet. The largest quasi-moon known, Kamoʻoalewa, is at most 100 metres wide — roughly the length of a football field. 2025 PN7 is 19 metres, about the length of a bus. The largest mini-moon likely to be around at any given moment is about 75 centimetres, the size of a large beach ball. None of them affects the tides, the seasons, or anything else on Earth. They have, as one report on Kamoʻoalewa put it, no effect on us at all.

That is why the verdict on the viral claim is "Misleading" rather than "False". The claim contains a grain of truth: Earth really does have small rocky companions beyond the Moon, and astronomers really did discover a new one in 2025. But the headline version — that Earth has gained a second moon — implies a second permanent, visible, Moon-like satellite, and nothing of the kind exists. The objects behind the story are too small to see, too temporary to matter, and, in the case of the quasi-moons, not orbiting Earth in the first place.

## A Brief History of Earth's Almost-Moons

The "second moon" is not a new idea. It has been announced, debunked, and announced again for nearly forty years, and the timeline shows how each generation of the story has added a real discovery underneath the hype.

**1986 — Cruithne is discovered.** Astronomers spot 3753 Cruithne, a 5-kilometre asteroid on a horseshoe orbit around the Sun. Within a decade it is being described in popular articles as Earth's second moon, a label that sticks for years despite astronomers' corrections.

**2006 — The first confirmed mini-moon.** The Catalina Sky Survey discovers 2006 RH120, a small asteroid that Earth captures for about a year. It is the first object confirmed to have completed a full orbit around Earth before escaping, and the first proof that our planet regularly steals temporary moons.

**2016 — Kamoʻoalewa joins the family.** The Pan-STARRS telescope discovers 2016 HO3, later named Kamoʻoalewa, a 40- to 100-metre asteroid and the most stable quasi-satellite of Earth known. It becomes the target of China's Tianwen-2 sample-return mission.

**2020 — A mini-moon, and a false alarm.** The Catalina Sky Survey finds 2020 CD3, the second confirmed mini-moon, which stays for about three years. The same year, a supposed mini-moon called 2020 SO turns out to be a spent NASA rocket stage from 1966 — a reminder that not every object near Earth is a rock.

**2024 — The lunar visitor.** The ATLAS survey discovers 2024 PT5 on 7 August. It is captured by Earth's gravity from 29 September to 25 November, and research published in January 2025 shows it is probably a chip blasted off the Moon itself.

**September 2025 — The "second moon" headlines.** De la Fuente Marcos and colleagues publish their paper on 2025 PN7, discovered a month earlier by Pan-STARRS. News reports around the world announce that Earth has a second moon. Astronomers point out, again, that it is a quasi-satellite, not a moon.

**2026 — The census grows.** China's Tianwen-2 mission rendezvous with Kamoʻoalewa to collect a sample that may settle its origin. The Minor Planet Center announces a new mini-moon, 2026 QQ8, in October. And at the Europlanet Science Congress in September, researchers explain that the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's decade-long survey should start finding mini-moons at a steady rate — one every one to four years.

## What the Surveys Will Find Next

If the history of almost-moons has a lesson, it is that the objects themselves are real and the count is only going up. Fedorets' modelling suggests that Earth almost always has more than one moon in the loose sense — the others are just very small, and they never stay long. The largest mini-moon present at any moment is typically only about 75 centimetres across, which is why most of them are never noticed at all. They are discovered only when a survey telescope happens to be looking in the right place at the right time, and only the biggest and brightest of them get announced.

That is about to change. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, with the largest digital camera ever built for astronomy, is beginning its ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time, scanning the entire southern sky every few nights. Fedorets' abstract for the Europlanet Science Congress argues that the survey should turn up mini-moons at a rate of about one every one to four years — and because Rubin will spot them sooner, astronomers will have more time to study them before they drift away. His team is already building a dedicated system to sift Rubin's alerts through the ANTARES real-time astronomical data broker, flagging potential mini-moons for rapid follow-up observations.

The prize is more than a curiosity. Temporary satellites are among the most accessible objects in the solar system: they are already close to Earth, moving slowly relative to us, and cheap to reach with a spacecraft. NASA once studied them as targets for its cancelled Asteroid Redirect Mission, and they remain attractive for scientific exploration, for testing how to mine asteroids in place, and for learning where the material in Earth's immediate neighbourhood actually comes from. Every new mini-moon is a free sample delivery — a rock that comes to us, stays a while, and leaves. The 2024 PT5 case showed that some of those samples are pieces of the Moon itself, blasted into space by impacts and only now drifting home. As Fedorets put it, temporary satellites are a key population for distinguishing between asteroids that wandered in from the main belt and fragments that were knocked off our own Moon.

That is the real story behind the "second moon" headlines, and it is better than the viral version. Earth does not have a second moon. It has something more interesting: a small, ever-changing crowd of fellow travellers, some of them chips off our own Moon, drifting through our neighbourhood on borrowed time — and new telescopes are about to introduce us to many more of them.

## Why This Matters Beyond the Headline

It is tempting to dismiss a "second moon" headline as harmless fun. Nobody's health or wallet depends on how many moons Earth has. But the pattern behind it matters, because it is the same pattern that distorts so many science stories. A precise technical term — quasi-satellite, temporary capture, 1:1 resonance — gets compressed into a vivid phrase, and the vivid phrase quietly changes the meaning. A rock that orbits the Sun in step with Earth becomes a moon. A two-month gravitational visit becomes a permanent companion. A 19-metre boulder becomes something you could supposedly see in the sky.

The cost is not just accuracy. When the correction arrives, as it always does, it can leave readers feeling tricked — and feeling tricked makes people less likely to believe the next real discovery. The 2025 PN7 story did not need the exaggeration. A 19-metre asteroid that has been silently pacing Earth since the 1950s, discovered only because a Hawaiian telescope happened to catch its faint glint, is a wonderful story on its own terms. So is the finding that a rock which visited us in 2024 was probably a piece of the Moon, knocked into space by an impact thousands of years ago and only now coming home. The truth, told plainly, is more interesting than the headline, and it has the advantage of being true.

For readers who love Moon lore, the record is worth keeping straight. Our Moon is not going anywhere, and it is not getting company. If you want to know what else shares our orbit, the answer is a handful of small rocks with forgettable names and remarkable stories — and if a headline tells you otherwise, check whether the object orbits the Earth or merely travels beside it. That single question separates a moon from a mirage, and it is the question the "second moon" headlines forgot to ask.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** Earth has one true Moon, and no second moon has ever been confirmed. The headlines of September 2025 were built on a real discovery — asteroid 2025 PN7, a 19-metre rock that has travelled in step with Earth since around the 1950s and will stay in that arrangement until about 2083 — but 2025 PN7 is a quasi-satellite, not a moon: it orbits the Sun, not the Earth, and it is not gravitationally bound to our planet.

What Earth does have is a small, shifting entourage of genuine companions. Temporary mini-moons like 2024 PT5 — a 10-metre chip probably blasted off the Moon itself — are captured by Earth's gravity for a few months and then released; only two, 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3, have ever been confirmed to complete a full orbit around us. Quasi-moons like Kamoʻoalewa, 40 to 100 metres across and stable for centuries, travel around the Sun in lockstep with Earth without ever orbiting it. New surveys like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory should find more of both kinds in the coming years. So the next time a headline announces a second moon, the right response is not excitement or dismissal — it is a question: does it orbit the Earth, or does it merely travel beside it?
