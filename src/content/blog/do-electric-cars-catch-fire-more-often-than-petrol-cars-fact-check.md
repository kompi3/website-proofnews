Every few months, a video of an electric car burning on a motorway or smouldering in a car park races around social media, and the same question follows it: are electric cars more likely to catch fire than petrol cars? The footage is dramatic, the smoke is thick, and the comments are certain. But the numbers behind the claim point in the opposite direction — by a very wide margin. Petrol cars catch fire far more often than electric ones. The part of the story that is true, and that deserves to be taken seriously, is different: when an EV battery does catch fire, it is a much harder fire to fight.

## The Claim

The claim, in its simplest form, is that electric cars are a fire risk — that their lithium-ion batteries make them more likely to burst into flames than the petrol cars they replace. It shows up in comment sections, in forwarded messages, and in dinner-table arguments, usually attached to a video of a burning EV. The implication is that the switch to electric driving has traded one danger for a worse one.

The claim has been around since the first mass-market EVs arrived, and it keeps finding fresh fuel. Every time a well-known EV catches fire — in a crash, in a garage, or after flood damage — the clip circulates as proof. Meanwhile, petrol car fires, which happen every day in large numbers, almost never make the news at all. That asymmetry is the first thing to understand about this claim: it is built on what we see, not on what happens.

## The Verdict

**False.** Electric cars do not catch fire more often than petrol cars. Every major dataset that compares the two — American, Swedish, Norwegian, and Australian — finds the opposite: petrol and diesel cars catch fire at rates several times to dozens of times higher than battery-electric cars. The most widely cited US comparison puts petrol cars at about 61 times the fire rate of EVs per vehicle. That does not mean EV fires are harmless or unimportant. A lithium-ion battery fire is a genuinely different and more difficult emergency than a petrol fire, and firefighters will tell you so. But "more difficult to extinguish" and "more likely to happen" are two different claims, and only the second one is what the viral posts assert. On the question asked — do they catch fire more often — the answer is no.

## What the Numbers Actually Say

The single most quoted comparison in this debate comes from an analysis by AutoinsuranceEZ, which cross-referenced data from the US National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), the Bureau of Transportation Statistics, and government recall databases. It counted fire incidents per 100,000 vehicles sold, and the result was not close:

| Powertrain | Fires per 100,000 vehicles sold | Compared with EVs |
|---|---:|---|
| Battery electric (BEV) | 25.1 | Baseline |
| Petrol / diesel (ICE) | 1,529.9 | About 61 times higher |
| Hybrid (HEV / PHEV) | 3,474.5 | About 138 times higher |

Read that table again, because it is the heart of this fact-check. For every 100,000 electric cars sold, about 25 caught fire. For every 100,000 petrol or diesel cars, about 1,530 did. Hybrids — which carry both a fuel tank and a battery pack — recorded the highest rate of all, at roughly 3,475 per 100,000, a figure that is partly explained by older hybrid models with ageing batteries and complex dual-powertrain systems.

The American numbers are striking, but a good fact-check does not rest on one dataset. The same pattern shows up in country after country:

**Sweden.** The Swedish Civil Contingencies Agency (MSB) reported that in 2022, just 23 fires occurred among roughly 611,000 electric vehicles — a rate of 0.004 percent. In the same period, about 3,400 fires occurred among approximately 4.4 million petrol and diesel vehicles, or 0.08 percent. Combustion vehicles were about 20 times more likely to catch fire than EVs in one of the world's most electrified car markets.

**Norway.** The Norwegian Directorate for Civil Protection (DSB) published propulsion-separated statistics covering 2018 to 2022, showing roughly 30 fires per 100,000 petrol and diesel vehicles each year, against roughly 5 per 100,000 electric vehicles — a ratio of about six to one. Norway matters because it has the highest EV share of any national fleet, so its data is the closest thing we have to a real-world preview of an all-electric future.

**Australia.** EV FireSafe, an Australian-government-funded project that tracks and verifies EV battery fires worldwide, estimates the EV battery-fire probability at about 0.0012 percent, against about 0.1 percent for petrol and diesel vehicles. Its verified global database, updated on 30 September 2026, now counts 1,072 battery-fire incidents studied since 2010 — a figure that has roughly doubled from the 511 it counted earlier, because the global EV fleet has grown enormously and tracking has improved. The count went up, and the conclusion did not move.

**Tesla's own fleet.** Tesla publishes vehicle fire data on its own website. Between 2012 and 2024, the company says, roughly one Tesla vehicle fire occurred for every 140 million miles travelled. By comparison, data from the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) and the US Department of Transportation indicates one vehicle fire for every 17 million miles travelled across all US vehicles — roughly an eightfold difference. Tesla also notes that its own count includes fires caused by structure fires, wildfires, and arson, while the NFPA figures exclude fires where a structure is involved, so Tesla is counting more kinds of fire and still coming out ahead. In September 2025, Tesla added a striking detail: across more than 265 billion miles of operation through the end of 2025, it said it had "no evidence of even a single case of a spontaneous battery failure leading to a vehicle fire in a Model 3, Model Y, Cybertruck or Semi."

**The United States as a whole.** The NFPA estimates that there were about 119,681 passenger-vehicle fires a year in the US on average from 2018 to 2022 — the overwhelming majority of them in petrol and diesel vehicles, because those still make up the vast bulk of the fleet. A 2023 investigation by Battelle for the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration concluded that the propensity and severity of fires involving lithium-ion battery systems are comparable to, or slightly lower than, those associated with gasoline and diesel fuels.

No dataset in circulation shows EVs catching fire more often than combustion vehicles. That is the consistent finding, across countries, across methods, and across a decade and a half of data.

## Why the Claim Feels True

If the numbers are so one-sided, why does the claim survive? Because human brains are not spreadsheets. Psychologists call it the availability heuristic: we judge how common something is by how easily examples come to mind. An EV fire is news. A petrol car fire is not.

A petrol car catches fire in the United States roughly once every two to three minutes, according to figures cited by the NFPA — so often that local news barely bothers to report it. An EV fire, by contrast, is rare, novel, and visually spectacular, so it gets filmed, shared, and discussed for days. The result is a distorted picture of the world in which the rare event feels common and the common event feels rare. Every EV fire you have ever seen in your feed was real. What was missing was the denominator: the tens of millions of EVs driving around without incident, and the hundreds of thousands of petrol cars that burned in the same period without anyone filming them.

There is also a genuine reason the footage looks so alarming. EV battery fires really are different, and the next section explains how. When people see a fire crew pouring thousands of gallons of water on a single car for hours, they reasonably conclude that something unusually dangerous is happening. They are right about the fire. They are wrong about the frequency.

The claim also benefits from a kernel of truth about hybrids. The same datasets that show pure EVs catching fire least often show hybrids catching fire most often — about 138 times the EV rate in the AutoinsuranceEZ analysis. People who lump all electrified vehicles together, or who remember headlines about hybrid recalls, can easily come away with the impression that batteries equal fire risk. But a hybrid carries a petrol tank, a fuel system, and a battery; a pure EV carries only the battery. Conflating the two is like blaming seatbelts for the crashes they survive.

## What Happens When an EV Does Catch Fire

Here is the part the viral posts get right, and it matters. A lithium-ion battery fire is not a petrol fire, and firefighters treat it differently for good reasons.

The process is called thermal runaway. If a battery cell is damaged — in a severe crash, by a manufacturing defect, or by deep water ingress, especially salt water, which can corrode and short the pack days after the flooding — it can heat up, vent flammable gases, and ignite the cells next to it in a chain reaction. The pack generates its own heat and its own oxygen inside a sealed steel box, which is why the fire can be so stubborn. EV FireSafe notes that a true vapour-cloud explosion, where vented gases accumulate in a confined space and ignite all at once, is real but rare: it has verified 14 such incidents worldwide since 2010, out of more than a thousand battery fires studied.

Fire-service testing reported by the NFPA in 2023 found that EV and petrol-car fires burn at similar temperatures, so the common line that EV fires "burn hotter" is not supported by those tests. What makes them harder is duration and reignition. A pack in thermal runaway keeps producing heat, so crews must cool it for a long time. Incidents cited by the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration typically use 3,000 to 8,000 gallons of water, and the extreme case — a 2021 crash in Texas — ran to roughly 30,000 gallons over four hours. Even after the flames are out, a damaged pack can reignite hours or even days later, which is why firefighters monitor burned EVs and why damaged cars are quarantined in salvage yards.

The NTSB examined this in its Safety Report 20/01, which looked at four EV fires and found that firefighters faced real risks from electric shock, thermal runaway, and reignition. Emergency services have responded with new training, new tactics — including letting a fire burn in a controlled way rather than fighting it directly — and new equipment. Car makers have responded too, with cell isolation, cooling systems, venting designs, and battery management systems that stop charging long before a cell is overstressed. Tesla says it can take 3,000 to 8,000 gallons of water applied directly to the pack to extinguish a battery fire, and its own responder guides warn about reignition.

None of this makes EVs more likely to catch fire. It makes the fires that do happen more demanding to deal with. That distinction is the whole story of this claim: rare but hard, not common and hard.

## The Honest Caveats

A careful verdict has to include the ways the numbers can mislead, because the claim's defenders sometimes raise real points. Here are the caveats, stated plainly.

**The fleets are different ages.** The average petrol car on American roads is roughly 12.6 years old, while most EVs have been sold since 2020. Older cars catch fire more often: fuel lines leak, wiring degrades, maintenance gets skipped. Part of the gap between EVs and petrol cars is a gap between new cars and old cars. As the EV fleet ages, its fire rate may rise — and researchers will be watching. But even age-matched comparisons do not flip the result. The Highway Loss Data Institute (HLDI), which works from US insurance claims, found in its 2021 bulletin that non-crash fire claims ran at 0.19 per 1,000 insured vehicle-years for EVs against 0.20 for petrol cars — essentially no difference once crashes are removed. That is a narrower, more careful comparison, and it still does not show EVs as worse.

**"Per vehicle sold" is not the only measure.** The AutoinsuranceEZ figures are per 100,000 vehicles sold, not per mile driven or per year on the road. Tesla's figures are per mile. Sweden's are per registered vehicle. Each measure has its own bias, and the ratios differ — 61 to one, 20 to one, six to one, eight to one — but they all point the same way. No measure reverses the direction.

**Counting is incomplete.** EV FireSafe is open about the limits of its own database: it captures only incidents researchers can verify, and only a small number of countries track EV fires nationally. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology estimated in a March 2026 analysis (Technical Note 2365) that the true total is around 5,718 EV and plug-in hybrid fires since 2011 — higher than the verified count, because verified counts miss incidents. Undercounting cuts both ways, though: petrol-car fires are also undercounted in many systems, and the verified EV count is the smaller number in every comparison.

**Not every country agrees perfectly.** Figures from Poland's State Fire Service, cited in 2025, showed EVs and petrol cars tied at 0.23 fires per 1,000 vehicles — an outlier against the pattern elsewhere, and a reminder that small fleets and different reporting rules can produce odd results. One outlier does not overturn a global pattern, but it is worth noting rather than hiding.

**Manufacturers' own data has limits.** Tesla's figures are self-reported and global, while the NFPA comparison is American. Tesla's fleet is newer, wealthier, and better maintained on average than the general car population. The company is transparent about the comparison's imperfections, and independent national datasets — Sweden, Norway, Australia — are the stronger evidence. The verdict does not depend on taking Tesla's word for it.

**EV fires can still hurt people and property.** The rarity of a fire does not make it harmless when it happens. People have been injured and killed in EV fires, usually in severe crashes where the fire was not the primary cause of death. Parking garages, car ferries, and apartment buildings have had to rethink their fire planning. The claim we are checking is about frequency, not about whether EV fires matter. They matter. They are just rare.

## Claim vs Evidence

| What the claim says | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| EVs catch fire more often than petrol cars | False. Petrol cars catch fire about 61 times more often per vehicle in the most-cited US analysis, and 6–20 times more often in Norwegian and Swedish national data |
| EV batteries spontaneously burst into flames | No evidence of a spontaneous battery failure causing a fire in Tesla's Model 3, Y, Cybertruck, or Semi across 265+ billion miles; most EV fires follow crashes, defects, or flooding |
| EV fires are impossible to put out | Difficult, not impossible. They need large volumes of water (typically 3,000–8,000 gallons) and long cooling, and can reignite — but fire services extinguish them routinely |
| Hybrids prove batteries are the problem | Hybrids have the highest fire rate, but they combine a fuel system with a battery; pure EVs have the lowest rate of all three powertrains |
| The statistics are rigged by counting only new EVs | Fleet age is a real caveat, but age-matched insurance data (HLDI 2021) still shows no EV disadvantage in non-crash fires |
| EV fires are a growing crisis as sales rise | The verified global count rose to 1,072 incidents since 2010 (EV FireSafe, September 2026), but the fleet grew far faster; the rate per vehicle remains tiny next to petrol cars |

## A Short History of the Scare

The fear of EV fires is nearly as old as the modern EV itself. When the first mass-market electric cars arrived in the early 2010s, a handful of high-profile fires — including early Tesla Model S incidents in 2013 — made global news and set the template: a burning EV, a viral video, a round of "are these cars safe?" coverage. In 2016, Samsung's Galaxy Note 7 battery fires put the words "lithium-ion" and "thermal runaway" into everyday language, even though phone batteries and car batteries are engineered very differently. The Chevrolet Bolt recall, covering model years 2017 to 2019, was a genuine manufacturing defect story — faulty cells from a supplier — and it reinforced the association between EVs and fire in the public mind, even though it affected one model, not the technology.

As EV sales grew through the 2020s, the fire data grew with them, and the picture became clearer rather than scarier. Sweden published its national figures, Norway published its, the NTSB data was analysed, and EV FireSafe built its verified global database. Each new dataset said the same thing: rarer, not more common. The claim survived anyway, because the videos kept coming and because the fires that do happen are genuinely hard to fight — a fact that gives the scare a permanent supply of fresh footage.

## What This Means for You

If you drive a petrol car, the risk of a vehicle fire is already low — about 1.5 percent of vehicles over the period measured in the US data — and most fires are survivable if you get out quickly. If you drive an EV, the risk is lower still. Neither risk should keep you awake at night, but a few practical points are worth knowing.

After any significant underbody impact, or after driving through deep water, have the battery inspected — salt water in particular can cause corrosion that leads to a fire days or weeks later. If your EV is in a crash and the battery is damaged, tell the emergency services that it is an electric vehicle, get well away from it, and do not try to extinguish a battery fire yourself. Never use water on a live high-voltage battery; leave that to the fire service. If you charge at home, use a properly installed charger rather than an extension lead, and follow the manufacturer's guidance on charging. And if you see a video of a burning EV in your feed, remember the denominator: for every EV fire you see, thousands of petrol cars burned the same year without a single camera pointed at them.

The same habit of checking the denominator is useful beyond cars. We have looked before at how a dramatic story can outrun the data — whether it is the question of <a href="/blog/do-solar-panels-work-on-cloudy-days-fact-check/">Do Solar Panels Work on Cloudy Days?</a>, where the real output figures are far less gloomy than the sceptics suggest, or <a href="/blog/do-power-lines-cause-cancer-fact-check/">Do Power Lines Cause Cancer?</a>, where decades of research have failed to find the link people fear. The pattern repeats with <a href="/blog/do-air-fryers-cause-cancer-fact-check/">Do Air Fryers Cause Cancer?</a>, where the warning labels sound alarming until you read what the studies actually measured. In each case, the fear was louder than the evidence. EV fires are the same story, told with better footage.

## The Verdict, Restated

Do electric cars catch fire more often than petrol cars? No. The evidence is consistent across the United States, Sweden, Norway, and Australia, and across every method of counting — per vehicle sold, per registered vehicle, and per mile driven. Petrol cars catch fire far more often. The honest version of the fear is narrower and worth respecting: when an EV battery does catch fire, the fire is harder to extinguish, needs far more water, and can reignite. Firefighters know this, manufacturers know this, and regulators know this. But a rare, difficult fire is not the same thing as a common one, and the claim that EVs burn more often than petrol cars is false.
