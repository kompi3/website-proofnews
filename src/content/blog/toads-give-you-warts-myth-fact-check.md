Every child who has ever crouched beside a garden pond has heard the warning, usually delivered in a tone of absolute certainty by an adult who heard it from an adult before them. Do not pick up that toad. If you touch it, you will get warts. The warning sounds like common sense. The animal is covered in bumps that look exactly like warts, so the conclusion feels obvious: touch the bumps, catch the bumps. Generations of parents have repeated it, generations of children have believed it, and generations of toads have been left alone in the damp grass as a result.

The warning is wrong. It has always been wrong. Touching a toad cannot give you warts, no matter how long you hold it, no matter how bumpy it is, and no matter how many toads you collect in a bucket on a summer afternoon. The reason is a satisfying piece of biology, and it has two halves. The first half is that the bumps on a toad are not warts at all. The second half is that human warts are caused by a virus that lives only in humans, and toads do not carry it, cannot catch it, and cannot pass it on. The myth survived for centuries not because it was true, but because it looked true, and because it was useful: a scary story is an effective way to keep small hands away from wild animals.

## The claim

The claim, in its classic form, is simple and specific. Toads have warts. If you touch a toad, the warts will spread to your skin, appearing on your hands as rough, raised bumps. Some versions of the story add a time limit, as if the warts arrive overnight, and some add a moral, as if the warts are nature's punishment for meddling with wildlife. Children, who are the most likely to pick up a toad and the most likely to get warts for entirely unrelated reasons, are the perfect audience for it. A child who handles a toad in June and develops a wart in September has all the evidence they think they need.

The claim also carries an unspoken assumption that deserves attention, because it is the assumption on which the whole myth rests. It assumes that a bump that looks like a wart is a wart, and that things that look alike behave alike. That assumption fails constantly in biology. A dolphin looks like a fish and is a mammal. A bat looks like a bird and is a mammal. A toad's bumps look like warts and are something else entirely: glands, built by evolution for defence, doing a job that has nothing to do with human skin disease.

## The Verdict

**Verdict: False.** Touching a toad does not cause warts. Human warts are caused by human papillomavirus, usually shortened to HPV, a virus that infects only humans. The bumps on a toad's skin are glands that produce defensive secretions, not viral growths, and they contain no HPV and no mechanism for creating a wart on human skin. As the Forest Preserve District of Will County puts it in its myth-busting explainer: "You won't get warts from handling a frog or toad." No amount of handling, the district adds, will cause a human to develop warts, and the same holds for any reptile or amphibian.

That verdict is not a close call or a matter of scientific debate. It is the settled position of dermatologists, museums, wildlife agencies and medical centres, and it follows directly from what warts are and what toad skin is. The rest of this article explains both, because the myth is more interesting once you see what replaced it: a story about chemical warfare, species-specific viruses, and a visual coincidence that fooled people for centuries.

## The bumps that started it all

To understand the myth, start with the toad. Pick one up, gently, and look at its back. The surface is rough, dry and studded with raised bumps of various sizes. The most prominent pair sits just behind the eyes, two large oval swellings that look like a matching set of warts. Those are the parotoid glands, and they are the toad's chemical weapons factory.

Parotoid glands are external skin glands found on the back, neck and shoulders of toads and some frogs and salamanders. They secrete a milky cocktail of alkaloid substances known collectively as bufotoxins, which act as neurotoxins to deter predators. When a predator grabs a toad, the glands release their contents, and the attacker gets a mouthful of bitter, irritating poison. Many predators learn from a single encounter and never try again. The glands are sometimes described as wart-like in appearance, but the resemblance is purely visual: warts are abnormal growths caused by viral infections, while parotoid glands are normal, healthy parts of the animals that bear them.

The smaller bumps scattered across the rest of the toad's body are more of the same machinery on a smaller scale. Clusters of skin glands produce and store toxic secretions, and their raised profiles give the toad its characteristic rough texture. Frogs tend to have smoother skin than toads, but many frog species carry similar glands, which is why the myth sometimes extends to frogs as well. None of these structures has anything to do with HPV or human warts. They are not infections, not tumours and not contagious. They are anatomy, the way a porcupine's quills are anatomy: dangerous to handle carelessly, but not a disease you can catch.

There is an instructive detail in the chemistry. Bufotoxins are genuinely potent substances, and some toad species are genuinely dangerous. The cane toad, an invasive species in Australia and parts of the Americas, produces secretions that can kill dogs that mouth them. The Colorado River toad produces a secretion containing a powerful psychoactive compound, which has attracted reckless human experimentation and occasional poisoning. These are real risks, and they are the reason wildlife agencies advise washing your hands after handling amphibians and keeping them away from your eyes and mouth. But a risk is not the same as the myth. Bufotoxins can irritate skin and mucous membranes, and they can poison a pet, but they cannot grow a wart. The myth took a real hazard, the toad's chemical defences, and attached it to the wrong disease.

## What actually causes warts

Human warts are caused by HPV, a family of viruses with more than 100 known types. Only a few of those types cause the common warts that appear on hands and fingers. The virus enters the skin through a break in the surface, a cut, a scrape, a hangnail or a patch of skin softened by moisture, and once inside it instructs skin cells to multiply faster than usual. The result is a small, grainy, rough growth, often with tiny black dots that are actually clotted blood vessels feeding the wart. It can take two to six months for a wart to appear after exposure, which is one reason the toad myth was so hard to kill: a child who handled a toad in spring and noticed a wart in autumn had months of apparent cause and effect to point to, even though the real exposure happened somewhere else entirely, probably at school, at a swimming pool or in their own home.

HPV is, crucially, a human virus. It infects humans and only humans. That species barrier is the core of the debunking. Toads do not carry HPV, they cannot be infected by it, and they cannot transmit it, because the virus's machinery is tuned to human cells. The same principle works in reverse: animals get their own warts from their own species-specific viruses. Dogs get warts from dog papillomaviruses, horses from horse papillomaviruses, and humans from human papillomaviruses, and the streams do not cross. The Indiana State Board of Animal Health makes the point with admirable bluntness: just as we do not get warts from frogs, toads or other animals, animals will not get warts from us.

Not everyone who encounters HPV develops a wart. Each person's immune system responds to the virus differently, and most adults have built up immunity to the common wart-causing types over a lifetime of low-level exposure. That is why warts are most common in children and young adults, whose immune systems are still learning the virus, and in people with weakened immune systems, such as organ transplant recipients or people living with HIV. A child is also more likely to have the small cuts and scrapes that give the virus its entry point, and more likely to spend time in exactly the places where the virus circulates: classrooms, locker rooms, swimming pools and playgrounds. The toad, in this picture, is a bystander. The wart was coming from the child's own world all along.

## How warts really spread

If toads are innocent, the obvious question is where warts actually come from. The answer is other people, and the objects they touch. Warts spread through direct skin-to-skin contact with someone who has a wart, and indirectly through shared objects and surfaces: towels, washcloths, razors, nail files, the floors of swimming pools and communal showers. The virus travels in shed skin, and shed skin is everywhere. When HPV takes hold, it grows a lump of thickened skin, and that skin sheds over time just as normal skin sheds, carrying the virus with it. If that shed skin reaches a crack or scrape in someone who has not developed immunity, a new wart can begin.

The practical advice that follows from this is the opposite of the toad warning, and considerably more useful. Do not pick at warts, your own or anyone else's. Do not share towels, nail clippers or razors. Wear flip-flops in locker rooms and around pools. Cover warts with a bandage during contact sports such as wrestling or gymnastics, where skin-to-skin contact is constant. Wash your hands often, and do not bite your nails or pick at hangnails, because both habits create exactly the small breaks in the skin that HPV needs. None of this advice involves avoiding toads, and all of it is more effective than the myth ever was.

It is worth noting one persistent misunderstanding about warts and swimming pools. Pools are often blamed, and they are not entirely innocent, since the damp, shared environment suits the virus, but there is no strong evidence that swimming pools are a major source of wart transmission. The larger risk is the shared changing area around the pool, the towels and the bare feet on warm, wet floors. The distinction matters, because it tells you where the real prevention happens: not in avoiding water, but in the small habits of hygiene around it.

## Why the myth stuck around

A myth that is simply wrong usually dies. This one survived for centuries, and the reasons it survived are worth examining, because they explain a lot about how misinformation works in general.

The first reason is the visual coincidence, and it is powerful. Humans are pattern-matching machines, and we are especially quick to match patterns on skin. A toad's back looks like a hand covered in warts. In a world before germ theory, before anyone knew what a virus was, the inference that the bumps were contagious was not foolish. It was the best available explanation, and it was reinforced every time a child who had recently handled a toad developed a wart from an unrelated exposure. The myth had a built-in confirmation mechanism: children handle toads constantly, children get warts constantly, and the two constants overlap by chance often enough to look like causation.

The second reason is folklore. Toads have been tangled up with witchcraft, poison and bad luck in European folklore for centuries. They were said to be witches' familiars, ingredients in potions, and creatures of damp, dark places. A creature already associated with curses and contagion does not need much help to acquire one more curse. The wart myth fit neatly into a ready-made reputation, and the reputation kept the myth alive long after the original observation had been forgotten.

The third reason is the myth's usefulness as a parenting tool. A warning that is vivid, specific and slightly disgusting is an effective way to keep a child from handling a wild animal. The toad warning works as behaviour management even though it fails as biology, and warnings that work tend to be repeated regardless of their truth. Parents who were told the myth as children repeat it to their children, not because they have checked it, but because it was told to them by someone they trusted, and because it seems to do no harm. The harm, if any, is subtle: a child taught to fear toads learns a false lesson about how disease works, and that false lesson can displace the true ones about handwashing and shared towels.

The fourth reason is that the correction is boring compared to the myth. "Wash your hands and do not share towels" is good advice and terrible storytelling. "That toad will give you warts" is bad advice and a great story. Misinformation often wins not because it is more persuasive, but because it is more memorable, and the toad myth is a perfect example: short, visual, disgusting, and easy to repeat at exactly the moment a child reaches for a toad.

## The one real risk from toads

Debunking the wart myth should not be read as a recommendation to handle toads carelessly. Toads carry real, if modest, risks, and they deserve to be described accurately, because accurate risk information is what replaces a myth rather than merely removing it.

The main risk is the toad's chemical defence. Skin secretions can irritate human skin, and they are considerably more troublesome if they reach the eyes or mouth. Dogs that pick up cane toads can become seriously ill, and veterinary emergencies from toad poisoning are a genuine problem in regions where cane toads are invasive. The second risk is bacterial. Amphibians can carry Salmonella and other bacteria on their skin, which is why wildlife agencies advise washing hands thoroughly with soap and water after handling any frog or toad, and why children should be supervised around them. Neither risk produces warts. Both are handled by the same simple measure: wash your hands, do not touch your face, and leave the animal alone afterwards.

There is also a risk in the other direction, to the toad. Amphibians absorb substances through their skin, and the oils, sunscreen and insect repellent on human hands can harm them. Handling a toad roughly can injure it, and removing it from its habitat disrupts its breeding and feeding. The best advice for both parties is the same: admire toads, do not collect them, and if you must move one, for example off a road, do it gently with wet hands and put it down nearby. The toad is not a danger to your skin, but it is a wild animal, and wild animals are best left wild.

## What to do about a real wart

Most warts are harmless and many disappear on their own. Left untreated, most persist for one to two years before the immune system recognises the virus and clears it, though some last longer and some resolve within months. Many people choose treatment because warts are bothersome, embarrassing or painful, particularly plantar warts on the soles of the feet, which are pressed inward by walking and can hurt with every step.

Over-the-counter treatments based on salicylic acid, applied daily over weeks, are the usual first step. Clinics offer cryotherapy, freezing the wart with liquid nitrogen, and a range of other options, including immune-based and irritant-based therapies and laser treatment, sometimes in combination. The goal of every treatment is the same: to get the immune system to recognise the wart virus and clear the infection, which can take several rounds. People with weakened immune systems may find warts particularly stubborn, and should seek medical advice early rather than persisting with home treatment.

See a doctor if a growth hurts, bleeds, burns or itches, if it persists, spreads or returns after treatment, if you are unsure whether it is a wart at all, if you have many warts, if you have a weakened immune system, or if warts appear on the face, feet or genitals. Those are the signs that separate a routine wart from something that needs professional attention. Note what is absent from that list: any mention of toads. A doctor will not ask whether you handled an amphibian, because the answer has no bearing on diagnosis or treatment.

One clarification matters for anyone reading about HPV in a broader context. The HPV types that cause common skin warts are not the same types that cause genital warts or cervical cancer. The HPV vaccine is designed to protect against the cancer-causing and genital-wart types, not against the types that cause common hand warts, though some early research suggests it may offer partial protection against skin warts as well. The vaccine is recommended for children from around ages nine to eleven for cancer prevention, and that recommendation stands entirely apart from the toad myth. Conflating the two kinds of HPV is a common source of confusion, and it is worth keeping them separate: the virus family is large, its members do different jobs, and only a few of them make warts on your hands.

## A myth worth retiring

The toad wart myth is, in the end, a small and forgiving error. Nobody has been seriously harmed by believing it, and it may even have protected a few toads from overenthusiastic children. But it is still worth retiring, for a reason that goes beyond toads. Every myth we keep is a slot in our understanding of the world occupied by something false, and false slots have a habit of crowding out true ones. A child who believes warts come from toads is a child who has not learned that warts come from a virus spread by people and shared towels, and that is the knowledge that would actually protect them.

The real story is better than the myth, anyway. A toad is not a bag of contagious warts. It is a small, warty-looking chemical factory, carrying its own poison glands as a defence against a world full of predators, surviving in damp corners on a strategy that has worked for millions of years. Its bumps are not a disease. They are a masterpiece of evolution, and they have been misread by humans for as long as humans have been looking at them. The next time a child crouches beside a garden pond and reaches for a toad, the honest warning is simpler and stranger than the old one: leave it alone, not because it will give you warts, but because it is a wild animal doing a difficult job, and because your hands, with their sunscreen and soap and everyday germs, are more of a danger to it than it will ever be to you.
