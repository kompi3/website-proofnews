Almost every coffee drinker knows the two warnings by heart. The first arrives in childhood, usually from a parent swatting away a reaching hand: "Put that down — coffee stunts your growth." The second arrives later, from a gym coach, a wellness influencer, or a well-meaning colleague: "Careful, coffee dehydrates you — you'd better drink a glass of water for every cup."

Both statements feel like science. Both have been repeated for decades. And both turn out to be wrong — but not in exactly the same way, and not without a grain of truth hiding underneath. Coffee does not stunt growth in children or teenagers, and it does not dehydrate the people who drink it daily. What coffee does do, particularly to young people, is real and measurable — it is just different from what the myths claim. This is the story of how two plausible-sounding ideas survived decades of research that said otherwise.

## The Viral Claims

The claims, in their strongest form, go like this. Claim one: drinking coffee as a child or teenager will stunt your growth — the caffeine interferes with bone development, and kids who drink coffee will end up shorter than they otherwise would have been. This is why so many parents have treated the coffee mug as a kind of parental no-go zone for minors, and why generations of kids grew up believing that one espresso would cost them an inch.

Claim two: coffee dehydrates you. The logic is tidy — caffeine is a diuretic, diuretics make you lose water, therefore coffee pulls water out of your body. Dedicated versions of the claim go further: coffee not only fails to hydrate you, it creates a net fluid deficit, so every cup must be chased with an equal glass of water. Some people extend the same reasoning to tea and, in a fuzzier way, to any caffeinated drink at all.

Both claims share a structure that makes them durable: a real biological mechanism (caffeine does affect calcium handling; caffeine does have a diuretic effect), stretched into a conclusion the evidence does not support. The myths survive because their ingredients are real. It is the recipe that is wrong.

## Where the "Stunts Your Growth" Idea Came From

Nobody knows exactly where the growth-stunting claim was born — pediatric endocrinologists quoted in the press say the origin is genuinely murky — but researchers have reconstructed a plausible family tree, and its roots reach back to the mid-20th century.

One thread begins with soda, not coffee. In the middle of the last century, as children's consumption of soft drinks was rising, some early studies noticed a link between heavy cola intake and lower bone density. Researchers at the time speculated that phosphoric acid — an ingredient in colas — might interfere with calcium absorption. That concern about a specific chemical in a specific beverage then broadened, the way folklore does, into a general anxiety about caffeine itself. The logic became: if soda might be bad for bones, and coffee contains caffeine too, then coffee must be bad for bones as well. Correlation hardened into causation somewhere along the way, and the nuance about cola's phosphoric acid got left behind.

A second thread runs through calcium. Caffeine does have a measurable effect on calcium handling: after a dose, the kidneys briefly excrete a bit more calcium in urine. For anyone reasoning at kitchen-table level — calcium builds bones, caffeine flushes calcium out, therefore caffeine weakens growing bones — this sounded airtight. The reasoning only breaks when you check the actual numbers.

A third thread comes from the animal lab. Some early animal studies, using extremely high doses of caffeine, did show reduced bone mineralization in rats. These findings were misinterpreted and generalized to humans without the necessary context. The doses fed to the rats had little relationship to the amount of caffeine a person absorbs from a cup of coffee, and humans are not overgrown rats — but the headline survived while the caveats did not.

Meanwhile, an older, larger theory was circulating in the background: that coffee causes osteoporosis, the bone-thinning condition of older age, and that osteoporosis makes people shorter. Even this reasoning collapses on inspection. Harvard Health's review of the evidence makes the point plainly: coffee does not cause osteoporosis, and osteoporosis does not, on its own, routinely make people shorter. And a 2025 review of past research actually found that long-term coffee and tea consumption might reduce the risk of osteoporosis rather than increase it.

So the growth-stunting claim was never really one idea. It was a pile of half-remembered findings — cola studies, calcium measurements, rat experiments, osteoporosis worries — assembled into a warning that sounded like it had to be true. None of it was ever tested as a claim about coffee and human height. When scientists finally did look at height directly, there was nothing there.

## What the Science Actually Says About Coffee and Height

The scientific consensus is about as clear as these things get. Harvard Health's nutrition experts state it without hedging: "There is no scientifically valid evidence to suggest that coffee can stunt a person's growth." Dr. Roy Kim, a pediatric endocrinologist at the Cleveland Clinic, answers the question he says parents have asked him for years with equal directness: "Caffeine does not meaningfully impact how tall a child gets."

Why did the calcium story fall apart? Because the size of the effect is far too small to matter. Controlled studies show that caffeine increases urinary calcium excretion for roughly three hours after a dose, and the effect scales with the dose relative to body size. But when researchers track total 24-hour urinary calcium, no net increase shows up at moderate intakes — the body appears to compensate for the small, transient losses by absorbing a bit more calcium from food later on. In younger adults consuming adequate calcium, the effect washes out entirely.

There was also a crucial confounding problem in the early studies that linked coffee to weaker bones. When researchers re-analyzed the data, they found that the people drinking the most coffee were drinking less milk and other calcium-containing beverages. It was probably the dietary pattern — low calcium and vitamin D intake among heavy coffee drinkers — not the coffee itself, that drove the association. The coffee was guilty by association, and the association was with the missing milk, not the coffee in the cup.

Timing delivers the final blow to the theory. Harvard Health notes that most growth occurs well before most people drink coffee regularly: girls usually reach close to their full height by age 15 to 17, and boys not long after. Bone growth, once complete, cannot be undone. The supposed mechanism would require caffeine to be acting during the growth window, but even then the measured effects on calcium are too weak to translate into a measurable difference in adult height. No reliable human study has ever found one.

Adult height, in the end, comes down to genetics first, with meaningful contributions from overall nutrition, sleep quality, and physical activity during the growth years. Coffee is simply not on the list of variables that matter.

## What Coffee Actually Does to Teenagers

Here is where the story turns — because while the height claim is false, the idea that coffee is harmless for kids and teens is false too. Caffeine has real, measurable effects on young bodies, and doctors are far more concerned about those effects than about anyone's height.

The single biggest concern is sleep. Caffeine delays sleep onset and reduces deep sleep, and deep sleep is precisely when a teenager's brain does some of its most important work: consolidating learning and releasing growth hormone. The irony is sharp — the hormone most directly involved in physical growth is released during the deep sleep that caffeine disrupts. Getting enough quality sleep each night will do far more for a teenager's final height than avoiding coffee ever could.

Teenagers are also more sensitive to caffeine's stimulant effects than adults. Children and adolescents may experience increased anxiety, heart palpitations, jitteriness, and mood swings at doses that would not trouble an adult. Caffeine is also an appetite suppressant — stimulants modestly lower appetite — and caffeine creates physical dependence: daily use leads to withdrawal symptoms like headaches, fatigue, and irritability when the caffeine is missed. These are legitimate, evidence-backed concerns for a developing brain, and pediatricians take them seriously.

Then there is displacement. When a teenager drinks coffee, particularly the sweetened milk-heavy versions popular today, that drink is often taking the place of water or milk — beverages that provide hydration or calcium without caffeine's downsides. The problem is not that the coffee blocks growth; it is that the coffee pushes aside the nutrients and rest the body actually needs.

Guidance reflects this. The American Academy of Pediatrics discourages caffeine intake for children and adolescents, and experts generally suggest a limit of around 100 milligrams per day for ages 12 to 18 — roughly one small 8-ounce cup of coffee — with no caffeine for younger children. For healthy adults, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration points to about 400 milligrams per day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects. One in three parents, according to a recent national poll on children's health, overestimated the recommended limit for teens — a sign of how thoroughly the real guidance has been drowned out by the myth.

So parents who withhold coffee from their children are not wrong to be cautious. They are wrong about the reason. The height warning is a folk remedy for a real problem: it gets the direction of concern approximately right while naming the wrong mechanism. Sleep disruption, anxiety, and dependence are the actual prices of early caffeine habits — not lost inches.

## The Dehydration Claim: A Real Diuretic, An Unreal Conclusion

The second myth starts from a genuine pharmacological fact: caffeine is a diuretic. It increases urine production, mainly by acting on the kidneys' handling of sodium. Nobody in the research community disputes this. The dispute is over what the diuretic effect means for the coffee drinker — and the answer is much smaller than the myth implies.

A 2015 meta-analysis of 16 studies put a number on the effect: caffeine increased urine output by about 109 milliliters on average, and even that modest effect was negated by exercise. In an acute dosing study, around 6 milligrams of caffeine per kilogram of body weight — roughly 500 milligrams or more taken at once — increased urine output over three hours at rest. But a moderate dose of around 3 milligrams per kilogram, about 250 to 300 milligrams, did not disturb fluid balance in habitual users.

Put those numbers against what a cup of coffee actually contains. Black coffee is roughly 95 percent water. A standard cup delivers about 200 milliliters of fluid while triggering, at most, a small fraction of that amount as extra urine. The arithmetic is straightforward: you take in far more water than the caffeine coaxes out. The cup is a net gain of fluid, not a net loss.

This is exactly what head-to-head studies have found. In 2014, researchers at the University of Birmingham led by Sophie Killer published a randomized, counterbalanced crossover trial in PLOS ONE — the cleanest test of the question. They studied 50 healthy men who habitually drank roughly three to six cups of coffee a day, putting each participant through both a coffee condition (four cups daily) and a water condition with matched fluid volumes over three days. The researchers measured total body water, 24-hour urine volume, urine concentration, body mass, and blood-based hydration markers. The results were nearly identical: total body water measured 51.5 kilograms on coffee versus 51.4 kilograms on water; 24-hour urine volume was 2,409 milliliters after coffee versus 2,428 milliliters after water. No statistically significant difference appeared on any measure. In plain terms, moderate coffee intake hydrated the habitual drinkers about as well as water did.

Two years later, Ronald Maughan and colleagues formalized the comparison with the Beverage Hydration Index, published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. They gave volunteers a range of common drinks and tracked how much fluid the body retained over the following hours. Coffee's hydration index came out statistically the same as water's. The mild diuretic pull of the caffeine was simply offset by the fluid volume of the beverage itself.

Major health bodies have converged on the same conclusion. The Mayo Clinic states that caffeine's mild diuretic effect does not appear to lead to dehydration or to a meaningful fluid deficit, and that caffeinated beverages can contribute to total daily water intake. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine concluded that caffeinated beverages contribute to daily water intake in the same way noncaffeinated beverages do. Nobody needs to "add back" a glass of water for a normal cup of coffee.

## Why Your Body Adapts

One of the most important factors in the dehydration story is tolerance, and it explains why studies on habitual drinkers are so decisive. The body adapts to regular caffeine exposure quickly — within days of consistent consumption, the kidneys become less responsive to caffeine's influence on sodium handling. This is why research on regular coffee drinkers consistently shows no hydration disadvantage compared with water.

Someone who rarely drinks coffee and then downs a large cup may notice they urinate a bit more than usual. Even then, the fluid in the coffee still contributes a net positive to hydration; the extra urine output is temporary and modest. And the effect nearly disappears during exercise, when the body's water-conservation systems override caffeine's gentle nudge to the kidneys.

There are, of course, boundaries. Very high single doses of caffeine — the territory of multiple energy drinks consumed rapidly — can increase urine output meaningfully at rest. People with certain medical conditions, people taking diuretic medications, and anyone working or exercising in extreme heat should treat caffeine with more care, since any additional fluid loss matters more in those settings. But those are edge cases. For the ordinary habit of a few cups a day, the dehydration warning is noise.

It is also worth noting what coffee is not: it is not "better than water." Drinks that genuinely retain fluid better than water tend to contain electrolytes or nutrients that slow fluid passage through the gut — milk, oral rehydration solutions, that sort of thing. Coffee does not outperform water at hydration; it merely matches it. The myth overshoots in one direction by claiming coffee dehydrates; the correction should not overshoot in the other.

## Why These Myths Refuse to Die

If the evidence is this one-sided, why do both claims still circulate? Part of the answer is that each myth does useful social work. The growth warning gives parents a vivid, memorable reason to keep stimulants away from children — and the real reasons (sleep architecture, anxiety, dependence) are abstract and hard to explain to a ten-year-old. "It'll stunt your growth" fits in a single sentence. "It disrupts the slow-wave sleep during which growth hormone pulses occur and to which adolescent cognition is especially sensitive" does not.

The dehydration warning does similar work for moderation: it gives the heavy coffee drinker a nagging reason to drink water too. There are worse pieces of health advice than "drink some water," and the myth persists partly because its prescription is harmless even when its mechanism is wrong.

Repetition does the rest. Once a claim is embedded in parental folklore and wellness culture, correcting it requires people to encounter the correction — and corrections are never as quotable as the myth. Academic research debunked the growth claim decades ago, but academic research does not sit at the breakfast table. The myth does.

## The Verdict

Our verdict is **Misleading** — because these two claims bundle a falsehood with a half-truth, and each needs its own ruling.

Claim one — coffee stunts your growth — is **false**. There is no scientifically valid evidence that coffee or caffeine affects height in children or teenagers, and no established biological mechanism by which it could. The calcium-loss mechanism behind the myth is real but far too small to matter, the early bone studies were confounded by coffee drinkers consuming less milk, and adult height is determined by genetics, nutrition, sleep, and activity — not by coffee. This part of the claim should be retired permanently.

Claim two — coffee dehydrates you — is **mostly false with a kernel of truth**. Caffeine is genuinely a mild diuretic, and very high doses or sudden intake in caffeine-naive people can increase urine output. But a normal cup of coffee is about 95 percent water, the diuretic effect is small and temporary, tolerance develops within days, and controlled trials show habitual coffee drinkers stay just as hydrated as water drinkers. You do not need to chase every cup with a glass of water.

The honest version of both warnings is less catchy but more useful: coffee will not make you shorter, and it will not dry you out — but it will keep a teenager up at night, it can raise anxiety, and it forms habits that are worth watching. The myths survive because they gesture at real concerns while naming the wrong ones. Retire the myths; keep the caution.

## The Last Word

The next time someone warns you that coffee will stunt your growth or dehydrate you, you can give them the short version: the science checked, and neither claim survived. What survived is the reminder underneath them — that caffeine is a real drug with real effects, especially on young, developing bodies, and that it deserves respect rather than folklore. Drink your coffee. Drink your water too. Just do it for the right reasons.
