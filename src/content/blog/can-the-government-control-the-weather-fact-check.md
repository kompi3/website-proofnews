Every time a powerful hurricane makes landfall, a familiar claim surges back across social media: the storm was not natural at all. It was steered, strengthened, or even created by the government, using a secret facility in Alaska, a fleet of spraying aircraft, or some classified technology that the public is not allowed to see. After Hurricanes Helene and Milton struck the southeastern United States in 2024, posts blaming government weather machines racked up millions of views, and meteorologists found themselves answering questions about radar images, cloud patterns, and antenna arrays in Alaska that they had debunked many times before.

The claim feels plausible to many people for one uncomfortable reason: it contains a grain of truth. Governments really have tried to modify the weather, and one government really did use it as a weapon of war. But the gap between what has actually been done — nudging a little extra rain out of clouds that were already there — and what the viral posts allege — steering hurricanes like remote-controlled cars — is enormous. It is the difference between blowing on a candle and claiming you can command a forest fire. This article separates the documented history from the fantasy, and explains why atmospheric scientists are so confident about where the line falls.

## The Claim Spreading Online

The modern version of the weather-control claim usually comes in one of three forms. The first blames HAARP, the High-frequency Active Auroral Research Program, a research facility near Gakona, Alaska, whose array of antennas beams radio waves into the upper atmosphere. Posts claim the facility can heat the sky, redirect the jet stream, manufacture hurricanes, trigger earthquakes, or hold a "heat dome" over a continent. The second form blames cloud seeding, the practice of spraying substances such as silver iodide into clouds to encourage rain, and treats every unusual flood or drought as evidence that seeding went wrong or was used as a weapon. The third form is vaguer and therefore harder to refute: unnamed government programs, hidden patents, and classified technology that supposedly do things the public science cannot explain.

These claims spike after disasters, and that timing matters. When a hurricane devastates a community, the human mind searches for an agent to blame — a person, an agency, a machine — because a random, indifferent atmosphere feels harder to accept than a villain with a plan. Conspiracy researchers have long noted that weather-control theories flourish precisely where the damage is worst and the science is hardest to visualize. The posts also borrow credibility from real things: HAARP is a real facility, cloud seeding is a real practice, and, as we will see, one government really did wage a secret weather war. Each of those facts is then stretched far beyond what the evidence supports.

It is worth noting how this pattern resembles other viral claims this site has examined. The idea that aircraft trails are a secret spraying program, covered in our fact-check of <a href="/blog/chemtrails-contrails-fact-check/">chemtrails and contrails</a>, follows the same template: take a real atmospheric phenomenon, add secrecy, and conclude a hidden program. The moon landing conspiracy, examined in our fact-check of <a href="/blog/moon-landing-faked-fact-check/">whether the moon landing was faked</a>, similarly relies on the argument that because a government once kept secrets, any secret is therefore possible. And the claim that vaccines contain tracking microchips, covered in our fact-check of <a href="/blog/vaccines-microchip-trackers-fact-check/">vaccines and microchip trackers</a>, shows how a real technology can be inflated into an all-powerful one. In each case, the right question is not whether something exists, but what it can actually do — and that question has a measurable answer.

## What HAARP Actually Is

HAARP is a scientific research facility that studies the ionosphere, the electrically charged layer of the upper atmosphere, roughly 60 to 1,000 kilometres above the Earth's surface. It was built in the 1990s with funding from the United States Air Force, Navy, and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, and since 2015 it has been operated by the University of Alaska Fairbanks as a civilian research site. Its main instrument is an array of 180 antennas that transmit high-frequency radio waves upward, briefly exciting small patches of the ionosphere so scientists can study how solar activity affects radio communications, navigation, and radar systems. The facility has held open houses for the public, and its experiments are published in the open scientific literature.

The key fact about HAARP is where it operates. Weather — clouds, rain, hurricanes, heatwaves — happens in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere, from the surface up to roughly 10 to 15 kilometres, and to a lesser extent in the stratosphere above it. HAARP's transmissions are aimed far above both of those layers, at altitudes of around 100 kilometres and higher. According to the facility's own frequently asked questions, the radio waves it transmits are not absorbed in either the troposphere or the stratosphere, the two levels of the atmosphere that produce Earth's weather. As the facility puts it, since there is no interaction, there is no way to control the weather. Independent experts say the same thing: the associate director of Boston College's Institute for Scientific Research, Keith Groves, has said there is no credible mechanism by which HAARP can modify the weather or the neutral atmosphere in any detectable way, calling such claims completely unfounded.

The energy numbers make the point even more plainly. HAARP's transmitter radiates around 3.6 megawatts of power into the thin upper atmosphere. Dr Ciaran Beggan of the British Geological Survey has pointed out that this is less power than a single large wind turbine, rated at about 5 megawatts, can produce turning in the breeze. A hurricane, by contrast, releases energy on a scale that dwarfs the entire human energy supply: a mature hurricane converts heat from warm ocean water into wind and rain at a rate often estimated in the hundreds of trillions of watts. The comparison is not close, and it is not a matter of needing a slightly bigger antenna. It is a difference of many orders of magnitude, like comparing a candle to a wildfire and suggesting that with enough candles you could steer the blaze.

There is also a timing problem. HAARP's heating of the ionosphere lasts for brief periods, and the effects dissipate quickly. Weather systems persist for days and span thousands of kilometres. No published research has demonstrated a mechanism by which a brief, localized disturbance in the ionosphere, tens of kilometres above the weather, could propagate downward and reorganize a storm. A 2017 paper in the Journal of Geophysical Research on ionospheric heaters found no downward propagation to the troposphere, and the American Meteorological Society's position is that weather modification is limited to small-scale cloud seeding, not large-scale steering — and HAARP is not involved in cloud seeding at all.

## The Energy Problem: Why Hurricanes Cannot Be Steered

To understand why scientists dismiss hurricane-steering claims so firmly, it helps to think about what a hurricane actually is. A hurricane is not an object that can be pushed; it is a heat engine. Warm, moist air rises from the ocean surface, cools and condenses as it climbs, and releases enormous amounts of latent heat, which drives the winds faster, which evaporate more water, which feeds the cycle again. The storm is the atmosphere reorganizing itself over an area the size of a large country, powered by the Sun's heating of the oceans over weeks. Steering such a system would require altering the temperature and pressure patterns across a comparable area — essentially re-engineering the weather of an entire region — and doing so continuously for days.

This is why the United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, NOAA, issued an unusually blunt fact-check in October 2024, after Helene and Milton. NOAA stated that no technology exists that can create, destroy, modify, strengthen or steer hurricanes in any way, shape or form, and that all hurricanes are natural phenomena that form on their own when ocean and atmospheric conditions align. The agency added that it does not modify the weather, does not fund or participate in cloud seeding, and has no authority to regulate weather modification by others — though a 1972 law does require anyone planning weather modification in the United States to report it to NOAA at least ten days in advance. That reporting requirement is itself useful evidence: weather modification activities are documented, not secret, and the public record shows a small number of modest cloud-seeding operations, not storm-steering programs.

The same energy argument disposes of the earthquake and volcano versions of the claim. Earthquakes release energy stored over decades or centuries as tectonic plates grind against each other, and the forces involved are vastly larger than anything a radio antenna array can produce. Seismologists note that no peer-reviewed research has established any physical mechanism by which heating the ionosphere could influence fault behaviour deep in the Earth's crust. The ionosphere and the crust are separated by the entire atmosphere, the oceans, and kilometres of rock, and nothing in the physics connects them.

None of this means scientists claim to understand the atmosphere perfectly. Weather forecasting remains difficult precisely because the atmosphere is a chaotic system: small differences in starting conditions grow into large differences in outcome, which is why a five-day forecast is less reliable than a one-day forecast. But chaos cuts against the conspiracy claim, not for it. If meteorologists with supercomputers and satellites cannot reliably predict exactly where a hurricane will go five days out, the idea that someone is secretly steering it with precision is even less plausible. Control requires far more knowledge and power than prediction, and prediction is already hard.

## The One Thing That Is Real: Cloud Seeding

Cloud seeding is the one genuine form of weather modification in regular use, and it is important to describe it accurately, because both its supporters and its critics tend to exaggerate it. The technique involves dispersing substances — most commonly silver iodide, but also dry ice, potassium iodide, or hygroscopic salts — into clouds that already contain supercooled water droplets. Silver iodide has a crystal structure similar to ice, so it gives water droplets something to freeze onto, encouraging the formation of ice crystals that can grow heavy enough to fall as snow or rain. Seeding is delivered by aircraft flying through clouds or by ground-based generators that release particles into the wind, to be carried up into suitable clouds over mountains.

Two limits define the technique. First, it cannot make rain from a clear sky. Seeding only works on clouds that are already present, already moist, and already close to producing precipitation on their own. If there are no suitable clouds, there is nothing to seed. Second, the effect is modest. The World Meteorological Organization, the American Meteorological Society, and the Weather Modification Association have all published assessments suggesting that orographic cloud seeding — seeding clouds over mountains, where the effect is best documented — can increase precipitation by roughly 10 percent in favourable conditions. Other careful studies find less. A numerical evaluation of a decade of operational seeding over Wyoming's Wind River Range, covering 190 seeded storms over ten cold seasons, found that precipitation in the target area increased by just 1.10 percent, plus or minus 0.13 percent, with a small number of storms producing most of the gain and many seedings done at unsuitable times contributing almost nothing.

That modest, conditional effect is consistent across the literature. An Australian study in the Snowy Mountains found a 14 percent increase in precipitation during seeded periods compared with unseeded ones, while India's long-running CAIPEEX experiment reported that seeded clouds produced about 46 percent more rain on average than unseeded ones in trials over Solapur — a large-sounding figure that applies to specific clouds under specific conditions, not to regional rainfall totals. In October 2025, Delhi conducted two cloud-seeding trials with the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, hoping to induce rain and clear smog; no rainfall was triggered because atmospheric conditions were unfavourable, and only light drizzle was reported. The pattern is consistent: sometimes seeding helps a little, sometimes it does nothing at all, and it never creates a storm.

Cloud seeding is also used in ways that are entirely mundane. In the western United States, private companies and water districts seed winter clouds over mountain basins to add snowpack that will melt into reservoirs in spring. In the desert southwest, seeding aims to replenish water supplies in summer. China operates the world's largest program, and has used seeding around major events — Beijing's 2008 Olympics are the famous example — to try to clear rain from the skies over the games, though how much of the dry weather was due to seeding and how much to luck remains undocumented. None of these programs involves steering storms, creating droughts, or attacking other countries. They are small, local nudges to clouds that already exist, and their results are debated even among their own practitioners.

## Operation Popeye: When a Government Really Did Try

Here is the part of the story that makes the conspiracy feel credible, and it deserves to be told straight, because it is genuinely remarkable. During the Vietnam War, the United States military ran a secret, presidentially approved program to modify the weather as a weapon. Operation Popeye, also known under the names Motorpool and Intermediary-Compatriot, ran from 1967 to 1972. Its goal, stated plainly in Department of Defense planning documents, was to produce sufficient rainfall along the Ho Chi Minh Trail to interdict or at least interfere with truck traffic between North and South Vietnam — to soften roads, trigger landslides, wash out river crossings, and extend the monsoon season by weeks, turning supply routes to mud.

The operation was large by cloud-seeding standards and tiny by weather-control standards. United States Air Force WC-130 aircraft, flying from Udorn Royal Thai Air Force Base in Thailand, released silver iodide flares into moisture-heavy monsoon clouds over Laos, Cambodia, and parts of Vietnam. More than 2,600 sorties were flown and roughly 47,000 seeding units expended over five years, at a cost later put at about 21.6 million dollars. The program was approved by Presidents Lyndon Johnson and Richard Nixon, and it was kept so secret that even the Secretary of Defense, Melvin Laird, testified to Congress in April 1972 that the United States had never engaged in weather modification over Vietnam — a statement that turned out to be wrong because the compartmentalized program had kept even him from knowing its full scope.

How well did it work? Even the military's own assessments were mixed. In a 1967 memorandum, a State Department official described the test phase as outstandingly successful, and one account claimed that 82 percent of seeded clouds produced rain quickly. But when military officials briefed Congress in 1974, they downplayed the impact, saying the most that could be claimed was a 10 percent increase in local rainfall, and even that was unverifiable. General William Westmoreland thought there was no appreciable increase in rain from the project. The honest summary, offered by historians of the program, is that Popeye achieved a measurable but modest and localized rainfall increase — exactly the kind of small nudge that cloud seeding can produce — using techniques that remain far short of controlling large weather systems. It is real evidence that a government weaponized weather at a small tactical scale, not evidence of the sweeping capabilities attributed to weather machines online.

The program's exposure changed international law. Journalist Jack Anderson published columns in 1971 and 1972 drawing on leaked documents, Senator Claiborne Pell pressed the Defense Department for answers, and the resulting pressure led to the Environmental Modification Convention, known as ENMOD, approved by the United Nations General Assembly in 1976 and entering into force in 1978. ENMOD prohibits the military or hostile use of environmental modification techniques — deliberately manipulating weather patterns, ocean currents, or other aspects of the natural environment as a weapon of war — and it has been ratified by more than 70 countries, including the United States. The treaty does not ban peaceful uses such as drought-relief cloud seeding, but it draws a clear legal line: using the environment itself as a weapon against another nation is illegal under international law.

There is a lesson in Popeye that cuts both ways. On one hand, it proves that governments have kept weather programs secret, which is why dismissing every weather claim with "the government would never do that" is historically naive. On the other hand, it shows the limits of what secrecy can hide and what the technology can do. Popeye involved thousands of aircraft sorties over five years, cost millions of dollars, and still produced, at best, a 10 percent local rainfall increase that its own commanders doubted. If that is what a five-year, presidentially approved, unlimited-budget military program achieved in the 1960s and 1970s, the idea that a single facility in Alaska is now quietly steering hurricanes requires believing in a technological leap of many orders of magnitude, achieved in secret, with no leaks, no whistleblowers, and no peer-reviewed evidence.

## Recent Flashpoints: Helene, Milton, and the Texas Floods

The most recent wave of weather-control claims followed real disasters, and each one was checked by meteorologists and fact-checkers in real time. After Hurricane Helene struck the southeastern United States in September 2024, posts claimed the storm had been steered into specific communities, sometimes pointing to radar imagery or to the timing of cloud-seeding operations. NOAA's fact-check addressed these claims directly, noting that the agency does not modify weather and that no technology can steer a hurricane. Reuters fact-checked claims that HAARP caused flooding in New Mexico, and found no connection. Full Fact, the United Kingdom's independent fact-checker, examined social media posts in July 2026 claiming that HAARP was behind heatwaves in the UK and Europe, and concluded that the facility cannot control or manipulate the weather — a finding it has repeated many times, including for claims that HAARP created distinctive clouds, which the facility cannot do because it does not produce water in the atmosphere and does not interact with clouds.

Similar claims followed the devastating flash floods in Texas in July 2025, when some posts blamed cloud seeding for the deluge. Meteorologists who examined the event pointed out that the flooding resulted from heavy storm convergence over already flood-prone terrain, and that cloud seeding can only enhance rainfall by a modest percentage in suitable clouds — nowhere near enough to create a catastrophic flood from an ordinary storm. CBS News debunked the seeding claims, noting that the amounts involved were far too small to explain the disaster. The episode had a darker consequence that is often overlooked: weather-modification conspiracy theories have led to real-world harassment of seeding companies and death threats against meteorologists and researchers, as documented in reporting on the aftermath of the 2024 hurricanes. When a false claim targets the people who study the weather, it can make the public less safe, not more, by driving experts out of public communication at exactly the moment their expertise is most needed.

There is also a political dimension that keeps the claims alive. In 2025, Florida's attorney general warned against geoengineering and cloud seeding, and legislators in several states pushed bans on weather modification with little scientific foundation, while figures such as Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene have promoted weather-control claims publicly. These political moves create a feedback loop: official attention makes the claims seem more credible, which generates more posts, which generates more political attention. But no state ban has produced evidence of a secret storm-steering program, because the bans target the modest, documented practice of cloud seeding, not the fantastical machines of the viral posts.

## Why the Secrecy Argument Fails

One of the most persistent defences of the weather-control claim is that the real program is classified, so the absence of evidence is itself evidence of a cover-up. This argument is worth examining, because it is unfalsifiable: any evidence against the claim can be dismissed as part of the cover-up, and any lack of evidence can be presented as proof of how good the cover-up is. But history shows that large secret programs do leak, and they leak precisely because they require many people, large budgets, and visible infrastructure.

Operation Popeye itself is the counterexample. It was one of the most tightly held secrets of the Vietnam War, compartmentalized so strictly that the Secretary of Defense did not know about it — and it still leaked, through a journalist working from classified documents, and was then confirmed in sworn Senate testimony. The Manhattan Project, the largest secret program in American history, involved more than 100,000 people and still leaked to the Soviet Union. A global weather-control program would require aircraft, satellites, ground stations, research teams, and budgets, all operating continuously, across multiple countries, for years. It would also have to work: the operators would need to demonstrate, internally at least, that their machine reliably steered storms, which would require tests, measurements, and records. None of that has ever surfaced — no whistleblower with documents, no leaked budget line, no defector with technical details, no satellite imagery of a storm being steered. What has surfaced, repeatedly, is the opposite: official denials backed by physics, and fact-checks backed by data.

There is also a simpler test. If a government could control the weather, it would use that power openly, or at least visibly, for its own benefit. Droughts cost the United States billions of dollars a year. Wildfires destroy entire towns. Hurricanes kill people and level cities. A government with a working weather machine would not need to hide it while its own citizens drowned in floods and burned in wildfires; it would end the drought in California, steer hurricanes away from Florida, and bring rain to Texas. The fact that governments demonstrably cannot do these things — that they spend billions on disaster relief precisely because they cannot prevent the disasters — is itself strong evidence that the machine does not exist.

## What Would Change Our Minds

A fair fact-check should say what evidence would overturn its verdict, and in this case the bar is clear. A peer-reviewed study demonstrating a physical mechanism by which ionospheric heating could reorganize tropospheric weather, with reproducible measurements, would change the picture. So would a documented, independently verified case of a hurricane's track deviating from every forecast model in a way consistent with artificial steering, with a plausible energy source identified. So would a whistleblower with verifiable technical documents, comparable to the Pentagon Papers or the Snowden disclosures, showing a working storm-steering program. None of these has appeared, despite decades of claims, despite the fact that HAARP's experiments are published openly, and despite the fact that thousands of atmospheric scientists worldwide study the sky with instruments far more sensitive than any conspiracy theorist's radar screenshot.

It is also worth distinguishing weather control from the broader field of geoengineering, which is sometimes folded into the same claims. Geoengineering proposals — such as injecting reflective particles into the stratosphere to cool the planet, or brightening marine clouds to reflect sunlight — are real research topics, debated openly in the scientific literature. But they are proposals for gradually influencing global climate over years, not for steering individual storms, and they face the same energy and scale problems at the local level. The existence of geoengineering research does not imply that anyone can aim a hurricane at a city, any more than the existence of climate science implies that anyone can order sunshine for a picnic.

## The Verdict

Can the government control the weather? Not in the way the viral claim means. No government, agency, or facility can create, destroy, strengthen, or steer a hurricane. HAARP cannot manipulate the weather: its radio waves are not absorbed in the atmospheric layers where weather forms, its power output is smaller than a single large wind turbine, and no credible mechanism connects its work to storms, droughts, or earthquakes. The claim that secret machines are steering storms and manufacturing disasters is false, and it has been debunked by NOAA, by independent fact-checkers, and by the basic physics of energy and scale.

But the verdict is Misleading rather than simply False, because the claim is built around a real kernel. Cloud seeding is a genuine, documented practice: governments and private companies really do spray silver iodide into suitable clouds to encourage a little extra rain or snow, and the United States military really did run a secret cloud-seeding war in Southeast Asia in the 1960s and 1970s. Those facts are real, and they are why the claim feels credible. What is misleading is the leap from those facts to the conclusion that governments can control the weather. Cloud seeding can nudge rainfall by perhaps 10 percent in favourable conditions, and sometimes by nothing at all. It cannot make rain from a clear sky, cannot create a hurricane, cannot steer one, and cannot manufacture a flood. The distance between seeding a cloud and steering a storm is not a matter of degree; it is a difference in kind, measured in orders of magnitude of energy.

So the next time a hurricane makes landfall and the posts begin, ask the question that cuts through the noise: how much energy would it take? A hurricane releases more energy in a day than humanity generates in months. No antenna array, no fleet of seeding aircraft, and no secret program has ever demonstrated even a tiny fraction of that power, aimed with the precision the claim requires. The weather is not controlled. It is studied, forecast with growing skill, and sometimes — modestly, locally, and only when the clouds cooperate — nudged. That is the truth, and it is remarkable enough without the conspiracy.

The sky above us is the most watched, most measured, most argued-over part of the planet. Satellites track every storm from birth to landfall, thousands of weather stations record every gust and downpour, and the data is public. If someone were steering the weather, the evidence would not be a blurry radar screenshot on social media. It would be in the data, visible to every meteorologist on Earth. It is not there. The storms are natural, the science is open, and the only weather machine that has ever worked is the modest, imperfect, and entirely unsecret practice of giving a cloud a little push — and hoping the cloud agrees.
