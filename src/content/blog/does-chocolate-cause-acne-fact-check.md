It is one of the oldest warnings in the parenting playbook: put down the chocolate bar, or you will wake up covered in pimples. Teenagers have heard it from parents, grandparents, and well-meaning aunts for generations, and social media has given the claim a second life — usually as a confident before-and-after post blaming a single bar of chocolate for a sudden breakout. The advice feels like common sense. Chocolate is sweet, greasy, and indulgent, and acne is a visible, embarrassing condition that people desperately want to control. A villain this convenient is hard to give up.

But does chocolate cause acne? The honest answer is more complicated than either side of the argument usually admits. After more than fifty years of research, scientists have not found that chocolate causes acne in everyone — yet a string of small, careful trials has found that chocolate can make existing acne worse in people who are already prone to it. That distinction matters, because the claim most people actually hear — that eating chocolate will give you spots — overstates what the evidence supports. This is the story of how a 1969 experiment settled the question for a generation, how modern trials quietly reopened it, and why dermatologists still will not tell you to ban chocolate outright.

## The Claim

The claim, in its most common form, is simple: eating chocolate causes acne. Sometimes it is phrased as a warning — chocolate will give you pimples. Sometimes it is phrased as a cure in reverse — quit chocolate and your skin will clear up. Either way, it treats chocolate as a direct cause of spots, the way a virus causes a cold.

The idea took hold in the 1960s, when acne was widely blamed on diet in general and on "junk food" in particular. Greasy food, sugary food, chocolate — all of it was lumped together as the kind of eating that supposedly showed up on your face. The belief was so widespread that it shaped how dermatologists talked to patients for decades, and it still shapes the advice teenagers get at home today. Search social media now and you will find thousands of posts, videos, and comment threads insisting that a chocolate binge caused a breakout, or that cutting chocolate out cleared someone's skin in a week.

But a claim that feels true is not the same as a claim that is true. Acne is one of the most common skin conditions in the world — it affects more than 50 million people in the United States each year, including about 85 percent of adolescents, according to figures cited in the American Academy of Dermatology's guidelines. With numbers that large, almost everyone has a personal anecdote about food and spots. Anecdotes, though, are exactly where this story gets tangled: people notice the chocolate bar they ate yesterday and forget the hormonal cycle, the stress, the new moisturizer, and the week of poor sleep that came with it.

## What Acne Actually Is

Before judging chocolate, it helps to know what acne is. Acne vulgaris is a chronic inflammatory condition of the pilosebaceous unit — the hair follicle and its attached oil gland. Dermatologists describe four factors working together: excess sebum (skin oil) production, abnormal shedding of skin cells inside the follicle, colonization by the bacterium *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly known as *Propionibacterium acnes*), and inflammation.

Hormones sit at the center of the process. Androgens — hormones that rise sharply during puberty — stimulate the oil glands to produce more sebum. That extra oil, mixed with dead skin cells, can clog a pore. Bacteria thrive in the clogged, oxygen-poor environment, the immune system responds, and the result is a blackhead, a whitehead, or an inflamed red bump. Genetics, stress, and some medications can all tip the balance. Diet, if it matters at all, matters by nudging one of these four factors — most plausibly by raising insulin and a related growth factor called IGF-1, which in turn can stimulate oil production and inflammation.

That mechanism is the thread running through the entire chocolate debate. Nobody serious argues that chocolate clogs pores from the outside, the way grease on the skin around the mouth might. The question is whether something in chocolate — the cocoa, the sugar, the milk, or the fat — pushes the hormonal and inflammatory levers that drive acne from the inside. And that is a much harder question to answer than a parental warning suggests.

## The 1969 Study That Settled It — Or Seemed To

The reason chocolate was declared innocent for so long is a single famous experiment. In 1969, researchers led by Dr. James Fulton at the University of Pennsylvania published a study in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* that tested chocolate directly. Teenagers with acne were given either real chocolate bars or chocolate-free bars designed to taste like chocolate, and neither the participants nor the researchers knew who got which. After several weeks, there was no difference in acne between the two groups. As dermatology professor Dr. Irwin Braverman of Yale later summarized the result, "The variation in the acne and induction of acne lesions was no greater in the chocolate group than in the nonchocolate group."

The Fulton study became the standard answer. Textbooks cited it, dermatologists quoted it, and for decades the official line was that chocolate had nothing to do with acne. WebMD's summary of the debate still reports the older consensus bluntly: chocolate has no link to acne, and neither do other frequently blamed foods such as pizza and potato chips.

But the 1969 study had weaknesses that modern researchers have been pointing out for years. It enrolled only 65 people, and it ran for a short period. More awkwardly, the study was supported by the Chocolate Manufacturers Association of the United States — the industry body with the most to gain from a "chocolate is innocent" result. That does not make the findings wrong, but it is the kind of funding detail that would raise eyebrows today. And the study's design had a subtle flaw that critics keep returning to: the "chocolate-free" control bars were made to mimic chocolate, and both bars contained similar amounts of sugar and fat. If sugar or fat were the real culprits, the experiment was designed in a way that could not have detected it, because both groups were eating them.

For a generation, though, Fulton 1969 was enough. The case was closed, the textbooks were written, and parents who warned their teenagers about chocolate were told they were repeating a myth.

## The Trials That Reopened the Case

The case did not stay closed. Beginning in 2011, a series of small but carefully designed trials began finding something the 1969 study had missed — and this time, the researchers went out of their way to separate cocoa from sugar and milk.

The first came in 2011, when researchers gave young men with a history of acne capsules containing either 100 percent pure cocoa or a matched placebo. There was no sugar, no milk, and no chocolate bar involved at all — just cocoa powder hidden in a capsule, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled design. Within one week, the group taking cocoa showed significantly more acne lesions than the placebo group. The study was tiny — only ten men — but its design was the point: if cocoa itself did nothing, the capsules should have done nothing. They did not.

A follow-up in 2014 used the same capsule approach with 14 men aged 18 to 35 who had a history of acne. Again the cocoa was unsweetened and hidden, so nobody knew what they were taking. Lesion counts rose by day four and again by day seven, and the more cocoa the men consumed, the more lesions appeared — a dose-dependent pattern, even if the correlation was only weak to medium. Because the cocoa was unsweetened and the participants were blinded, the result cannot easily be blamed on sugar, on milk, or on the power of suggestion.

Then came the most widely quoted study of all. In 2016, researchers at University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center ran a single-blind randomized crossover study of 54 college students with an average age of 21.4. One group ate a 43-gram Hershey's milk chocolate bar; the other ate jelly beans chosen to deliver the same glycemic load — the same sugar hit, without the cocoa. After 48 hours, a dermatologist who did not know which group anyone was in counted their lesions. Four weeks later, the groups swapped. The chocolate eaters developed significantly more acne lesions than the jelly bean eaters, a difference that was highly statistically significant. The study's lead author, Dr. Gregory R. Delost, put the result in plain language: "We found that, on average, people had about five more pimples with the ingestion of chocolate." He added that five extra pimples might sound trivial, but "if someone is getting ready for their high school dance … then five pimples is definitely clinically relevant in that situation."

A second 2016 study, published in the *International Journal of Dermatology*, tested dark chocolate specifically. Twenty-five acne-prone men ate 25 grams of 99 percent dark chocolate every day for four weeks — virtually no sugar, no milk. Acne scores, blackheads, and inflamed bumps rose significantly by week two and stayed higher at week four. The study had no control group and enrolled only men, which limits what it can prove, but it pointed in the same direction as the others: something about chocolate, beyond sugar and dairy, seemed to aggravate acne in susceptible people.

The largest and most recent trial arrived in 2024. Published in the journal *Foods*, it was a crossover study of 92 people with acne who ate 50 grams of 85 percent dark chocolate a day for four weeks while following an anti-inflammatory diet. Acne severity rose by about one point on a five-point scale during the chocolate weeks — from roughly 2.5 to 3.4 — in both groups. In one group of 51 participants, 42 saw their acne get worse and only three improved; in the second group of 41, 37 got worse. It was the clearest demonstration yet that the pattern was real, at least in people who already had acne.

## What the Trials Found, Side by Side

| Study | Year | Who took part | What they ate | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fulton et al., *JAMA* | 1969 | 65 teenagers with acne | Chocolate bars vs. chocolate-free bars | No difference in acne between groups |
| Block et al., *J Am Acad Dermatol* | 2011 | 10 young men, acne history | Pure cocoa capsules vs. placebo, 1 week | Significantly more lesions with cocoa |
| Caperton et al., *J Clin Aesthet Dermatol* | 2014 | 14 men aged 18–35 | Unsweetened cocoa capsules, 1 week | Lesions rose by day 4 and day 7; more cocoa, more lesions |
| Delost et al., *J Am Acad Dermatol* | 2016 | 54 college students, avg. age 21.4 | 43 g milk chocolate vs. jelly beans | About 5 more pimples on average after chocolate; no effect from jelly beans |
| Vongraviopap & Asawanonda, *Int J Dermatol* | 2016 | 25 acne-prone men | 25 g of 99% dark chocolate daily, 4 weeks | Acne scores, blackheads and inflamed bumps rose by week 2 |
| *Foods* crossover trial | 2024 | 92 people with acne | 50 g of 85% dark chocolate daily, 4 weeks | Severity rose ~1 point on a 5-point scale; 42 of 51 worsened in one group |

Looked at together, the pattern is striking: every trial that measured skin after chocolate or cocoa found more spots during the chocolate period, including trials that used pure cocoa with no sugar or milk at all. But the limits are just as consistent. Two of the trials enrolled 25 people or fewer. Two enrolled only men. One had no control group. Only the capsule studies hid from participants whether they were eating cocoa. The longest lasted four weeks. And none of them tested whether chocolate can cause acne in people who are not already prone to it — they tested whether it can make existing acne worse.

That is why a 2021 systematic review of 53 diet-and-acne studies, published in the *International Journal of Dermatology*, listed chocolate among the possible acne-promoting foods while concluding that the role of cocoa itself, versus dark or milk chocolate, remains unresolved. The evidence points somewhere, but it does not point firmly.

## The Sugar and Dairy Problem

Here is the complication that haunts every chocolate study: a chocolate bar is never just cocoa. A standard milk chocolate bar combines two ingredients that each carry stronger independent evidence for aggravating acne than cocoa does — sugar and dairy.

High-glycemic foods — refined sugar, white bread, sugary drinks, and processed snacks — spike blood sugar and insulin, and insulin in turn raises IGF-1, the growth factor that stimulates oil glands. The American Academy of Dermatology's own guidance notes that low-glycemic diets have shown some limited evidence of improving acne, and dermatologists routinely advise patients that high-glycemic eating is the dietary pattern most plausibly linked to breakouts. <a href="/blog/does-sugar-cause-diabetes-fact-check/">Does sugar cause diabetes?</a> No — but the same sugary drinks that raise diabetes risk also deliver the kind of glycemic load that dermatologists worry about for skin.

Dairy has its own evidence base, and it is larger than chocolate's. A meta-analysis of 78,529 children, adolescents, and young adults found that any dairy consumption raised the odds of acne by 25 percent, with milk specifically raising the odds by 28 percent — and the relationship was dose-dependent, with one glass of milk a day raising acne risk by 41 percent compared with drinking less than one glass a week. The proposed mechanism involves whey protein and the hormones naturally present in milk, which can raise IGF-1 levels. That is why some dermatologists point out that milk chocolate, with its sugar and dairy together, sits squarely in the category of foods most likely to aggravate acne — while cocoa itself may be a secondary player.

This is also why the famous 2016 jelly bean study matters so much, and why its critics focus on it. Jelly beans delivered the same sugar load as the chocolate bar but produced no extra pimples. If sugar alone were the explanation, the jelly beans should have caused breakouts too. They did not — which suggests that something in chocolate beyond sugar was doing the work. On the other hand, the jelly beans contained no dairy, no cocoa butter, and no cocoa flavonoids, so the study cannot say which of those was responsible. It can only say that chocolate did something the sugar-matched control did not.

The honest summary is that chocolate sits at the intersection of three separate suspects — sugar, dairy, and cocoa — and the research has not yet cleanly separated them. As one dermatology review put it, most chocolate products contain significant quantities of sugar and, in the case of milk chocolate, dairy, both of which have more independent evidence of a potential link to breakouts. It is therefore extremely difficult to isolate whether cocoa itself is the active variable.

## The Mechanism: What Cocoa Might Actually Do

If cocoa itself does aggravate acne, how would it work? The leading hypothesis involves the immune system. A laboratory study published in the journal *Cytokine* in 2013 found that chocolate consumption primed human blood immune cells to release more interleukin-1 beta, an inflammatory signal, when exposed to acne bacteria. Since inflammation is one of the four drivers of acne, a food that amplifies the inflammatory response could plausibly make existing spots angrier and more numerous.

Cocoa butter, the fat in chocolate, offers a second candidate mechanism. It is rich in oleic acid and stearic acid, and oleic acid has been shown to alter how skin cells mature inside the follicle — a process called keratinization — which can contribute to the clogged pores that become comedones. Dark chocolate, which contains the most cocoa butter, would on this theory be the worst offender, which fits the pattern of the 2016 dark-chocolate trial.

There is also an irony worth noting: cocoa is not all bad for skin. Cocoa beans are packed with flavanols, plant compounds with genuine antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. In a 12-week trial, women who drank a high-flavanol cocoa drink daily showed 25 percent less redness after UV exposure, and a separate 24-week trial found that cocoa flavanol supplementation improved skin elasticity by about 9 percentage points compared with a placebo group. Cocoa may soothe skin in one context and aggravate it in another — which is exactly the kind of contradiction that makes simple claims about food and skin so unreliable.

## Dark, Milk, or White: Does the Type Matter?

If you are wondering whether switching to dark chocolate will save your skin, the research offers no comfort. The trials that found the clearest effects used dark chocolate or pure cocoa — the 2016 study used 99 percent dark chocolate, and the 2024 trial used 85 percent dark chocolate. Milk chocolate, meanwhile, brings sugar and dairy into the mix. White chocolate contains no cocoa solids at all, only cocoa butter, sugar, and milk — so it dodges the cocoa question entirely while keeping the sugar and dairy.

Dermatologists interviewed about the question generally refuse to rank the types. Board-certified dermatologist Dr. Mona Gohara has said it is not fair to blame chocolate alone, because "high glycemic foods, processed foods, and sugar are definitely triggers for acne," and chocolate with high milk and sugar content falls into those categories. Her colleague Dr. Naana Boakye makes the same point: "It's not chocolate alone, it is the group of foods to which it belongs as a whole." The type of chocolate matters less than the overall pattern — how much sugar and dairy you eat, how often, and whether your skin is already prone to breakouts.

That also explains why the claim survives as a folk belief even though the science is murky. People who eat a lot of milk chocolate are often eating a lot of sugar and dairy generally, and people who cut out chocolate often cut out a whole category of high-glycemic snacks at the same time. When their skin improves, chocolate gets the credit — or the blame — for a change that may have had several causes at once.

## What Dermatologists Actually Advise

The most authoritative word on the subject comes from the American Academy of Dermatology's 2024 acne management guidelines. They are strikingly cautious: low-glycemic diets have only very limited evidence of improving acne, and, as summarized by the *American Family Physician* review of the guidelines, "there is no clinical research to evaluate the effect of low-dairy or whey diets or the effect of omega-3 fatty acids and chocolate on acne." The guidelines make no dietary recommendations at all, because the research is still too limited to support them.

That caution is not the same as saying diet does not matter. The AAD's public guidance notes that acne is not caused by eating any particular food, but that some people report their breakouts worsening after certain foods — and it suggests paying attention to whether specific foods trigger your breakouts and experimenting with eliminating them for a week or a month to see what changes. The emphasis is on personal testing, not universal bans.

The practical advice that follows from the evidence looks like this. If you have acne and you suspect chocolate plays a role, run a controlled personal experiment: cut out chocolate for about four weeks while keeping everything else — your diet, your skincare, and any prescribed treatment — exactly the same, and track your breakouts. Then reintroduce it and watch what happens. That is essentially what the trials did, and it is the only way to know whether chocolate matters for your skin in particular. What the evidence does not support is the reverse: telling everyone with acne to give up chocolate permanently, or promising that quitting chocolate will clear your skin. For most people, proven treatments — benzoyl peroxide, retinoids, and other medicines your dermatologist can prescribe — will do far more than any dietary change.

It is also worth remembering how common the confusion is. <a href="/blog/does-sugar-make-kids-hyperactive-fact-check/">Does sugar make kids hyperactive?</a> Decades of studies say no, yet the belief persists for the same reason the chocolate claim does: a vivid, memorable event — a birthday party, a chocolate bar, a sudden breakout — feels like proof, even when the controlled evidence says otherwise. ## How the Claim Spread

The chocolate-acne claim did not come from a single study or a single celebrity. It grew the way most food myths grow: a plausible mechanism, a memorable warning, and endless repetition. In the 1960s, when acne was poorly understood and diet was blamed for almost everything, chocolate was an easy target — sweet, fatty, and popular with exactly the teenagers who get acne. The 1969 Fulton study should have killed the claim, but instead it created a strange stalemate: doctors quoted the study, parents kept warning their children, and teenagers kept noticing breakouts after chocolate binges.

Social media changed the stakes. A single viral post claiming that quitting chocolate cleared someone's skin can now reach millions of people in a day, and the algorithm rewards the confident version of the story, not the careful one. The 2024 *Foods* trial, the largest ever conducted on the question, was barely noticed outside dermatology circles, while anecdotal "I quit chocolate and my acne vanished" videos circulate endlessly. The claim's staying power has never depended on evidence. It depends on how satisfying it is to have a single, avoidable cause for a condition that is otherwise frustrating, hormonal, and largely out of a teenager's control.

## The Verdict

Does chocolate cause acne? As a blanket claim — that eating chocolate will give you pimples — it is **Misleading**.

The evidence does not support universal causation. Chocolate has never been shown to cause acne in people who are not already prone to it, and the largest dermatology guidelines in the world make no recommendation to avoid it, because the clinical research is still too thin. The famous 1969 experiment found no effect at all, and the claim that a single chocolate bar causes a breakout has no basis in the data.

At the same time, the claim is not pure myth either. A consistent run of small, well-designed trials — the 2011 and 2014 cocoa capsule studies, the 2016 milk-chocolate-versus-jelly-beans study, the 2016 dark chocolate study, and the 2024 crossover trial of 92 people — all found that chocolate or cocoa worsened acne in people who already had it. The effect was modest, on the order of a few extra pimples, and it appeared within days. Sugar and dairy, the other ingredients in most chocolate, carry their own evidence as acne aggravators, which makes it hard to say how much of the effect belongs to cocoa itself.

So the fair statement is this: chocolate does not cause acne, but it can make acne worse for some people who already have it — and if that sounds like a small distinction, it is the difference between a fact and a myth. Anyone whose breakouts reliably follow chocolate can reasonably cut it out for a month and see what happens. Everyone else can keep the chocolate bar and spend their energy on the treatments that actually work.
