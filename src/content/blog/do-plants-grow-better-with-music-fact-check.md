Somewhere between the watering can and the windowsill, a curious piece of gardening advice has taken root: play your plants some music and they will grow faster, taller, and happier. Classical music, the story goes, makes seedlings lean toward the speaker like an audience leaning toward the stage, while heavy metal makes them wither and die. Garden blogs repeat it, plant shops print it on care cards, and social media videos show ferns apparently thriving next to Bluetooth speakers.

It is a charming idea. It is also, as usually stated, wrong — or at least far ahead of the evidence. After half a century of experiments, no study has convincingly shown that playing music makes your houseplants grow better. What the science does show is stranger and more interesting: plants can sense vibrations, and certain sound frequencies can switch genes on and off in laboratory plants. But a vibration is not a melody, a greenhouse is not a living room, and "grew a little in a lab" is not the same as "your monstera loves Mozart."

This is the story of where the claim came from, what the famous experiments actually found, and what modern plant science says about sound, music, and growth.

## Where the Claim Comes From

The modern version of the music-and-plants story traces back to 1973, a year that produced two books that would shape the idea for decades. The first was *The Secret Life of Plants* by Peter Tompkins and Christopher Bird, a bestseller that claimed plants could read minds, feel emotions, and respond to human thoughts — ideas that mainstream science rejected but the public loved. The second was *The Sound of Music and Plants* by Dorothy Retallack, a graduate student at Colorado Women's College who had run a series of small experiments playing different kinds of music to potted plants.

Retallack's findings were dramatic and perfectly shaped for retelling. Plants exposed to classical music and jazz, she reported, grew toward the speakers, wrapped themselves around them, and looked healthier than the silent control group. Plants exposed to rock music, by contrast, grew spindly and pale, leaned away from the speakers, and in some cases died within two weeks. She later concluded that the genre itself mattered less than the instruments and their resonance — but the simple version, "classical good, rock bad," is the one that went viral in the pre-internet sense, spreading through magazines, gardening columns, and word of mouth.

There was just one problem: the experiments behind the claim were, by modern standards, barely experiments at all.

## The Experiment That Started It All — and Why It Doesn't Hold Up

Retallack's work was never peer-reviewed, and later examinations of her setup turned up a long list of uncontrolled variables. Her sample sizes were tiny — in some accounts, as few as four plants per group. The treatment groups were kept in separate rooms, with no careful control of watering, fertilizer, temperature, or light. Heat radiating from the speaker cabinets could have warmed one group more than another. The plants were not randomized or blinded, and no statistical analysis was ever reported, so there is no way to know whether the differences she saw were real effects or ordinary noise.

Most tellingly, nobody has ever been able to faithfully replicate her results. In the decades since, many researchers and hobbyists have tried the same basic test — classical versus rock versus silence — and the outcomes have been all over the map. Sometimes plants seem to do slightly better with classical music. Sometimes they prefer hard rock, or Chilean folk music, or the sound of someone talking to them. Most of the time, when the test is done carefully, the results are not significantly different at all.

Retallack herself was a sincere investigator who asked a legitimate question, and her book deserves credit for sparking serious scientific interest in how plants respond to sound. But her data cannot answer the question she posed. She also believed plants were capable of extrasensory perception, a belief that tells you something about the standards of evidence at work. The music-and-plants claim, as it is usually repeated today, rests on a foundation of four plants, two rooms, and no statistics.

That does not mean the whole idea is nonsense. It means the interesting part of the story starts somewhere else entirely — not with melody, but with vibration.

## The MythBusters Test: Death Metal Wins

The claim got its most famous test in 2004, when the television show *MythBusters* set up seven small greenhouses on the roof of their workshop and ran a controlled — if not exactly laboratory-grade — experiment. Four greenhouses played looped voice recordings, two with positive speech and two with negative speech. A fifth played classical music. A sixth played death metal. The seventh was kept silent as a control.

The results surprised everyone, including the show's hosts. Every greenhouse with sound outgrew the silent control. Positive speech, negative speech, classical, death metal — all of them beat silence. The content of the words did not matter. The tone did not matter. What mattered was that there was sound at all. And the winner, by a measurable margin, was death metal.

*MythBusters* rated the claim "plausible" — not confirmed, not busted, but plausible, meaning something real seemed to be happening even if nobody could fully explain it yet. The result also quietly demolished the folk version of the claim: if death metal outperforms classical, then the idea that plants have musical taste is dead. Something else must be going on.

That something else is vibration. Sound is a pressure wave moving through air, and when it hits a leaf, it shakes it — gently, at a microscopic scale, but measurably. Plants have no ears and no nervous system, so they cannot hear music the way we do. But they are exquisitely sensitive to mechanical stimulation. They feel wind, rain, touch, and vibration, and they respond to all of it. The question is whether the gentle shaking produced by a speaker is enough to change how a plant grows — and the honest answer from modern research is: sometimes, a little, in the lab, under very specific conditions.

## What Modern Science Actually Shows

The serious research on plants and sound looks nothing like a stem leaning toward a speaker. It looks like molecular biology: scientists exposing plants to precise frequencies at precise volumes, then sequencing which genes switched on or off.

In South Korea, molecular biologist Mi-Jeong Jeong and her colleagues exposed rice plants to specific sound frequencies and found that two genes involved in photosynthesis — one encoding fructose 1,6-bisphosphate aldolase, the other the small subunit of Rubisco, the enzyme that captures carbon dioxide — were switched on by sound at 125 and 250 hertz. The effect was real and measurable, and it suggested that sound could, in principle, nudge photosynthesis. But it was a single-frequency tone in a controlled chamber, not a song on a speaker, and the researchers were careful not to claim that playing music to rice paddies would raise yields.

A more detailed study came in 2016, when Ritesh Ghosh and colleagues at Yeungnam University published in *Scientific Reports* (volume 6, article 33370) the results of exposing *Arabidopsis thaliana* — the laboratory mouse of plant science — to five different sound frequencies (250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 3,000 hertz) at a constant 80 decibels for one hour. The 500-hertz treatment stood out: it upregulated seventeen sound-regulated genes, including several genes normally switched on by touch, and shifted levels of plant hormones, particularly salicylic acid, which is involved in defense against disease. The researchers concluded that sound vibration triggers global changes in gene expression, protein levels, and hormones — a molecular basis for the idea that plants "hear" their environment.

A 2018 review in *Frontiers in Plant Science* (Jung and colleagues, volume 9, article 25) pulled the literature together and found a consistent pattern: sound waves can promote the plant growth hormones auxin and gibberellin, enhance seed germination, and prime plant immune defenses. One study the review cites found that sound at 0.4 kilohertz and 106 decibels significantly increased the germination index, stem height, and fresh weight of paddy rice seeds. The same review also noted the limits: when the sound exceeded 4 kilohertz or 111 decibels, the same processes were inhibited rather than promoted. Too much of a good thing becomes stress.

A 2024 review in the journal *Biology* (volume 13, article 326) reached a similar conclusion with a sharper warning about variability: the effects of sound on plants depend strongly on the frequency, the volume, the duration of exposure, and the setup. In one cited trial, cotton plants treated with a commercial system called Plant Acoustic Frequency Technology showed improvements across several growth measures. In transgenic rice, a reporter gene was switched up at 250 hertz but switched down at 50 hertz. The same sound that helps at one frequency can hurt at another.

Perhaps the most striking single finding comes from Monica Gagliano, a biologist at the University of Western Australia, who reported in 2017 that pea plant roots grew toward the recorded sound of flowing water — even when there was no actual moisture gradient to follow. The roots, in other words, could detect the sound of water and grow toward it. It is a remarkable result, and it has been debated, but it fits the larger picture: plants use sound as information about their environment, just as they use light, gravity, and touch.

None of this, however, is evidence that music helps your houseplants grow. Every one of these studies used pure tones or narrow frequency bands, delivered at controlled volumes for controlled durations, to plants in controlled environments. Music is the opposite of that: a messy, constantly changing mix of frequencies, amplitudes, and rhythms, played at whatever volume your speaker happens to be set to, in a room where light, temperature, humidity, and watering vary from day to day. And the measured effects, even in the lab, are modest — small shifts in gene expression and growth, not the dramatic transformations the folk claim promises.

There is also a gap in the research that matters. Nobody has run a large, well-powered, properly controlled trial of "playing music to houseplants" and found a consistent, reproducible benefit. The studies that exist are small, use different species, different sounds, different volumes, and different ways of measuring growth — which is why their results cannot be neatly combined into a single answer. When a 2021 meta-analysis reviewed fifteen studies, it concluded that specific vibrations can promote root growth in some species, such as beans and tomatoes, by up to 20 percent — but that music as a whole is not a reliable growth booster.

## Why the Genre Doesn't Matter

If plants respond to sound at all, they respond to its physical properties: frequency, amplitude, and duration. They do not respond to melody, harmony, lyrics, or genre, because they have no auditory cortex, no eardrums, and no way to process musical structure. A plant cannot tell Bach from Black Sabbath. It can only tell that air is moving against its leaves.

The mechanism, as far as scientists understand it, runs through mechanosensitive ion channels — tiny pores in plant cell membranes that open when the membrane is stretched or deformed. When a sound wave shakes a leaf, these channels open and calcium ions flood into the cell, triggering a cascade of signals that can switch genes on and off. The same channels respond to wind, rain, and touch. This is why the *MythBusters* result makes sense: any sound, including speech and death metal, shakes the leaves, and any shaking can trigger the response. The silent control greenhouse lost because it was the only one that got no mechanical stimulation at all.

Plants also have a well-documented response to touch called thigmomorphogenesis: plants that are regularly touched, shaken, or exposed to wind grow shorter, stockier, and stronger, with thicker stems. Gardeners have known a version of this for centuries — brushing your hand over seedlings, or letting a fan blow gently across them, produces sturdier plants. Sound is, in this sense, just touch at a distance. A speaker is a very expensive way to do what a breeze does for free.

This is also why the popular claim is so durable. The kernel of truth is real: sound can affect plants. The leap from that kernel to "play Mozart to your monstera and watch it thrive" is where the evidence runs out. It is the same pattern as other plant myths we have checked on this site — a real biological phenomenon, stretched by retelling into a household rule it cannot support. <a href="/blog/does-a-ducks-quack-echo-fact-check/">Does a duck's quack echo</a> turned out to be a question with a similarly mundane answer, and <a href="/blog/can-dogs-predict-earthquakes-fact-check/">can dogs predict earthquakes</a> turned on the same distinction between what animals can sense and what people claim they can sense. The pattern repeats: animals and plants sense more than we assume, and less than the stories claim.

## The Verdict

**Misleading.** The claim that plants grow better with music takes a real but narrow scientific finding — that certain sound vibrations can affect plant gene expression and growth under laboratory conditions — and stretches it into a household rule that the evidence does not support. No well-controlled study has shown that playing music makes houseplants grow faster or healthier, the famous early experiments behind the claim were too flawed to prove anything, and the one well-known test that did find an effect found that death metal beat classical — which tells you the active ingredient is vibration, not music.

That verdict comes with an important nuance. "Misleading" does not mean "nothing is happening." Plants genuinely do respond to sound. Rice plants switch on photosynthesis genes at 125 and 250 hertz. *Arabidopsis* switches on seventeen genes at 500 hertz. Pea roots grow toward the sound of running water. These are real, peer-reviewed findings, and they point to a genuine area of plant biology that scientists are still exploring. What they do not show is that your playlist will make your pothos grow. The gap between a one-hour tone at 80 decibels in a growth chamber and a Spotify playlist in a living room is exactly the gap the claim papers over.

The claim is also misleading in a second, subtler way: it misdirects attention. The factors that determine whether a plant thrives — light, water, soil, nutrients, temperature, airflow — account for nearly all of the difference between a flourishing plant and a struggling one. Sound, even under the most optimistic reading of the research, would account for a fraction of a percent. A person who plays Beethoven to a plant in a dark corner while forgetting to water it is doing the equivalent of reading poetry to a patient who needs antibiotics. The gesture is kind. The treatment is wrong.

## What Actually Makes Plants Grow

If the goal is a healthier plant rather than a more musical one, the evidence points somewhere far less glamorous. Light is the dominant factor: most houseplants need bright, indirect light for most of the day, and a plant that is stretching toward a window is telling you it needs more of it, not more music. Water is next — consistent, appropriate watering, with the soil allowed to dry between waterings for most species, beats any acoustic intervention ever measured. Then come the basics: fresh potting soil or fertilizer at the right season, a pot with drainage, and temperatures in the range the species evolved for.

Air movement deserves a special mention, because it is the closest thing to a free version of the music claim. A gentle breeze, or a fan on its lowest setting a few feet away, provides exactly the mechanical stimulation that sound provides — shaking the leaves, triggering the same calcium signaling, encouraging sturdier stems — without the speaker. Commercial growers have used this for decades: greenhouses that circulate air produce stronger transplants than still ones, a fact that has nothing to do with music and everything to do with physics.

None of this is to say you should stop playing music around your plants. If you enjoy it, and the volume is moderate, there is no evidence that music harms them, and there is plenty of evidence that caring for plants — paying attention to them, noticing when they need water, catching pests early — is the real variable behind every "my plants love my music" story. The person who plays music to their plants is usually the person who also looks at their plants every day. The attention is doing the work; the music is just the soundtrack.

## If You Still Want to Try It

There is nothing wrong with experimenting. If you want to test the claim on your own plants, the research suggests a few ground rules — and they are the opposite of what the viral version of the claim implies.

First, keep the volume moderate. The laboratory effects were found at roughly 80 decibels, about the level of a conversation in a busy restaurant, and the 2018 review noted that sounds above about 111 decibels inhibited the very processes they promoted at lower volumes. Very loud, bass-heavy, or distorted sound can stress plants, and some experiments have recorded stunted growth or death under harsh acoustic conditions. Background-music level is the safe zone; concert level is not.

Second, keep sessions short. Some of the positive laboratory results came from an hour of exposure, and one early study found that growth declined when exposure exceeded three hours a day. A couple of hours of gentle sound is the range the evidence points to — not music playing around the clock.

Third, do not expect a genre effect. If anything happens, it will happen with any sound — classical, jazz, talk radio, or the hum of a fan — because the mechanism is vibration. Picking the "right" music is the least important variable in the experiment, and anyone selling you a special plant playlist is selling you a story, not a result.

Fourth, and most importantly, control for everything else. If you want to know whether music made a difference, you need two plants of the same species, the same age, and the same size, in the same light, with the same watering schedule, one with music and one without. Most home experiments fail this test, which is why most home experiments "work" — the plant by the speaker is usually also the plant by the bright window, or the plant its owner waters most carefully. The same confounding explains a surprising number of animal claims too: <a href="/blog/do-mosquitoes-prefer-certain-blood-types-fact-check/">do mosquitoes prefer certain blood types</a> turned out to be a question where the headline effect was real in the lab but swamped in real life by bigger factors like breath and body heat. Music and plants follow the same pattern — a real laboratory effect, drowned out in real life by everything else.

## The Bottom Line

The idea that plants grow better with music has survived for fifty years because it contains a grain of truth wrapped in a story people want to believe. Plants do sense sound. Sound can switch their genes on and off. In a laboratory, under precise conditions, it can even change how fast they grow. But nobody has shown that playing music — any music — to the plants in your home makes them grow better, and the famous experiments that launched the claim would not pass a high-school science fair today.

So play music to your plants if it makes you happy. Sing to them, talk to them, put on death metal if that is your taste — the *MythBusters* plants liked it best, after all. Just do not mistake the soundtrack for the care. Your plants do not need a playlist. They need light, water, and someone who notices them. Give them those, and they will grow — with or without the music.
